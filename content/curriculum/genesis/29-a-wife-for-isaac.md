Genesis 24 is the longest chapter in Genesis: in this app's BSB, 67 verses and about 1,660 words by a script count, more than any other chapter on either measure. The plot is simple. Abraham's servant finds Rebekah at a spring among Abraham's relatives, she agrees to go, and Isaac marries her. The length comes from the telling: the meeting is narrated (24:10-27) and then retold by the servant (24:34-49). This lesson does not retell the chapter. It compares the two tellings, counts one key word, and reports four real disagreements among named Jewish and Christian readers: what the oath "under my thigh" meant (24:2-9); whether the servant's request for a sign is a model or an exception (24:12-14); what Rebekah was asked when she said "I will go" (24:57-58); and what Isaac went out to do in the field (24:63). It also grades a popular Christian reading of the chapter as a picture of Christ and the Church (Teach-Back 5). Every BSB quotation below and in the three connection files was checked by script against this app's shipped corpus; see Sources and Limits.

## Context

**What happens, in outline.** Abraham makes "the chief servant of his household" swear not to take a wife for Isaac "from the daughters of the Canaanites" (24:2-3), but "do not take my son back there" (24:8). At a spring near "Nahor's hometown in Aram-naharaim" (24:10), the servant asks for a sign (24:12-14), and Rebekah, the granddaughter of Abraham's brother Nahor, meets its terms (24:15-24). At her family's house he retells everything (24:34-49), and Laban and Bethuel answer, "This is from the LORD" (24:50). Asked whether she will go, Rebekah says, "I will go" (24:58). At evening Isaac is in the field (24:63), and "Isaac loved her and was comforted after his mother's death" (24:67).

**Where this unit sits.** Stage 5 of this app's eleven-stage lens ("Genesis 12 – Malachi — Israel: The Twelve Tribes," machine-readable `chapters: ["1.12"]`). The chapter before records Sarah's death (23:2); the chapter after records Abraham's (25:8) and that "Isaac was forty years old when he married Rebekah" (25:20). Rebekah's birth was announced at 22:23.

**Key words, read as observations.**

- **The unnamed servant.** The servant is never named in Genesis 24. A script scan finds "Eliezer" in Genesis only at 15:2 ("the heir of my house is Eliezer of Damascus"). Keil and Delitzsch report that the servant is Eliezer "according to general opinion," and Haydock identifies him the same way. The identification is a reading. The chapter itself does not make it.
- **"he prayed" (24:12) and "praying in my heart" (24:45).** Bible Hub's interlinear shows that the Hebrew of 24:12 opens with the ordinary verb "and he said" (Strong's 559), and that 24:45 reads "speaking in my heart" (1696). The BSB's "prayed" and "praying" describe words addressed to the LORD; the Hebrew does not use a prayer verb here. (Contrast 20:17, where the interlinear shows *palal*, 6419, and 25:21, where it shows *atar*, 6279, "entreated"; both are verbs of asking God.)
- **"kindness" (chesed).** Bible Hub's concordance lists eleven occurrences of chesed (Strong's 2617) in Genesis: 19:19, 20:13, 21:23, 24:12, 24:14, 24:27, 24:49, 32:10, 39:21, 40:14 and 47:29. Four of the eleven are in this chapter, and no other chapter of Genesis has more than one. All four are the servant's words, from his first request ("show kindness to my master Abraham," 24:12) to his last question to the family ("if you will show kindness and faithfulness to my master," 24:49). In 24:27 and 24:49 chesed is paired with emet (Strong's 571). At 24:27 the Cambridge Bible calls that combination "almost proverbial" and glosses it: "'Mercy' denotes the goodness, 'truth' the fidelity of God, in the fulfilment of His promises." The Cambridge Bible's own note on 24:49 cites 47:29, where the pair returns with the hand under the thigh (see the connections). The BSB renders all four as "kindness".
- **"Isaac loved her" (24:67).** A script scan finds "loved" first in the BSB at 24:67. The only earlier "love" in Genesis is 22:2, Abraham's love for Isaac ("your only son Isaac, whom you love"). So 24:67 is the first verse in the BSB to say that a man loved a woman. Radak reads the clause as telling "that Yitzchok loved the outstanding qualities of Rivkah."
- **"to meditate" (24:63).** Bible Hub's lexicon lists the verb (Strong's 7742, *suach*) as occurring "only once, Genesis 24:63." In the BSB, "meditate" occurs in Genesis only here. What the word means is Positions 4.
- **Beer-lahai-roi (24:62).** Isaac "had just returned from Beer-lahai-roi" when Rebekah arrived. A script scan finds the name in Genesis only at 16:14, the well where the angel of the LORD found Hagar, at 24:62, and at 25:11. Keil and Delitzsch give the cross-reference but call it "impossible to determine" why Isaac had been there.

## Literary Design

**One meeting, told twice.** The meeting at the spring is told by the narrator (24:10-27) and again by the servant to Rebekah's family (24:34-49), who were not there. Laid side by side, the two tellings differ at checkable points.

| | The narrator | The servant's retelling |
|---|---|---|
| Abraham's instruction | "go to my country and my kindred" (24:4) | "go to my father's house and to my kindred" (24:38) |
| The release clause | "if the woman is unwilling to follow you" (24:8) | "if they refuse to give her to you" (24:41) |
| The request | "please grant me success today, and show kindness to my master Abraham" (24:12) | "if only You would make my journey a success!" (24:42) |
| Gifts and question | gives "a gold ring" and "two gold bracelets" (24:22), then asks, "Whose daughter are you?" (24:23) | "I asked her, 'Whose daughter are you?'" then "I put the ring on her nose" (24:47) |

Rashi notices the reordered gifts at 24:47: "He changed the order of proceedings for in fact he had first given the presents and afterwards questioned her. But he did this in order that they should not catch him by his own words and say, 'How could you give her anything when you did not yet know who she was!'" On the chapter's length, Rashi at 24:42 cites Rabbi Acha: "The ordinary conversation of the patriarchs' servants is more pleasing to God than even the Torah (religious discourse) of their children, for the chapter of Eliezer (the account of his journey) is repeated in the Torah," while many principles of the Law are derived only from slight hints. Ramban, on the servant's wording in 24:40-41 ("from my kindred and from my father's house," BSB 24:40), says: "These words were said by the servant to honor them so that they would listen to him." Haydock remarks at 24:41 that the steward here "gives the meaning of his master" rather than his exact words. No source read comments on the change from the woman's unwillingness to the family's refusal, and the lesson supplies no motive for it.

**Readings of the whole chapter.** S. Lewis Johnson, a Bible teacher whose sermons the SLJ Institute preserves, says in a sermon on the chapter that "the story of how Abraham uses the unnamed servant to obtain a bride for his son Isaac has down through the centuries reminded the Christian church of how the first person of the Trinity, our Heavenly Father, has used the Holy Spirit to obtain a bride for the second person of the Trinity, the Lord Jesus Christ." He calls the chapter both an "illustration" and "a beautiful and remarkable typical event," and he grounds it in two New Testament texts that do not mention Genesis 24 (2 Corinthians 11, on the church betrothed to Christ, and James's words in Acts 15). Haydock's Catholic commentary, on 24:65, reports that Rebekah "prefigures the Gentiles, whom Jesus calls by his servants laden with his gifts, to become his spouse, or his Church, (Calmet) at the fountain of baptism," and adds Du Hamel's reading of the jewelry as "the ear-rings of obedience, and the bracelets of good works." A Jewish reading of the ending looks back to Sarah, not forward. On 24:67, Rashi, citing Genesis Rabbah, says that when Isaac brought Rebekah into his mother's tent, "she became exactly like his mother Sarah." The light, the blessing in the dough and the cloud over the tent had ceased at Sarah's death, but "when Rebecca came, they reappeared." The textual warrant for the Christian typology is weighed in Teach-Back 5.

## Positions

Four questions follow, each reported through named readers in their own words and none decided here.

**1. "Place your hand under my thigh" (24:2, 24:9).** The text records the gesture, the oath "by the LORD, the God of heaven and the God of earth" (24:3), and nothing about what the gesture means. A script scan finds "thigh" in the BSB's Genesis only at 24:2, 24:9 and 47:29, where Jacob asks the same of Joseph. That is in English; the Hebrew *yarekh* also appears at 32:25-32 and 46:26. Readers give four explanations.

- **Touching the sign of the covenant.** Rashi: "whoever takes an oath must take in his hand some sacred object. such as a Scroll of the Law or Tefillin." He continues: "As circumcision was the first commandment given to him and became his only through much pain it was consequently dear to him and therefore he selected this as the object upon which to take the oath." Genesis Rabbah 59:8 gives the same reason.
- **An appeal to descendants.** Keil and Delitzsch call it "the so-called bodily oath," noting that "the early Jewish commentators supposed it to be especially connected with the rite of circumcision," but themselves hold it "no doubt connected with the significance of the hip as the part from which the posterity issued (Genesis 46:26), and the seat of vital power." The Cambridge Bible says the phrase "probably" contains "a survival of a very ancient piece of symbolism" that "seems to refer to the organs of generation, and also, possibly, to the covenant rite of circumcision. The appeal is made to those who hereafter should be born, on the one hand, to attest the oath, and, on the other, to avenge its violation."
- **A sign of a servant's subjection.** Ibn Ezra: "I believe that it was customary in those days for a person to place his hand under the thigh of his master, its meaning being, 'If you are subject to my authority, place your hand under my thigh.'" Radak, similarly: "it was customary to make a person reinforce the promise that he would carry out an assignment by his placing his hand within the control of his master." Calvin, after reporting the other readings: "The more plausible opinion is, that the ancients in this manner swore by Christ; but because I do not willingly follow uncertain conjectures, I leave the question undecided. Nevertheless the latter supposition appears to me the more simple; namely, that servants, when they swore fidelity to their lords, were accustomed to testify their subjection by this ceremony."
- **Faith in the promised seed, and so in Christ.** Calvin reports that "Christian writers conceive that the hand was placed under the thigh in honor of the blessed seed." Haydock lists three Catholic readings: "either to shew their subjection, (Sa.) or their faith in Christ, who should be born of Abraham, (St. Jerome, ep. 140) or to testify that their oath shall be no less binding than the covenant of circumcision." He adds that because Abraham and Jacob, "progenitors of Christ," are the only two in Scripture who use the gesture, Augustine and Ambrose, as Bonfrère reports them, connect it with the Redeemer's birth. Johnson, in the modern sermon above, reads it as "an expression of faith that God is going to from his own loins bring forth a seed."

All agree the oath itself is sworn "by the LORD" (24:3). Readers use that fact differently. Calvin cites it to show the rite was "no profane rite": "It is true that the servant placed his hand under the thigh of Abraham, but he is adjured by God, the Creator of heaven and earth." Ibn Ezra cites it against the circumcision reading: "if this were the case, then Abraham should have made him swear by the covenant of circumcision, and not by the Lord." This lesson does not choose among the four readings.

**2. The servant's sign (24:12-14): a model or an exception?** The servant asks that the girl who answers "Drink, and I will water your camels as well" be "the one You have appointed for Your servant Isaac" (24:14). Before he finishes, Rebekah comes and says almost exactly that (24:15-19). The text does not comment on the method, and readers divide over whether it should be copied.

- **Permitted, with limits.** Matthew Henry: "We have leave to be particular in recommending our affairs to the care of Divine providence. He proposes a sign, not that he intended to proceed no further, if not gratified in it; but it is a prayer that God would provide a good wife for his young master; and that was a good prayer." His limit: "We must take heed of being over-bold in urging what God should do, lest the event should weaken our faith, rather than strengthen it. But God owned him by making his way clear." Haydock's Catholic commentary, citing Chrysostom and Calmet, says, "This was no vain observation. God heard his fervent prayer," and, citing Worthington and Augustine: "It is sometimes lawful to ask a sign or miracle of God, (Acts i. 24; iv. 30; 1 Kings xiv, &c.,) but we must carefully avoid whatever the Church disapproves."
- **An exception, not a rule.** Calvin judges that the method "seems scarcely consistent with the true rule of prayer," since "no one prays aright unless he subjects his own wishes to God," and he asks: "Where, then, it may be asked, is the religion of the servant, who, according to his own pleasure, imposes a law upon God?" He answers that "God, however, in hearkening to his wish, proves, by the event, that it was acceptable to himself," and that the servant prayed "by the secret impulse of the Spirit." The general rule binding all believers "does not prevent the Lord, when he determines to give something extraordinary, from directing the minds of his servants towards it; not that he would lead them away from his word, but only that he makes some peculiar concession to them in their mode of praying." Calvin limits its use: the sign came "from something present," and "This observation may be of use to prevent inquisitive men from adducing this example as a precedent for vain prognostications."
- **An improper request, answered graciously anyway.** The Talmud (Taanit 4a) reports Rabbi Yonatan's list of three who asked God improperly, rendered "unreasonable" in the Koren-Steinsaltz translation: "Eliezer, servant of Abraham; Saul, son of Kish; and Jephthah the Gileadite." The objection is that the sign, as worded, could have been met by an unsuitable woman. God answered Eliezer and Saul well, and Jephthah, whose vow brought out his daughter, badly. Genesis Rabbah 60:3 makes the same point with four names (adding Caleb): "What if some maidservant would emerge and give him to drink, would he have taken her to marry his master's son?" Radak answers that case from the words "to whom I say" (24:14): because the servant chose whom to address, he "freed himself from the obligation to address a blind or lame girl," though not from every unsuitable match, and so "he prayed that G'd would do a kindness to his master and arrange that the girl whom he would approach to offer water would be a girl suitable as a potential wife for Yitzchok." A related line in the Talmud makes the servant's sign the model case of divination. In Chullin 95b, Rav is quoted as saying that "any divination that is not like" that of "Eliezer, the servant of Abraham," or of "Jonathan, son of Saul," "is not divination." The Koren-Steinsaltz explanation connects this to the prohibition against relying on an omen to make a decision.

Some observations are shared. The Cambridge Bible notes that the sign itself "would be no mere formality, but a practical and laborious act of kindness towards a stranger." Keil and Delitzsch note that the servant "did not give himself blindly up to first impressions, however, but tested the circumstances." In the text, he watches "silently" (24:21) and asks whose daughter she is (24:23) before he worships (24:26). The Catechism of the Catholic Church does not discuss this verse; its general teaching says that "God can reveal the future to his prophets or to other saints" (2115) and that "All forms of divination are to be rejected," naming "interpretation of omens and lots" (2116).

The readers above disagree about whether the servant's way of asking is one to imitate. If you are weighing a major decision, such as a marriage, a move or a job, by waiting for a sign, this lesson does not settle that question for you. Whatever you conclude about signs, bring the decision to people who know you and will speak honestly, such as a pastor, a mentor or a trusted friend, before you act on it.

**3. Rebekah's "I will go" (24:57-58): what was she asked?** After the servant refuses a delay, the family says, "We will call the girl and ask her opinion," and asks her, "Will you go with this man?" She answers, "I will go." Earlier, Laban and Bethuel had already said, "Take her and go, and let her become the wife of your master's son" (24:51). Readers differ over what her answer decided.

- **Her consent to the marriage itself.** Genesis Rabbah 60:12 draws a rule from 24:57: "from here we learn that one may marry off an orphan girl only with her consent" (the midrash holds that Bethuel had died). Rashi states it more generally: "From this we may infer that a woman should not be given in marriage except with her own consent." Calvin reads the verse the same way. Moses "declares that he did not exercise tyranny over his daughter, so as to thrust her out reluctantly, or to compel her to marry against her will, but left her to her own free choice." He adds: "Truly, in this matter, the authority of parents ought to be sacred: but a middle course is to be pursued, so that the parties concerned may make their contract spontaneously, and with mutual consent." Matthew Henry writes: "As children ought not to marry without their parents' consent, so parents ought not to marry them without their own."
- **Her consent to leaving now, the marriage being already agreed.** Radak reads 24:51 as showing that the family "would not have said 'here Rivkah is before you take her and go,' unless they had first secured Rivkah's willingness to marry Yitzchok," so the question of 24:57 is whether she will go "at this time." He states the principle no less firmly: "It is forbidden to force marriage on anyone." Challoner, as Haydock reports him: "Not as to her marriage, as she had already consented, but of her quitting her parents and going to her husband."

*How readers hear her answer.* In Genesis Rabbah 60:12, Rabbi Yitzhak hears the family's question as a hint to refuse ("Will you [really] go?") and her reply as resolve: "I am going against your will, even if you disapprove." Henry: "Rebekah consented, not only to go, but to go at once." Calvin reads it as agreement, not defiance: "It is not right to understand that Rebekah in answering so explicitly, showed contempt for the paternal roof, or too anxiously desired a husband; but since she saw that the affair was transacted by the authority of her father, and with the consent of her mother, she also herself acquiesced in it." Haydock: "I will go, without delay, being well convinced that the good steward was directed by God. Hence she was guilty of no imprudence or levity, in yielding herself up to the divine will, and consenting so readily to the proposed marriage."

The Catechism of the Catholic Church, in its teaching on marriage rather than on this chapter, states: "The consent must be an act of the will of each of the contracting parties, free of coercion or grave external fear. No human power can substitute for this consent" (1628). Every reader quoted here holds that Rebekah's consent mattered. They differ on whether the question in 24:57 concerned the marriage or the departure. If you are being pressured or threatened into a marriage or a relationship you do not want, that is not a question for a study note. **4. What did Isaac go out to do (24:63)?** The verb occurs only here (see Key Words). The BSB renders it "to meditate." The Cambridge Bible calls it "A strange and poetical word to be used in this context" that "has given rise to very various renderings," listing the Septuagint's ἀδολεσχῆσαι, the Latin *ad meditandum*, and the Syriac "to walk about." Readers give four main senses.

- **To pray.** Rashi: "this means 'to pray' (Genesis Rabbah 60:14)," citing Psalm 102:1, where the cognate noun stands in the heading (BSB: "pours out his lament before the LORD"). The Talmud (Berakhot 26b), an older source, reads the verse the same way: "Isaac instituted the afternoon prayer," the Minchah. Berakhot 26b reports this within a dispute, though. Rabbi Yosei son of Rabbi Hanina held that the prayers "were instituted by the Patriarchs," and Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi that they "were instituted based on the daily offerings"; the Talmud cites a teaching in support of each. Keil and Delitzsch hold that the purpose "was undoubtedly to lay the question of his marriage before God in solitude," and they call the rendering "to pray" in the Aramaic Targum "and by Luther and others" one given "with substantial correctness."
- **To meditate.** Ellicott: "Many Jewish commentators translate to pray," but "though the verb is rare, the substantive is used in Psalm 104:34 of religious meditation." Haydock: "To meditate on the obligations of the state on which he was about to enter." Haydock, citing Calmet, notes that the Septuagint's verb, in non-biblical authors, "means to talk about trifles," but holds that "the known piety of Isaac, and the authority of that version, forbid that we should take it here in that sense." Calvin allows both ("may mean either") but adds: "It is probable that he did this according to his custom, and that he sought a place of retirement for prayer."
- **To walk among the shrubs.** Ibn Ezra: "The word la-su'ach (to meditate) means to walk among the trees." His translators' note points to *si'ach ha-sadeh* in 2:5 (BSB: "no shrub of the field"). Radak: "to walk among the bushes," at "the time of day when many people go for a walk."
- **To lament.** The Cambridge Bible reports that "Many modern scholars, e.g. Knobel, Ewald, Strack, and Gunkel, render 'to wail,' or 'lament,'" and notes that "this rendering has the merit of agreeing with the mention of Isaac's need of being comforted (Genesis 24:67)." (Those scholars are reported as the Cambridge Bible lists them; their own works were not read.)

All of these readers work from the same sixty-seven verses. This lesson reports them and does not rank them.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge. It models the shape; it is not an answer key, and the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate step.

**Passage element.** Genesis 24:33. The servant is welcomed and his camels fed. "Then a meal was set before the man, but he said, 'I will not eat until I have told you what I came to say.' So Laban said, 'Please speak.'"

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the servant puts his errand ahead of a meal already set out, and opens by naming whose errand it is: "I am Abraham's servant" (24:34). Inference: for a first audience that knew the duties of hospitality, his refusal would likely have marked him as a man more concerned with his master's business than his own comfort. The text itself states no moral.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When someone has trusted you with an errand on their behalf, you can finish it, or at least state it plainly, before you settle into your own comfort.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a task done on someone else's behalf, a comfortable pause that could delay it, and a choice to state the errand first. Not carried forward: the oath, the master-servant relationship, the marriage arrangement, the sign and the camels.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed. Nothing promises that putting the errand first will bring success.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work or volunteering. A coordinator asks you to deliver a sensitive request to another team at an off-site gathering. When you arrive, lunch is starting and everyone is relaxed.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: faithfulness (keeping a trust given by someone else). One faithful response: before sitting down, find the right person, say briefly, "Before we eat, I said I'd pass this on for [the coordinator]," state it plainly, and then join the meal.

**Cautions.** This concerns an ordinary task between colleagues. It does not mean neglecting food, rest or health for an employer, or putting an errand above one's safety or conscience. The passage is not about workplace ethics; the bridge is an analogy the learner may find does not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. Using the table in Literary Design, which differences between the two tellings seem to you to carry weight, and which look like ordinary retelling? Rashi gives a motive for one. Would you give motives for the others?
2. All four occurrences of chesed in this chapter are in the servant's mouth. What does it change, if anything, that the narrator never uses the word here?
3. Calvin and the Talmud both treat the servant's sign as unusual, yet they give different explanations of it. Using only 24:12-27, what in the text would each point to?
4. In 24:51 the family says, "Take her and go"; in 24:57 they say, "We will call the girl and ask her opinion." How would you test Radak's reading of these verses against Rashi's, using only the text?
5. If *suach* in 24:63 means "lament," how does that change your reading of 24:67, "and was comforted after his mother's death"? If it means "pray," what changes?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) give the outline of 24:1-67 in six or seven steps, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) Name two places where the servant's retelling (24:34-49) differs from the narrator's account. (c) Say what the Hebrew of 24:12 has where the BSB says "he prayed."
2. **Five-minute outline.** One minute each: the two tellings; the count of chesed; Positions 1-2; Positions 3-4; the connections, the typology and the boundary (prompts 4-5).
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: setting Calvin's "exception" beside the Talmud's "improper request" may make the servant look worse than the text does, which records an immediate answer (24:15) and no rebuke. A second candidate, running the other way: Henry's and Haydock's "permitted, with limits" may make the method look more repeatable than the text does, since nothing in Genesis 24 commands or commends it. Check both against what the text records.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) The chapter does not say what the hand under the thigh meant; the four readings in Positions 1 are inferences from 24:2, 24:9, 46:26 and 47:29. (b) The chapter does not name the servant. "Eliezer" comes from 15:2 and from later readers. (c) The chapter does not present Rebekah's experience as a pattern for deciding marriages today; the rules drawn from 24:57 in Positions 3 are those readers' applications.

Choose one row and defend it, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` for a candidate. This lesson looked for a New Testament use of Genesis 24 and found none. A script scan of the BSB New Testament finds "Rebecca" only at Romans 9:10 ("Rebecca's children were conceived by one man, our father Isaac"), which concerns the twins of Genesis 25 and belongs to that lesson; it is cross-referenced here, not duplicated. "Nahor" appears only in Luke 3:34's genealogy. No New Testament verse quotes Genesis 24.

Two candidates were not authored. (a) **The bride typology.** Abraham as the Father, the servant as the Spirit, Isaac as the Son, Rebekah as the Church. The named sources read are Johnson and Haydock's report of Calmet and Du Hamel (Literary Design). Johnson grounds the picture in 2 Corinthians 11 and Acts 15, and neither text mentions Genesis 24, Rebekah or the servant. Johnson calls the chapter both an "illustration" and "a beautiful and remarkable typical event," but the New Testament texts he cites do not draw the line themselves. Without a New Testament text that draws the line, `no_warrant_yet` is the honest grade. It is a grade about textual warrant, not a verdict on the devotional use. (b) **Galatians 3:16** ("and to your seed," meaning One, who is Christ). The servant quotes the promise "To your offspring I will give this land" (24:7), the Hebrew wording of 12:7 (BSB: "I will give this land to your offspring"). No source read for this lesson ties Galatians 3:16 to the servant's quotation of the promise rather than to the promise's earlier statements, so no row is authored here.
