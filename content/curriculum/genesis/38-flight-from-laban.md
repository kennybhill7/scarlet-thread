After twenty years in Haran, Jacob leaves without telling his father-in-law. The LORD tells him, "Go back to the land of your fathers and to your kindred, and I will be with you" (31:3). Leah and Rachel agree, with a complaint of their own about their father. While Laban is away shearing, "Rachel stole her father's household idols" (31:19). Laban pursues, searches every tent, and fails to find them because Rachel is sitting on them and tells him she cannot stand up. Jacob, who does not know, has already sworn that whoever has them "shall not live" (31:32). The two men end with a heap of stones, two names for it in two languages, and Laban's words, "May the LORD keep watch between you and me when we are absent from each other" (31:49). The narrator never says why Rachel took the idols. This lesson counts what the chapter repeats, lets the daughters' complaint be heard in their own words, and reports, without choosing, how named readers have filled the gaps.

## Context

**What happens, in order (31:1-55).** Laban's sons resent Jacob's wealth and Laban's face changes (31:1-2). The LORD tells Jacob to go home (31:3). Jacob tells Rachel and Leah that their father "has cheated me and changed my wages ten times," and that the angel of God said in a dream, "I am the God of Bethel" (31:4-13). The sisters agree (31:14-16). While Laban is shearing, Rachel takes the idols, and Jacob leaves secretly across the Euphrates (31:17-21). God warns the pursuing Laban in a dream (31:22-24). Laban accuses Jacob and asks, "why have you stolen my gods?" (31:25-30). Jacob swears an oath and invites a search; Rachel sits on the idols and excuses herself (31:31-35). Jacob lists twenty years of grievances (31:36-42). Laban claims, "Everything you see is mine!" (31:43), and proposes a covenant sealed with a pillar, a heap, an oath and a meal; then he blesses his family and goes home (31:44-55).

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens puts Genesis 12 through Malachi in stage 5. The Bethel lesson in this curriculum (Genesis 28:10-22) already carries a connection to 31:13, "I am the God of Bethel, where you anointed the pillar and made a solemn vow to Me" (conn-gen28-bethel-gen31-13, explicit_reference, explicit); this lesson points to that row and does not repeat it. The breeding scheme of 30:25-43 and the meeting with Esau (Genesis 32-33) belong to other units. In the BSB, 31:55 closes the chapter; Hebrew Bibles number that verse 32:1, which is why Sefaria's Genesis Rabbah quotes Laban's farewell as "(Genesis 32:1)."

**Key words, read as observations.** Every count below was made by this lesson's own script over the app's BSB text, case-insensitive and on word boundaries, unless a lexicon is named.

- **"Household idols" and "gods" (31:19, 31:30-35).** The BSB has "household idols" in eight verses: three in this chapter (31:19, 31:34, 31:35), four in Judges 17-18 and one in 1 Samuel 19:13, where Michal puts one in David's bed. The phrase "household gods" occurs in the BSB only at Judges 18:14 and 2 Kings 23:24, not in Genesis. The Hebrew behind 31:19, 31:34 and 31:35 is *teraphim*; Bible Hub's concordance lists 15 verses with the word, and its Brown-Driver-Briggs entry calls the etymology "dubious." When Laban and Jacob speak (31:30, 31:32), the word is not *teraphim* but *elohim*, "gods," with "my" and "your" (Bible Hub's interlinear). "My gods" occurs in the BSB only at 31:30 and Daniel 3:14.
- **Stealing (31:19-39).** Bible Hub's interlinear shows the verb *ganav*, "steal," eight times in this chapter: 31:19, 31:20, 31:26, 31:27, 31:30, 31:32, and twice in 31:39. The BSB renders it "stole" for Rachel (31:19) but "deceived" for Jacob (31:20, 31:26, 31:27); the Hebrew of 31:20 is literally that Jacob "stole the heart" of Laban. Rashi glosses 31:27 "thou didst steal away my mind." Matthew Michael (*Journal for Semitics*, 2018) lists the same verses and argues that the chapter sets Rachel's theft from her father beside Jacob's earlier deception of Isaac (Genesis 27).
- **"Ten times" (31:7, 31:41).** Jacob says it twice. In the BSB "ten times" occurs in six verses: these two, Numbers 14:22, Nehemiah 4:12, Job 19:3 and Daniel 1:20. Ellicott glosses it "a good many times" and Poole "ofttimes." Gill allows that it could be "precisely ten times," since sheep there bred twice a year. Genesis Rabbah 74:3 records a fuller count: "The Rabbis say: One hundred times," reading the Hebrew *monim* as tens. The Cambridge Bible notes that Jacob's account here "differs from that in the preceding chapter," where Jacob set the terms and used his own "artifice."
- **"The Fear of Isaac" (31:42, 31:53).** The BSB capitalizes "Fear" as a divine title only in these two verses (a deliberately case-sensitive count of "Fear of"); the Hebrew is *paḥad*. The Targums make it a description: Onkelos on 31:53 has "He who Yitchok his father feared," and Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on 31:42 "He whom Izhak feareth." Calvin takes it "passively for the God whom Isaac revered." Rashi explains why Jacob did not say "God of Isaac": "God does not associate His name with the righteous whilst they are alive," and Isaac was still living. Ibn Ezra gives two senses: on 31:42, "Isaac's fear of the Lord aided me, for the merits of a father help his son," and on 31:53, "the One whom his father feared," adding that a reference to the binding of Isaac "is not far-fetched." Ramban notices that "Ibn Ezra's explanations of the same expression are thus not alike." No modern critical study of the title was read for this lesson.
- **Two names for one heap (31:47).** "Laban called it Jegar-sahadutha, and Jacob called it Galeed." Rashi: "This is the Aramaic of גלעד," and both mean "heap of testimony." Barnes calls it "the first decided specimen of Aramaic, as contradistinguished from Hebrew," and Keil and Delitzsch "the oldest proof" that "Aramaean or Chaldaean was spoken" in Mesopotamia. Genesis Rabbah 74:14 (Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥman) lists it first of three places where the language is honored, "in the Torah, in the Prophets, and in the Writings" (with Jeremiah 10:11 and Daniel 2:4); Sefaria's translator renders the midrash's word as "Persian" and explains it as Aramaic. The Cambridge Bible thinks the verse "must be a learned gloss," and the Pulpit Commentary notes that "Mizpah," which Laban also speaks, "is not Chaldee or Aramaic but Hebrew."

**Leah and Rachel's own voice (31:14-16).** The sisters answer together: "Do we have any portion or inheritance left in our father's house? Are we not regarded by him as outsiders? Not only has he sold us, but he has certainly squandered what was paid for us." They close: "So do whatever God has told you." The Cambridge Bible explains the complaint: Laban kept "the full profits of Jacob's fourteen years' service as the gift, or mohar," but gave his daughters nothing. Rashi reads "sold us" as "in return for your labour during fourteen years." Calvin paraphrases them, "what a cruel rejection is this," and notes that "they did not so much yield to his wish as to the command of God." Tikva Frymer-Kensky (Jewish Women's Archive) writes that both sisters "express their anger at Laban, who never gave them any of the bride wealth earned by Jacob's fourteen years of service," and that "like Rebekah, they affirm their choice to leave their parental home." Ellicott calls their answer "a marked asperity." Genesis Rabbah 74:4 records a darker reading of one detail: Rabbi Yudan says Rachel died before Leah "Because she spoke before her sister." Readers who hear in this complaint a word on family money or control today may want more than a study note (see the Practice Bridge).

## Literary Design

**Two searches and a reversal.** Jacob once deceived a blind father who felt him with his hands (27:21-23). Here a father "searched everything in the tent" (31:34) and is deceived by a daughter. Michael points to shared verbs, "search" and "find," and reads Rachel as placed "in the same character zone" as Jacob. He reports Esther Fuchs's view that the scene punishes Laban twice: "the deceiver is deceived," and his idols "are being desecrated." These are readings of an arrangement; the narrator states no verdict on either theft.

**What the narrator tells, and withholds.** The narrator tells the reader at once that Rachel stole the idols (31:19) and that "Jacob did not know" (31:32), so the reader watches the search knowing what Laban and Jacob do not. The narrator gives no motive for the theft, and Greenberg suggests why: the motive may have seemed "self-evident to his readers." Ktziah Spanier, as Michael quotes her, says the opposite: Rachel's is "the only act of thievery which receives no textual explanation."

## Positions

Four questions follow. Each is reported through named readers in their own words and is not adjudicated. Nothing in a reading's statement is this lesson's own conclusion.

**1. Why did Rachel take the household idols?** The text says only that she "stole her father's household idols" while Laban was shearing (31:19), that Laban calls them "my gods" (31:30), and that she hid them and sat on them (31:34). Five readings follow.

- **To draw her father away from idolatry.** Genesis Rabbah 74:5: "her intentions were only for the sake of Heaven. She said: 'What, am I going to go on my way and leave this elder in his corruption?'" Rashi follows it: "her intention was to wean her father from idol-worship." The Pulpit Commentary names Basil, Gregory, Nazianzen and Theodoret for this reading (its page spells them "Bazil" and "Nazisnzen"). Benson hopes she acted "with a design to convince her father of the folly of his regard to those as gods which could not secure themselves," and Gill calls it "more to her credit and character."
- **To stop Laban learning their route from them.** Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer 36 says the teraphim "speak," citing Zechariah 10:2, "On that account had Rachel stolen them, so that they should not tell Laban that Jacob had fled, and not only that, but also to remove idolatrous worship from her father's house." Rashbam: "in order that Lavan should not be able to locate the whereabouts of Yaakov and his family." Ibn Ezra: Laban "was an astrologer, and Rachel feared that he would look at the stars and discover which way they fled." Radak holds the same, and Poole gives it first ("lest her father by consulting them should discover their flight"). Greenberg reports that most of the major medieval Hebrew commentators adopt this explanation, while "RaSI follows Genesis Rabba."
- **For protection on the road.** Josephus (*Antiquities* 1.19.9 in Whiston's numbering) writes that Rachel took them, "although Jacob had taught her to despise such worship," so that "in case they were pursued, and taken by her father, she might have recourse to these images, in order to obtain his pardon." Barnes: she took them "as relics of a home she was to visit no more, and as sources of safety to herself against the perils of her flight." Moshe Greenberg (*Journal of Biblical Literature*, 1962) builds on another passage of Josephus (*Antiquities* 18.9.5, in Louis Feldman's translation as Greenberg quotes it), where a widow carries off her family's images because "it is the custom among all the people in that country to have objects of worship in their house and to take them along when going abroad." He concludes that Rachel, leaving secretly, "absconded with the original images themselves," and adds that, like Michal, the other biblical woman known to have had teraphim, Rachel "was anxious for children." Michael reports that Gordon Wenham "opts for the interpretation provided by Josephus."
- **To claim inheritance or family headship (the Nuzi argument).** In the twentieth century, cuneiform tablets from Nuzi and Arrapha were read as explaining the theft. Greenberg quotes the key tablet (Gadd 51): an adopted son-in-law inherits, but a natural son "shall take the (house) gods." E. A. Speiser's Anchor Bible commentary, as Michael quotes it, holds that "possession of the house gods could signify legal title to a given estate, particularly in cases out of the ordinary, involving daughters, sons-in-law, or adopted sons," and remembers Rachel as "a resolute woman who did not shrink from taking the law--or what she believed to be the law--into her own hands." Ktziah Spanier (*Vetus Testamentum*, 1992), as Michael quotes her, reads the theft as a bid for family primacy over Leah: "Her objective was to prevail over her sister in the contest for family supremacy." Frymer-Kensky allows that the teraphim "may have had something to do with property rights," or "may have been primarily religious images, intended to invoke the protection of the ancestors."
- **Attachment to her family's gods.** Calvin: "It was not, then, the piety of Rachel, but her insane hankering after superstition which impelled her to the theft." The Pulpit Commentary's own conclusion is that the theft was "probably due to avarice," if the images were precious metal, "or to a taint of superstition," naming Chrysostom and Calvin. Ellicott thinks she took them "upon the supposition that they would bring prosperity to her and her husband." Poole's second reason is that "she seemed yet to retain a superstitious conceit of them."

**Objections each reading faces.**

- *Against drawing Laban from idolatry,* Calvin asks, "if this had been her design, why, in crossing the Euphrates, did she not cast away these abominations? Why did she not, after her departure, explain to her husband what she had done?" Ibn Ezra asks the same: "why did she take them with her and not bury them on the way?"
- *Against hiding the route,* Gill objects that it "looks as if she had an opinion of them," that is, it credits her with believing the images could speak.
- *Against protection on the road,* Greenberg grants of the earlier protection theories that "no material, biblical or extrabiblical, has yet been adduced in support of it closely paralleling the tale," and of his own Josephus parallel that "The passage is, to be sure, late."
- *Against the Nuzi argument,* Greenberg holds that the tablets show "bequeathal of the household gods determines" who is head of the family, not who inherits; that "it was not the gods that made the paterfamilias, but the father's act of bequeathing them"; and that an heir "had nothing to gain and everything to lose by making off with the family gods during the adopter's lifetime." M. J. Selman (*Tyndale Bulletin*, 1976) counts eleven Nuzi references to household gods and notes that "heirs also participated in the division who did not receive the gods," so that possession "clearly did not represent an automatic claim to an inheritance." C. H. Gordon, an early proponent of the theory, later wrote (as Greenberg quotes him) that "it is not likely that the gods conveyed valuable property rights," though their possession "may rather have betokened clan leadership."
- *Against attachment to idolatry,* Gill argues that "had she been tinctured with such sort of superstition and idolatry, she would never have used them so indecently, as to have sat upon them" in her condition.

These readings do not agree on what the teraphim were, what Rachel believed about them, or whether the theft was piety, prudence, a claim or a fault. This lesson does not decide between them.

**2. "I am having my period" (31:35): what was Rachel doing?** The BSB has, "Sir, do not be angry that I cannot stand up before you; for I am having my period." The Hebrew, in Bible Hub's interlinear, is *derekh nashim li*, glossed "the way of women [is]" followed by *li*, "to me"; the King James Version has "the custom of women is upon me." Radak compares Sarah, of whom Genesis 18:11 uses a related phrase. Readers divide on whether Rachel spoke truly, and on why Laban did not insist.

- **A lie added to a theft.** Calvin: "To her stratagem she also adds lying words, so that she deserves manifold censure." The Pulpit Commentary quotes Kalisch, "covering theft by subtlety and untruth," and judges that "It is just possible Rachel may have been speaking the exact truth, though the probability is she was guilty of fabrication."
- **A custom that explains the search.** Ramban asks, "Do women in that condition not rise or stand?" and answers that "in ancient days menstruants kept very isolated," so Rachel was saying, "I cannot come near you." Keil and Delitzsch think Laban stopped "less from fear of defilement, than because he regarded it as impossible that any one with the custom of women upon her should sit upon his gods." Barnes thinks the same, adding that the Levitical law "was not yet in force." Poole offers either real "infirmity and sickness" or a custom that "menstruous women were anciently esteemed polluted." Josephus: Laban stopped, "not supposing that his daughter in such circumstances would approach to those images." These readers explain the excuse without deciding whether it was true.
- **A miracle that spared her.** Genesis Rabbah 74:9 records Rabbi Yoḥanan: "He did not find household idols, but he found jugs. The household idols were transformed into jugs so as not to embarrass Rachel."
- **A trickster's words, and a mockery of the idols.** Michael summarizes Esther Fuchs (*Semeia*, 1988) as taking "the way of women" not as a statement about menstruation but as "a powerful idiom in the context of Genesis where women tricksters deceive their male counterparts." Frymer-Kensky: "Her womanhood perhaps disqualifies her from receiving the teraphim legitimately, so she uses her womanhood to prevent Laban from taking them away once she has taken them illegitimately." Michael sees "a mocking representation of the idols" in "a woman sitting down on some helpless idols."

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the "lie" reading, Josephus tells the excuse as fact ("her natural purgation hindered her rising up"), and the Pulpit Commentary itself grants that she may have spoken "the exact truth." Against Ramban's custom, Keil and Delitzsch and Barnes read Laban's restraint as respect or disbelief rather than fear of defilement. Against the miracle, Gill writes that Laban "did not oblige her to get up, nor could he imagine that ever the images could be under her in such circumstances," a natural account that, in this lesson's gloss, needs no change in the idols. Against Fuchs's idiom, Radak and Ramban read the words plainly as menstruation, and the narrator, unlike in 31:19, does not call them false. This lesson does not decide between these readings.

**3. "He shall not live" (31:32): does Jacob's oath bear on Rachel's death?** Jacob, not knowing, says, "If you find your gods with anyone here, he shall not live!" Laban never finds them. Four chapters later, "with her last breath—for she was dying—she named him Ben-oni" (35:18), and "So Rachel died and was buried on the way to Ephrath" (35:19). Genesis 35 says she died in hard labor; it does not mention the oath, and neither does Jacob's later memory, "to my sorrow Rachel died along the way" (48:7).

- **The oath took effect.** Genesis Rabbah 74:9 reads the oath and the two verses together: "and so it was, 'like an error that emerges from the ruler' (Ecclesiastes 10:5). 'Rachel stole...' (Genesis 31:19); 'Rachel died...' (Genesis 35:19)." In 74:4 Rabbi Yosei holds that "she died only from the curse of the elder." Rashi: "In consequence of this curse Rachel died on the journey." Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer 36 says that "the utterance of a righteous person is like the speech from the mouth of an angel," so Rachel "bare and died." Targum Pseudo-Jonathan renders the oath "let him die before his time."
- **A rash oath, and no more.** Ibn Ezra reads "He shall not live" as "Because I will put him to death," and answers the curse reading: "If this be so, let them tell us who prayed and thus caused the death of Phinehas' wife?" (who also died in childbirth, 1 Samuel 4:19-20). Rashbam: Jacob "himself will avenge such a crime." Gill reports that "the Jewish writers" link the oath to her death "but without any foundation." Poole: "A rash and inconsiderate sentence." In Sefaria's Sforno, an editor's bracketed note adds that Jacob "had not cursed the thief but the one with whom the stolen goods would be found," and Laban "never did find" them.

Genesis Rabbah 74:4 also keeps a different explanation beside Rabbi Yosei's, Rabbi Yudan's "Because she spoke before her sister," which shows that the rabbis did not agree on one cause either. *A pastoral note:* if you have lost someone and are asking whether words you once spoke caused it, that is a weight a study note cannot carry. A pastor, chaplain or grief counselor can walk through it with you. If grief ever brings thoughts of harming yourself, in the United States you can call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline; elsewhere, call your local emergency number.

**4. "May the LORD keep watch between you and me" (31:49): a wary pact, and a later blessing.** In context the words belong to a boundary treaty. Laban continues, "If you mistreat my daughters or take other wives, although no one is with us, remember that God is a witness between you and me" (31:50), and the heap is a witness "that I will not go past this mound to harm you, and you will not go past this mound and pillar to harm me" (31:52). The Hebrew *yitseph* is a jussive, which Bible Hub's interlinear glosses "May watch", the root of *Mizpah*, "watchtower."

- **Read in its setting: a call for God to watch and judge.** Calvin: "Laban commits to the judgment of God, for vengeance, whatever offense either of them should be guilty of against the other in his absence." Gill: Laban "imprecates that God would watch over them both." The Geneva Bible's note on "watch": "To punish the trespasser." Genesis Rabbah 74:14 (Rabbi Abbahu) hears the plural "we are absent" as "Until now we have been seeing one another, from now on we will not be seeing one another." Ibn Ezra reads it more gently: "We will be distant one from another and will not be able to see each other. Nevertheless, God will see each one of us."
- **Reused as a blessing for those apart.** In the nineteenth century the verse, in the King James wording, was engraved on rings and brooches. Lang Antiques describes "mass-produced gold, silver or brass rings or brooches that were particularly popular during the Victorian era," with motifs such as "twin hearts, flowers, birds, and anchors." The Art of Mourning essay reads such tokens as given in a time of "high mortality, large scale war, empire and mass transit," and argues that even the wary context strengthened the gift: "There is almost a threat involved if the pact is ever broken by the two, bolstering the sentiment of love."
- **The reuse questioned.** The Cambridge Bible: "The popular use of the word Mizpah, based on this verse, ignores the context, and, in particular, Genesis 31:50. God is here invoked, because of the mutual distrust of the two parties." John Walton, in the NIV Application Commentary (quoted secondhand from a Baker Book House staff blog), writes that using it as a benediction or on wedding rings shows "our misunderstanding of the words. Here in Genesis they express suspicion," and paraphrases Laban: "I don't trust you out of my sight, but since I can no longer personally hold you accountable, may God do so."

Each side has an answer. Against reading the verse only as suspicion, the Art of Mourning essay holds that the covenant and its warning are part of what the tokens meant, and Benson reads the name as a reminder of "the all-seeing eye of God, whose providence watches over the actions of mankind." Against the devotional reuse, Walton's and the Cambridge Bible's point is that 31:50 names the danger the watching guards against. This lesson reports the reuse and the objection to it, and does not rule on whether a reader may pray the words today.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, as a model of the shape, not an answer key. It does not build a bridge from the teraphim, the oath or Rachel's death, because Positions 1-3 show how sharply readers disagree about them.

**Passage element.** Genesis 31:7 and 31:41: Jacob says Laban "has cheated me and changed my wages ten times" and "you have changed my wages ten times!" With 31:14-15, where Leah and Rachel say their father "sold us" and "squandered what was paid for us."

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the complaint about changed terms is made twice, once to the wives and once to Laban's face, and the daughters add their own complaint about money taken from them. Inference (this lesson's own): for a first audience, the chapter names a household where the one who holds the property keeps changing the terms for those who work and depend on him. Whether Jacob's own conduct in 30:37-43 complicates his complaint is a question the Cambridge Bible raises and this bridge does not settle.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When the terms of work or support keep changing, it matters to write down what was agreed and to say out loud what has been taken.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: an agreement about pay, changed repeatedly by the stronger party, and people who name the pattern. Not carried forward: flight, theft, oaths, household gods, or any verdict on who in a modern workplace is "Laban."

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a narrative pattern applied by analogy, not a command. Promise scope: none. "God has not allowed him to harm me" (31:7) is Jacob's claim about Jacob; it is not a promise about anyone else's wages.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. A seasonal worker's employer keeps changing the hourly rate after the work is done, and deductions appear that were never agreed.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: truthfulness and lawful redress. One faithful response is to keep a dated record of hours, rates and pay stubs, raise it in writing, and seek outside help rather than leave in secret or take anything in return.

**Cautions.** The bridge does not say the worker should endure the pattern, or that every pay dispute is exploitation. Wage disputes have legal remedies. In the United States, the Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division has a toll-free line, 1-866-487-9243 (1-866-4-USWAGE), and a licensed employment attorney or legal aid office can advise; elsewhere, look for your country's labor authority. If money is being used to control you within a family, a counselor or legal aid office is a better next step than a study note.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. The narrator tells you that Rachel stole the idols (31:19) and that Jacob did not know (31:32), but not why. Which reading in Position 1 needs the fewest things the text does not say?
2. The BSB renders the same Hebrew verb "stole" for Rachel and "deceived" for Jacob (31:19-20). What does a reader lose or gain from the English choice?
3. Laban says, "Everything you see is mine!" (31:43); his daughters say they have no "portion or inheritance" (31:14). How do the two claims sit together?
4. Genesis 35:2-4 does not name Rachel. What would you need before saying the "foreign gods" include her father's idols?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 31:1-55 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) Give the two complaints, Jacob's (31:7, 31:41) and the daughters' (31:14-16), in the BSB's words. (c) Say what the two names in 31:47 mean and in which languages.
2. **Five-minute outline.** (0:00-1:00) the plot; (1:00-2:00) the key words and the daughters' complaint; (2:00-3:30) the five readings of the theft and their objections; (3:30-4:15) 31:35 and the oath of 31:32; (4:15-5:00) Mizpah in context and in later use.
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: the Nuzi reading explains so much of the Jacob-Laban story that it must be right. Weigh it against Greenberg's own warning, "A key that opens so many doors can hardly be the wrong one!", which he states in order to test it, and against Selman's count. A second candidate, running the other way: if Rachel's motive was "self-evident" to the first readers, as Greenberg thinks, why did the rabbis, Calvin and Josephus each find a different one?
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) It does not say why Rachel took the idols. (b) It does not say that Jacob's oath caused Rachel's death; 35:16-19 and 48:7 give childbirth and do not mention the oath. (c) It does not make Laban's words in 31:49 a blessing; in context they belong with 31:50-52.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** The reviewed connections attached to this lesson:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genesis 35:2-4 | motif | strong | The household puts away "foreign gods"; Josephus, Cambridge, Barnes, Pulpit, Ellicott and Greenberg include Laban's idols, Rashi and Gill do not, and 35:2 names none |
| Genesis 44:9-12 | motif | plausible | A death oath on whoever is "found" with a stolen object, then a search that ends with Rachel's son; Wenham and Sternberg via Michael; the Hebrew penalty differs |
| Joshua 24:2-15 | motif | plausible | Nahor and the fathers' gods "beyond the Euphrates"; Ibn Ezra on 31:53 cites Joshua; Joshua does not refer to Laban |
| Genesis 31:13 (from 28:18-22) | explicit_reference | explicit | Already authored with the Bethel lesson (conn-gen28-bethel-gen31-13); listed here for reference |

Choose one row and defend it, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` for a candidate. Four candidates this lesson chose not to author:

- **31:32 to 35:16-19 (the oath and Rachel's death).** Genesis Rabbah, Rashi, Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer and Targum Pseudo-Jonathan make the link; Ibn Ezra, Rashbam, Gill and Poole do not; the narrator draws none. A connection row would put a disputed reading into the app's map as if it were a link in the text: `no_warrant_yet`.
- **The New Testament.** By script, "Laban" occurs in 55 BSB verses, none in the New Testament, and neither "Mizpah" nor "Galeed" occurs there. Matthew 2:18 ("Rachel weeping for her children") quotes Jeremiah 31:15 and belongs with Rachel's death in Genesis 35. No forward thread from this chapter survived checking: `no_warrant_yet`.
- **1 Samuel 19:13-16 (Michal's household idol).** Ibn Ezra uses it to show teraphim had human form, and Greenberg pairs the two women; a woman deceiving with a household idol is a real parallel, but no reader read here argues one story echoes the other. A candidate for a 1 Samuel lesson.
- **Judges 11:29 ("Mizpah of Gilead").** Rashi and Rashbam identify the place; the Cambridge Bible calls Mizpah "Probably a common name for a height." A place identification, not a thread.

## Sources and Limits

- **BSB text.** Every BSB-attributed quotation in this lesson and its three connection files was printed by a Node script from `web/public/bible/BSB/*.json` before drafting and re-checked afterward by a second script asserting each fragment is a literal substring of the cited verse or verses. Every count was made by script over the same files, case-insensitive and on word boundaries.
- **Hebrew.** Hebrew forms (*teraphim*, *elohim*, *ganav*, *derekh nashim*, *paḥad*, *yitseph*, and the verbs of 31:32 and 44:9) were read on Bible Hub's interlinear and Strong's pages, not in BHS. The eight occurrences of *ganav* were counted on the interlinear and assigned to verses by their order on the page.
- **Read directly, with raw copies saved:** every source in `sources` not listed below as secondhand. Sefaria texts came through its API; Greenberg, Selman and Michael were read as PDFs.
- **Secondhand only, and labeled:** Speiser, Spanier, Wenham, Fuchs and Sternberg (through Michael); Gordon, Draffkorn, Smith, Gunkel and Josephus's *Antiquities* 18.9.5 in Feldman's translation (through Greenberg); Kalisch, Basil, Gregory, Theodoret and Chrysostom (through the Pulpit Commentary); Walton (through the Baker Book House blog). On Bible Hub's 31:19 and 31:35 pages, Poole's question-and-answer notes appear without a heading after Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; Poole's own chapter page was fetched to confirm the attribution.
- **Not found or not used.** No modern critical study of "the Fear of Isaac" was read, so readings that treat it as an old divine title are not reported. No Catholic or Orthodox magisterial or liturgical source on Genesis 31 was read; that is a gap in this lesson's sourcing, not a claim that those traditions are silent. No source was found that calls the Mizpah jewelry a "misuse"; Walton's word is "misunderstanding," and the Cambridge Bible's is "ignores the context." The Art of Mourning and Lang Antiques pages are a collector's essay and a dealer's reference, not histories.
- **Pastoral referrals.** The Wage and Hour Division number was read on dol.gov on 2026-10-08 through a web-fetch tool (the site refused a raw request). The 988 details follow this app's existing registry entry for a US service. Numbers can change; readers outside the United States should use local services.
