Thirteen years after Ishmael's birth (16:16), "When Abram was ninety-nine years old, the LORD appeared to him" (17:1). The chapter is mostly speech: two new names, a covenant called "everlasting" three times (17:7, 17:13, 17:19), a sign cut into the body of every male in the household, a son promised by name, and a prayer for the son already born. The rite it institutes is still central to Jewish life as *brit milah*, "the covenant of circumcision." Christian traditions read the chapter through Paul and through baptism, and disagree with one another about it. This lesson reports those readings in each tradition's own words and does not choose among them. Every BSB quotation was checked by script against this app's shipped text (see Sources and Limits).

## Context

**What happens, in order (17:1-27).** The LORD says, "I am God Almighty. Walk before Me and be blameless" (17:1), and Abram falls facedown (17:3). God renames him Abraham, "for I have made you a father of many nations" (17:5), and promises "an everlasting covenant" with his descendants (17:7) and the land "as an eternal possession" (17:8). God gives the sign: "Every male among you must be circumcised" (17:10), "when he is eight days old" (17:12), and an uncircumcised male "will be cut off from his people" (17:14). God renames Sarai as Sarah and promises "a son by her" (17:15-16). Abraham laughs (17:17) and asks, "O that Ishmael might live under Your blessing!" (17:18). God answers that the covenant will be established with Isaac, and that Ishmael will be blessed (17:19-21). "On that very day" Abraham circumcises Ishmael, aged thirteen, and every male of his household, and is circumcised himself at ninety-nine (17:23-27).

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens (`content/lens/eleven-stages.json`) files Genesis 12 through Malachi under stage 5, "Israel: The Twelve Tribes." Chapter 15 ended in a rite that Abram prepared (15:10) but did not pass through: while he lay in "a deep sleep" (15:12), "a smoking firepot and a flaming torch appeared and passed between the halves of the carcasses" (15:17). Chapter 17 is the first scene that puts an obligation on Abram's side: "You must keep My covenant" (17:9).

**Key words, read as observations.**

- **New names, and one that the text explains.** For Abraham the text gives a reason: "for I have made you a father of many nations" (17:5). For Sarah it gives none (17:15-16). The meanings found in commentaries are the commentators' own, and they disagree. Keil and Delitzsch derive "Abraham" from Arabic *ruhâm*, multitude. The Cambridge Bible calls the link "a resemblance through assonance, and not a real derivation of a proper name." Rashi reads "Sarai" as "my princess" ("a princess to me and not to others") and "Sarah" as "princess over all." The Cambridge Bible says Sarai "cannot mean, as used to be asserted," my princess. By script, "Abram" occurs for the last time in Genesis at 17:5, and "Sarai" for the last time anywhere at 17:15.
- **"God Almighty" (17:1).** On this lesson's script check, 17:1 is the first verse in the BSB with "God Almighty." The phrase occurs 12 times in all, five of them in Genesis. The Hebrew behind it, *El Shaddai*, is read three ways by three medieval Jewish commentators. Rashi: "I am He whose (ש) Godship suffices (די) for every creature," where the bracketed Hebrew letters show the wordplay he finds in the name. Ibn Ezra: "Shaddai is an adjective meaning mighty." Ramban, rejecting Ibn Ezra's reading, says "These are two distinct Divine names, each one descriptive in itself."
- **"Covenant" thirteen times.** By script, the word "covenant" occurs exactly 13 times in the BSB of Genesis 17. The count is old. The Mishnah (Nedarim 3:11) reports Rabbi Yishmael saying, "So great is the mitzva of circumcision that thirteen covenants were sealed with regard to it, for the word covenant appears thirteen times in the biblical passage that discusses circumcision." Maimonides lists the thirteen phrases one by one (Mishneh Torah, Circumcision 3:9). The BSB happens to preserve the Hebrew count in English.
- **"A sign of the covenant."** On this lesson's script check, "sign of the covenant" occurs in only four verses of the whole BSB: three in the Noah covenant (9:12, 9:13, 9:17), where the sign is the rainbow, and here (17:11). "Everlasting covenant" also appears first in Genesis 9 (9:16), then at 17:7, 17:13 and 17:19. Both Genesis covenants with a named sign are called everlasting. What readers make of the parallel is their own step.
- **"Be blameless" (17:1) and the rite.** Jewish tradition has connected that word with circumcision itself. The Mishnah (Nedarim 3:11) reports Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi as saying that "despite all the mitzvot that Abraham our Patriarch did, he was not called wholehearted until he circumcised himself," citing this verse. Rashi gives that reading as the Midrash's, alongside a plainer one ("be whole-hearted in all the trials I impose upon you"). Ibn Ezra glosses the command differently: "Do not question the reason for circumcision."
- **"Cut off from his people" (17:14).** By script, the phrase *cut off from his* (or *her*, or *their*) *people*, in exactly that wording, occurs first at 17:14. It occurs 11 times in the BSB, and every other occurrence is in Exodus, Leviticus or Numbers. Seven more verses use the variant "cut off from among" a people (Exodus 31:14; Leviticus 17:4, 18:29, 20:5, 20:18; Numbers 15:30; Acts 3:23). The verse does not state what the penalty involves. Rashi reads it as "he shall depart hence childless and shall suffer an untimely death." Rabbi Daniel Kohn (My Jewish Learning) writes that "it is unclear what the punishment of being" cut off "means in the biblical context," and that "it is clear that circumcision is a necessary condition for a male to be included in the divine promise to the Jewish people."
- **Who receives the sign.** The command covers "those born in your household and those purchased from a foreigner—even those who are not your offspring" (17:12). It is carried out on "every male among the members of Abraham's household" (17:23), including Ishmael, who is told in the same speech that the covenant will be established with Isaac (17:21). The text does not explain why the sign goes to people outside the covenant line or in the household by purchase; the household slavery behind "purchased with his money" (17:23) is taken up in the Genesis 16 lesson.
- **Abraham laughs (17:17).** The text reports the laugh and the question Abraham asks himself, but not whether the laugh was joy, astonishment, or doubt. Onkelos renders the verb "laughed [rejoiced]." Calvin calls Abraham "as one astonished," while denying that it was "the language of one who doubts." The comparison with Sarah's laugh in 18:12 belongs to the next unit.

**Ishmael: blessed, circumcised, and not the covenant line (17:18-21).** Abraham's only spoken request is for the son he already has. The answer has two parts: "As for Ishmael, I have heard you, and I will surely bless him" (17:20), and "But I will establish My covenant with Isaac" (17:21). The Pulpit Commentary reads "I have heard thee" as "an allusion to the significance of the name Ishmael" (compare 16:11, "the LORD has heard your cry of affliction"). Named readers differ on what the distinction leaves Ishmael. Rashi, on 17:19, reads it as excluding "the children of Ishmael and the children of Keturah" from the covenant's establishment: "The Covenant of circumcision shall be transmitted only to the descendants of Isaac." Jamieson-Fausset-Brown hold that "though the visible Church did not descend from his family, yet personally he might, and it is to be hoped did, enjoy its benefits." What the text gives is a real blessing and a real distinction, side by side, and Ishmael receives the sign on the same day as his father (17:26). The previous lesson reports how one verse about Ishmael (16:12) has been misused against Arab peoples. Nothing in 17:18-21 describes any later people. The blessing of 17:20 is spoken about Ishmael and his own line, and it is a blessing.

**What the New Testament says about circumcision, laid out text by text.** Several New Testament passages address circumcision. Only some of them point to this chapter (see the connections). Read side by side in the BSB, they say different things for different audiences:

- To Gentile believers in Galatia who were considering it: "if you let yourselves be circumcised, Christ will be of no value to you at all" (Galatians 5:2). In the same passage: "in Christ Jesus neither circumcision nor uncircumcision has any value. All that matters is faith, expressed through love" (5:6). And again: "neither circumcision nor uncircumcision means anything. What counts is a new creation" (6:15).
- To Corinth: "Was a man already circumcised when he was called? He should not become uncircumcised. Was a man still uncircumcised when called? He should not be circumcised" (1 Corinthians 7:18).
- In Rome, on Jewish advantage: "What, then, is the advantage of being a Jew? Or what is the value of circumcision?" (Romans 3:1) "Much in every way" (3:2), and earlier, "circumcision is a matter of the heart, by the Spirit, not by the written code" (2:29).
- At the Jerusalem council, the question is whether Gentiles must be circumcised. Some teach, "Unless you are circumcised according to the custom of Moses, you cannot be saved" (Acts 15:1). James's judgment is that "we should not cause trouble for the Gentiles who are turning to God" (15:19). Soon after, Paul circumcises Timothy, "the son of a believing Jewish woman and a Greek father" (16:1), "on account of the Jews in that area" (16:3).

None of these passages quotes Genesis 17 or names Abraham's circumcision. Galatians 5 and Acts 15 concern whether Gentile believers should take on the rite. That is a different question from what the rite means for Jews, and a reader should not merge the two. Acts 15:1 ties the custom to "Moses," while John 7:22 places its origin "from the patriarchs." How Christian traditions put these texts together is the subject of Positions (1) and (2).

**Brit milah in Jewish life today.** Circumcision on the eighth day remains a defining practice across the Jewish movements whose own published materials were read for this lesson. Rabbi Carl Astor, in material from the Conservative movement's *The Observant Life*, writes that "The purpose of b'rit milah is to mark the eternal covenant between God and the Jewish people." He writes that circumcision "represents both an obligation on the part of the Jewish people to fulfill the mitzvot, and God's concomitant promise to protect and watch over Israel." He adds that because it "is deemed symbolic of all the mitzvot," Scripture calls it an everlasting pact at Genesis 17:13. The Union for Reform Judaism calls it "the oldest religious rite in Judaism," first mentioned "in Genesis 17." It gives the blessing the father recites at the ceremony: "who has sanctified us through Your mitzvot and has commanded us to bring our sons into the covenant of Abraham our father." Rabbi Daniel Kohn (My Jewish Learning) explains the name: "Milah by itself means circumcision. However, it is always referred to as brit milah, including the word brit, or covenant." These sources describe a living practice that its own tradition understands as the covenant of this chapter. This lesson reports that understanding as such and does not treat it as superseded; where Christian writers below take a different view, that is their position, reported in their words. The Reform page also describes ceremonies welcoming daughters "into the covenant of the Jewish people," which this lesson did not study further. A medical question about circumcising a child belongs with a physician, as well as with the family's own tradition.

## Literary Design

**Five speeches, and "As for..."** God speaks five times, each introduced by its own narrative formula (17:1, 17:3, 17:9, 17:15, 17:19). Between them Abraham falls facedown twice (17:3, 17:17), speaks once to himself (17:17) and once aloud (17:18). Three times the BSB begins a section of God's speech with "As for": "As for Me" (17:4), "As for Sarai your wife" (17:15), and "As for Ishmael" (17:20). Rashi links the "and as for thee" of 17:9 to the "As for Me" of 17:4, glossing God's side as "As for Me, behold My covenant is with thee" and Abraham's as "and as for thee, thou shalt be careful to keep it" (Rashi on 17:9). The BSB's "God also said to Abraham" at 17:9 does not show that pairing. This lesson did not check it against an interlinear (see Sources and Limits).

**The promise widens, then narrows.** It moves outward to "many nations" (17:4-6) and "kings of peoples" (17:16), then inward to one unborn son as the covenant line (17:19, 17:21). The sign is given to the whole household (17:12, 17:27) while the covenant is "established" with Isaac alone (17:21). Whether the narrator built this deliberately is not settled here.

## Positions

Two questions follow. Each is reported through named readers in their own words. Neither is adjudicated here, and nothing in a position's statement should be read as this lesson's own conclusion.

**1. What does "an everlasting covenant" (17:7, 17:13, 17:19) promise about the covenant and its sign?** At 17:13 the text attaches the word to the sign itself: "My covenant in your flesh will be an everlasting covenant." The land question ("an eternal possession," 17:8, the only BSB verse with that phrase, by script) is left to the Genesis 13 lesson, which reports five readings of "forever." Here the question is the covenant and its sign.

- **Jewish readings: an everlasting covenant with Abraham's descendants, kept in the flesh.** Sforno, on 17:13, says it is fitting that "the covenant which represents the eternal intimate and reciprocal relationship of G'd and the Jewish people should be symbolized in this manner," in the organ "instrumental in ensuring a man's continuity beyond death." Maimonides writes, "How great is the circumcision!" He rules that a Jew who "breaks the covenant of Abraham our Patriarch and leaves his foreskin uncircumcised," or hides his circumcision, "does not have a portion in the world to come... despite the fact that he has studied Torah and performed good deeds" (Circumcision 3:8; the translator's notes refer to Hellenistic-era Jews who hid their circumcision and point to Mishnah Avot 3:11). It is a law about Jews and their own covenant, not a statement about non-Jews, whose circumcision Maimonides treats separately (3:7). Rashi, on 17:19, reads the covenant of circumcision as "transmitted only to the descendants of Isaac." The modern movements' statements quoted in Context show the same reading in present practice.
- **A sixteenth-century Reformed reading (Calvin): the covenant everlasting in Christ, the outward sign ended.** Calvin, on 17:13, weighs two meanings: "either that God promises that his grace, of which circumcision was a sign and pledge, should be eternal; or that he intended the sign itself to be perpetually observed. Indeed, I have no doubt that this perpetuity ought to be referred to the visible sign. But they who hence infer, that the use of it ought to flourish among the Jews even of the present time, are (in my opinion) deceived." His reason is that "since Christ is the end of the law, the perpetuity which is ascribed to the ceremonies of the law, was terminated as soon as Christ appeared." He continues: "although the use of circumcision has ceased," it remains "an everlasting, or perpetual covenant, if only Christ be regarded as the Mediator." On 17:7 he calls the covenant "perpetual, as lasting until the renovation of the world; which took place at the advent of Christ." He grants that it "may with propriety be called eternal, as far as the whole Church is concerned," but adds at once that "the regular succession of ages was partly broken, and partly changed, by the coming of Christ," and that Abraham's natural descendants were in the end, in his word, disinherited.
- **A nineteenth-century Lutheran reading (Keil and Delitzsch): eternal in essence, not in its first institution.** Keil and Delitzsch, both German Lutheran scholars (per Wikipedia, a tertiary source), write that "Eternal duration was promised only to the covenant established by God with the seed of Abraham, which was to grow into a multitude of nations, but not to the covenant institution which God established in connection with the lineal posterity of Abraham." So "it was only in its essence that circumcision could be a sign of the eternal covenant."
- **A twentieth-century dispensational reading (Walvoord): unconditional and still to be fulfilled for Israel.** John F. Walvoord (*Bibliotheca Sacra*, 1952) cites the words "for ever" (13:15), "everlasting covenant" (17:7), and "everlasting possession" (17:8; KJV wording, where the BSB has "eternal possession"). He argues that their "emphasis upon its unending application" (the promise's) "carries with it the necessity of complete and unconditional fulfillment." His article applies this mainly to the land promise and to a future possession of the land by Israel, which the Genesis 13 lesson reports in more detail. It does not address circumcision.
- **A Catholic statement.** The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* states that the books of the Old Testament "retain a permanent value, for the Old Covenant has never been revoked" (CCC 121). It describes Jesus's own circumcision "on the eighth day after his birth" as "the sign of his incorporation into Abraham's descendants, into the people of the covenant" (CCC 527). The Catechism paragraphs read for this lesson do not address how 17:13 bears on Jewish practice today.

**How this chapter has been used against Jews.** Early Christian writers turned this chapter against Jews directly. The Epistle of Barnabas (ch. 9) says that "the circumcision in which they trusted is abolished," and attributes Jewish practice to deception by "an evil angel." Justin Martyr (*Dialogue with Trypho* 16) reads the sign "which is from Abraham" as given so that Jews would be set apart to suffer what he calls a just punishment, with their land made desolate. Genesis 17 says nothing of the kind. The sign is "a sign of the covenant between Me and you" (17:11), and the covenant is "everlasting" (17:7, 17:13, 17:19). The Catholic Church's Second Vatican Council repudiated this kind of use in 1965: "the Jews should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God, as if this followed from the Holy Scriptures," and the Church "decries hatred, persecutions, displays of anti-Semitism, directed against Jews at any time and by anyone" (*Nostra Aetate* 4). Reporting any reading in this lesson is not endorsing such use. The Christian readings above rely on New Testament texts that Genesis 17 does not contain. As noted in Context, those texts speak mainly to whether Gentile believers should be circumcised, and the readings differ in how far they carry those texts to Jewish practice. A reader for whom this question touches family, faith, or community should take it to a rabbi, pastor, or teacher in their own tradition. A study note can report the positions but cannot settle them.

**2. Does baptism correspond to circumcision (Colossians 2:11-12)?** Colossians 2:11 reads, "In Him you were also circumcised, in the putting off of your sinful nature, with the circumcision performed by Christ and not by human hands." Verse 12 (2:12) continues, "And having been buried with Him in baptism, you were raised with Him through your faith in the power of God, who raised Him from the dead." Paul names circumcision and baptism in consecutive verses. He does not name Abraham or Genesis 17 (see the Colossians connection). Christian traditions that agree on much else divide sharply over whether this passage, read with Genesis 17 and Romans 4:11, supports baptizing the infants of believers. The two positions below are given in their own confessions' words, then with one historic commentator and one modern pastor or church from each side.

- **Reformed paedobaptist: baptism succeeds circumcision as the covenant sign, and the children of believers receive it.** The Westminster Confession (28.4) states, "Not only those that do actually profess faith in and obedience unto Christ, but also the infants of one, or both, believing parents, are to be baptized." Its proof texts for that sentence, in the edition read for this lesson, begin with "Gen. 17:7–8" and include "Col. 2:11–12" and "Rom. 4:11–12." Chapter 27.5 adds that "The sacraments of the old testament, in regard of the spiritual things thereby signified and exhibited, were, for substance, the same with those of the new." Calvin (*Institutes* 4.16.4) argues that the promise and "the thing figured" are "one and the same" in both signs, and that "The only difference which remains is in the external ceremony." He concludes that "baptism has been substituted for circumcision, and performs the same office." On Colossians he argues that "the apostle makes the one to be not a whit more spiritual than the other" (4.16.11). He answers the timing question from Genesis 17 itself: "Why does the sacrament come after faith in Abraham, and precede all intelligence in his son Isaac?" An adult entering the covenant "should previously learn its conditions; but it is not so with the infant born to him" (4.16.24). In the same section he holds that adults coming from outside "are not to receive the sign of baptism without previous faith and repentance." A 2019 Q&A published by the Orthodox Presbyterian Church grants that Colossians 2:11-12 "does indeed support the credobaptist position" for an unbaptized adult believer. It then appeals to Genesis 17:7 and says "circumcision was replaced by baptism as the sign of the covenant," adding that "baptism, for a child, is not a sign of the baptized child's faith. Nor was circumcision."
- **Baptist credobaptist: the counterpart of circumcision is the new birth, and baptism follows profession of faith.** The Second London Baptist Confession (1689), chapter 29.2, states, "Those who do actually profess repentance towards God, faith in, and obedience to, our Lord Jesus Christ, are the only proper subjects of this ordinance." Chapter 29.1 calls baptism "a sign of his fellowship with him, in his death and resurrection; of his being engrafted into him," citing Colossians 2:12 among its proof texts. Neither paragraph cites Genesis 17. John Gill (1697-1771), whom Wikipedia (a tertiary source) describes as "an English Particular Baptist theologian," reads "the circumcision made without hands" of Colossians 2:11 as "that of the heart, in the spirit," the "internal circumcision, the antitype and perfection of circumcision in the flesh; for the former, and not the latter, is here meant." On 2:12 he writes that "without faith, this ordinance will be of no use." John Piper, whose Desiring God biography says he served 33 years as pastor of Bethlehem Baptist Church in Minneapolis, puts the argument this way in a 1997 sermon: "What is the New Testament counterpart or parallel to the Old Testament rite of circumcision? Answer: it is not the New Testament rite of baptism; it is the New Testament spiritual event of the circumcision of Christ." He presses the phrase "through faith" in 2:12. He argues that "If baptism were merely a parallel of the Old Testament rite of circumcision it would not have to happen" through faith, "since infants did not take on circumcision" that way.

Other traditions that baptize infants frame the link in their own terms. The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* says of Jesus's circumcision that "This sign prefigures that" circumcision of Christ "which is Baptism" (CCC 527). Lutheran and Orthodox statements on this passage were not read for this lesson.

These two sides read the same verses and reach opposite practices. In the sources quoted above (this summary is the lesson's own), each side answers the other's strongest text: the Reformed side answers "through your faith" with Genesis 17's infant sign, and the Baptist side answers Genesis 17 with "not by human hands" (Colossians 2:11). This lesson reports these positions and does not rank them. Whether, when, and how to baptize a child, or to be baptized oneself, is not a study-note question. A reader working this out for their own family needs a pastor, not a study note.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, a model of the shape and not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate step. This example deliberately does not build a bridge from the rite itself (17:10-14), since Positions (1) and (2) show how differently traditions take its ongoing force.

**Passage element.** Genesis 17:18-21. Abraham asks, "O that Ishmael might live under Your blessing!" The covenant will go to Isaac, but the answer begins "As for Ishmael, I have heard you, and I will surely bless him" (17:20).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text records the request, states that it was heard, and keeps the blessing and the distinction side by side. Inference: a first audience who knew both lines as real peoples would likely have heard that a request for someone outside the chosen line was neither dismissed nor fully granted. The text states no such moral.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When a request on someone's behalf cannot be granted, it matters whether the answer says plainly that it was heard and names what can be given.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: an advocate, a decision-maker who cannot grant the main request, and an answer that states both the "no" and the real provision. Not carried forward: the divine speaker, covenant election, the blessings of 17:20, and any claim about how God answers prayer.

**Application class and promise scope.** Principle carried by analogy, not a command. Promise scope: none claimed.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. A team lead asks her director to promote a long-serving colleague into a role already filled.

**Response type and faithful response.** An honest partial yes. The director tells her the request was heard, says plainly the role will not change, and names what will actually be done for the colleague, without vagueness or promises that cannot be kept.

**Cautions.** Not every distinction is fair; some decisions are unjust and should be named as unjust. If discrimination, retaliation, or an employment-law question is involved, a qualified person, not a study note, is the right next step. The bridge is an analogy the learner is testing.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. The text explains Abraham's new name (17:5) and does not explain Sarah's (17:15). Before reading any commentator, what does 17:15-16 itself say happens to Sarah when her name changes? Is there anything in the verse that a meaning for "Sarah" would have to fit?
2. Abraham falls facedown and laughs (17:17), then asks aloud for Ishmael (17:18). Reading only 17:15-19, does the text give you enough to decide whether the laugh is joy, astonishment, or doubt? What would you be bringing from elsewhere?
3. Ishmael is told he will not carry the covenant (17:21), yet he is circumcised the same day as Abraham (17:26). What does the text itself say the sign marks, according to 17:11? Does that wording, by itself, explain why Ishmael receives it?
4. Paul reads the order of Genesis 15 and 17: Abraham's faith was credited "not after, but before" his circumcision (Romans 4:10). Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi reads 17:1 against Abraham's earlier deeds: Abraham "was not called wholehearted until he circumcised himself" (Mishnah Nedarim 3:11). What does each need the text to show, and does Genesis 17 itself rank faith and the sign?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate Genesis 17 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) Quote in the BSB what God says the sign is (17:11) and what happens to a male who is not circumcised (17:14). (c) Name one New Testament passage that quotes or names this chapter, and its connection type.
