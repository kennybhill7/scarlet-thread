After the binding of Isaac, Genesis turns to two pieces of family news. The first is a birth notice from far away: Abraham's brother Nahor has sons, and a granddaughter named Rebekah (22:20-24). The second is a death at home: "Sarah lived to be 127 years old," and "Abraham went out to mourn and to weep for her" (23:1-2). The rest of the chapter is a negotiation in which Abraham, "a foreigner and an outsider" (23:4), buys a field and a cave from a man named Ephron for "four hundred shekels of silver" (23:16). This lesson reads the negotiation closely, checks by script the words it introduces, and reports, without settling them, three real disagreements among named readers: how to judge Ephron, what the purchase meant, and how to explain Stephen's account of it in Acts 7:16. Some older comment on Ephron generalized from him to whole peoples, and the lesson names that misuse plainly in Positions 1. Readers who are grieving will find a short note and resources at the end of Positions.

## Context

**What happens, in order (22:20-23:20).** Abraham hears of Nahor's eight sons by Milcah, ending with Bethuel, and "Bethuel became the father of Rebekah" (22:20-23); a concubine bears four more (22:24). Sarah dies "in Kiriath-arba (that is, Hebron) in the land of Canaan" (23:2). Abraham asks the Hittites for "a burial site" (23:3-4), and they offer "the finest of our tombs" (23:6). He asks them to approach "Ephron son of Zohar" for "the cave of Machpelah," "for full price" (23:7-9). Ephron offers the field and cave as a gift (23:10-11); Abraham insists on paying (23:12-13); Ephron names "four hundred shekels of silver" (23:15); Abraham weighs it out (23:16). The field passes to Abraham before witnesses, and he buries Sarah there (23:17-20).

**Where this unit sits.** Stage 5 of this app's eleven-stage lens ("Israel: The Twelve Tribes," machine-readable `chapters: ["1.12"]`). The previous lesson, on the binding of Isaac, already reports the midrash that ties Sarah's death to the binding (Genesis Rabbah 58:5; Rashi on 23:2); it is not repeated here. The text does not say how long after the binding Sarah died. Ramban reasons from 22:19 that Abraham had settled at Beersheba, so that "Sarah did not die during that period immediately following the Binding," and then offers a way to reconcile this with the rabbinic tradition. Rebekah's appearance in 22:23 sets up the next lesson (Genesis 24), where she is introduced as "the daughter of Bethuel son of Milcah" (24:15). Barnes calls 22:20-24 a notice "to explain and prepare the way for the marriage of Isaac." Rashi, following Genesis Rabbah 57:3, reads it as the answer to Abraham's worry after Moriah: he was told "that Rebecca, the one fit to be his (Isaac's) consort, had been born." Genesis Rabbah 58:2 puts the sequence as a proverb: "Before the Holy One blessed be He caused Sarah's sun to set, Rebecca's sun rose."

**Key words, read as observations.** Each count below was made by this lesson's own script over the app's BSB text, unless a Hebrew source is named.

- **"127 years" (23:1).** Keil writes that "Sarah is the only woman whose age is mentioned in the Scriptures," and Barnes that she "is the only woman whose age is recorded in Scripture." As worded, the claim is too broad: Mark 5:42 says of Jairus's daughter, "She was twelve years old," and Luke 2:37 says Anna "was a widow to the age of eighty-four." Read more narrowly, as the only woman whose full lifespan is recorded, the claim may hold, but this lesson's scripts did not test that version.
- **"to mourn and to weep for her" (23:2).** In this app's BSB, 23:2 is the first verse in the Bible with any form of "mourn." Bible Hub's concordance likewise lists 23:2 as the first occurrence of the Hebrew verb *saphad* ("to mourn, to lament"). "Weep" is not new here. Hagar "lifted up her voice and wept" at 21:16, as she waited for her son to die, and the concordance for *bakah* lists 21:16 first and 23:2 second. Genesis 23:2 is the first verse in which someone weeps for a person who has already died. Jamieson-Fausset-Brown distinguish the two verbs: "the 'weeping' indicates the natural outburst of his sorrow." Calvin writes that "to feel no sadness at the contemplation of death, is rather barbarism and stupor than fortitude of mind." He also reads Abraham's rising from the body (23:3) as "spoken in praise of his moderation," following Ambrose. The Cambridge Bible explains that verb more plainly, by "the habitual attitude of prostration in mourning."
- **"I am a foreigner and an outsider among you" (23:4).** The exact phrase occurs only here in this app's BSB. "Outsider" first occurs here, and its only other Genesis occurrence is 31:15. In Hebrew the pair is *ger* and *toshab*. Bible Hub's concordance lists 23:4 as the first occurrence of *toshab*. *Ger* first appears at 15:13, where God tells Abram that "your descendants will be strangers in a land that is not their own." Abraham now uses that word of himself. Ibn Ezra, in his translators' English, takes *ger* alone as "a transient stranger" and *toshab* as "a resident stranger." Radak has Abraham say he is a stranger "seeing that I have come from another country," yet a resident, "seeing that I have lived among you for many years and I intend to continue to remain among you." The Cambridge Bible calls it "a proverbial phrase." The Septuagint renders it Πάροικος καὶ παρεπίδημος, a pair that matters for the connections below.
- **"God's chosen one among us" (23:6).** The Cambridge Bible gives the Hebrew as "a prince of God," "The Hebrew idiom for the superlative." Ibn Ezra explains it, "Because thou art a prophet."
- **"four hundred shekels of silver" (23:15-16).** In this app's BSB, 23:15 is the first verse in the Bible with "shekel." Barnes agrees: "The shekel is here mentioned for the first time. It was originally a weight, not a coin."
- **The first purchase of land narrated in Genesis.** A script scan of Genesis for "buy," "bought," "purchase," "sell" and "sold" finds no earlier verse about land. The only earlier hits (17:12, 17:13, 17:23, 17:27) concern servants "purchased from a foreigner" or "purchased." Abraham's earlier claim at Beersheba was to a well, not to land: "as my witness that I dug this well" (21:30). The Cambridge Bible calls the chapter "the tradition respecting the first possession of Canaanite soil by the Israelite patriarchs." Matthew Henry notes "that a burying-place was the only piece of the land which Abraham possessed in Canaan."
- **"Machpelah."** The name occurs in six verses of this app's BSB, all in Genesis (23:9, 23:17, 23:19, 25:9, 49:30, 50:13). The Septuagint renders it "the double cave" (τὸ σπήλαιον τὸ διπλοῦν); the Cambridge Bible and Ramban take it as the name of the locality. Abraham, Isaac, Rebekah, Leah and Jacob are later buried there (25:9; 49:31; 50:13).
- **Ephron's name, letter by letter (23:16).** In Sefaria's Hebrew text ("Miqra according to the Masorah"), the name is spelled with a *vav* in 23:8, 23:10, 23:13, 23:14, 23:17 and the first time in 23:16. The second time in 23:16, after the silver is weighed, it is spelled without one (לעפרן). Positions 1 shows why readers care.

**Machpelah today.** The traditional site at Hebron lies within a monumental enclosure that UNESCO's 2017 World Heritage listing dates to the first century AD. The listing describes it as venerated by Judaism, Christianity and Islam. Present-day questions about the site lie outside this lesson.

## Literary Design

**A negotiation in five moves.** The Cambridge Bible lays out the stages: "Abraham in Genesis 23:9 asks to buy the cave only; Ephron in Genesis 23:11 offers to give the whole field and the cave in it for nothing; Abraham in Genesis 23:13 offers to pay for the field; Ephron in Genesis 23:15 mentions the price for the land; Abraham in Genesis 23:16 duly pays for the field and the cave." The object grows from "the cave of Machpelah ... at the end of his field" (23:9) to "the field ... and the cave that is in it" (23:11). Abraham's word for the transaction moves from "give" (23:4) to "sell" and "full price" (23:9) to "pay" (23:13).

**Refrains the English partly hides.**

- *"Listen."* "Listen" occurs five times in the BSB of chapter 23 (23:6, 23:8, 23:11, 23:13, 23:15), in five of the chapter's six speeches. In the Hebrew text this lesson's script finds a sixth form of the same verb, *shama*, at 23:16, where the BSB has "Abraham agreed to Ephron's terms." The King James wording printed on the Cambridge Bible's page keeps the verb, "Abraham hearkened unto Ephron," so its last use describes the act that closes the sale.
- *"Bury your dead."* "Dead" occurs eight times in seven verses (23:3, 23:4, 23:6 twice, 23:8, 23:11, 23:13, 23:15). In nearly every speech one side or the other says it, and Ephron ends both of his offers with "Bury your dead" (23:11, 23:15).
- *Witnesses.* "Presence" or "hearing" occurs in six verses (23:9, 23:10, 23:11, 23:13, 23:16, 23:18). "The gate of his city" frames the sale, at 23:10 and 23:18. The Cambridge Bible: "The presence of witnesses is evidently requisite for the validity of the transaction: cf. Ruth 4:9-11."
- *"Sons of Heth."* The BSB renders the Hebrew phrase as "Hittites" and once as "sons of Heth" (23:10). In the Hebrew of the chapter, this lesson's script counts "sons of Heth" eight times (23:3, 23:5, 23:7, 23:10 twice, 23:16, 23:18, 23:20) and "the Hittite" once (23:10). Genesis Rabbah 58:8 counts the phrase "ten times, corresponding to the Ten Commandments." This lesson does not resolve the difference, which may lie in what is counted.

**A deed inside a story.** Verses 17-18 change register. The Cambridge Bible: "The sentence probably represents the form of a deed of sale, such as was included in Hebrew contracts," noting "the particular mention of 'the field,' 'the cave,' 'all the trees,' 'all the border.'" Genesis Rabbah 58:8 derives from the same list the rule that a seller must write down "the field and its [specific] border markers."

**"In the land of Canaan" at both ends (23:2, 23:19).** Ramban's own reading is that the phrase marks Hebron as Canaanite country, not "the land of the Philistines," where Abraham had been living.

**How the chapter came to be.** The Cambridge Bible: "This chapter is entirely from P, as is shewn by the characteristics of language, by the frequent repetitions, by the legal and statistical minuteness." Kitchen, below, treats the account as early. This lesson does not depend on either answer.

## Positions

Three questions follow, each reported through named readers in their own words. None is decided here. Positions report what commentators conclude; the sections above report what the text says.

**1. Ephron and the four hundred shekels.** The text records Ephron's two offers (23:11, 23:15), the price, and that Abraham paid it "according to the standard of the merchants" (23:16). It makes no comment on Ephron. Readers supply three kinds of judgment.

- **Generous in words, grasping in deed.** The Babylonian Talmud (Bava Metzia 87a) records Rabbi Elazar's teaching that the righteous "say little and do much," while the wicked "say much and do not do even a little." Abraham offered a "morsel of bread" (18:5, in the Talmud's translation) and then served a calf (18:7). The second half is learned "from Ephron," who said "what is that between me and you?" and then took the silver at "current money with the merchant." The Talmud reads that phrase to mean that "he took from him only centenaria," that is, superior coins. Genesis Rabbah 58:7 applies Proverbs 28:22, "A greedy man rushes after wealth," to Ephron, "who cast a greedy eye upon the wealth of the righteous man," and reads the shortened spelling of his name in 23:16 as the "diminishment" that befell him. Rashi combines both. The missing letter indicates "that there was something missing in Ephron viz, sincerity — because he promised much but did not do even the very least." Ramban reports the same view without adopting it: "In the words of our Rabbis, however, Ephron set an exorbitant arbitrary price."
- **Courteous, and a fair seller.** Calvin rejects the charge. Reporting Jerome's argument from the missing letter, he answers that "the name of Ephron is found written in the very same manner, after that event, as before." This is true of 23:17 but not of the second spelling in 23:16 (see Key Words). On the substance he writes: "Nor ought it to be imputed to Ephron as a fault, that, being pressed, he took the lawful price for his estate; when he had been prepared liberally to give it." Matthew Henry: "Ephron honestly and fairly makes a good title to the land." The Cambridge Bible reads 23:15 as a fair statement: "Ephron has mentioned a full price; he is poor, Abraham rich: the figure could not possibly be a hindrance to the bargain. The important thing is that he, the owner, is willing to sell." Radak takes Ephron's "what is that between you and me?" at face value: "what difference does such a paltry sum make." He closes the chapter by saying that the townspeople "were quite keen for Avraham to own an ancestral plot in their midst." Ramban, in his own voice, offers a plain-sense reading of the price: "Perhaps it was Ephron's intent to say that the price of the land was so fixed in that place."
- **A formal bargaining move, whatever Ephron's heart.** Keil reads the offer of a gift as "a turn in the affair" whose design, "so far as it is seriously meant at all," is "either to obtain a present in return which will abundantly compensate for the value of the gift, or, what is still more frequently the case, to preclude any abatement in the price to be asked." He calls the price "a very considerable amount for that time." Ramban leaves Ephron's motive open: he enlarged the sale to the whole field "by way of good conduct or trickery." K. A. Kitchen (*Ancient Orient and Old Testament*, 1966) proposes a legal reason, following Manfred Lehmann's 1953 argument from the Hittite Laws (§§46-47). On that reading, whoever buys a whole property takes over its dues. Kitchen concludes: "Abraham wanted only the cave, without complications; but Ephron knew that Abraham must buy quickly (to bury Sarah), and so he insisted that Abraham should acquire the whole plot of land (and so have to pay the dues as well)." He adds that "Abraham evidently shouldered this responsibility without complaint."

For scale, the Cambridge Bible notes that David paid "fifty shekels of silver" for Araunah's threshing floor and oxen (2 Samuel 24:24); no source read here treats such comparisons as decisive.

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the rabbinic reading, Calvin answers that the name is spelled in full after the sale and that Ephron "had been prepared liberally to give it." Against Calvin's reading, the Hebrew text of 23:16 does carry the shortened spelling the rabbis describe, and Keil, without citing them, also reads Ephron's offer as designed "to preclude any abatement in the price." Against Keil's and Kitchen's readings, Gene Tucker (*JBL*, 1966) and H. Petschow (*JCS*, 1965), as Kitchen reports them, compared Genesis 23 with Neo-Babylonian "dialogue-document" contracts and concluded that the chapter "is a late tradition and cannot be used as evidence for practices of the patriarchal periods." Kitchen also reports Tucker's argument that the chapter's silence about land dues tells against the Hittite-law background. Kitchen answers that "Genesis 23 is not itself a dialogue contract-document, but is simply the report of an agreement." The text itself records Ephron's words and the payment, and each judgment of his intentions goes beyond what it states.

**A misuse to name.** Some nineteenth-century Christian commentators read here moved from Ephron to whole peoples. The plainest case is Jamieson-Fausset-Brown on 23:15: "They spoke in the common forms of Arab civility, and this indifference was mere affectation." Keil's "still customary in the East," Barnes's "Eastern understanding," and the Cambridge Bible's "the complimentary style of Orientals" make the same move in milder words. The Pulpit Commentary's note on 23:16 quotes a travel book that uses an anti-Jewish slur about coin-clipping; this lesson does not reproduce it. Each turns one man's words into a trait of living peoples. Genesis 23 gives them no ground: it names Ephron alone and judges no one. The Hittites as a body offer Abraham "the finest of our tombs" (23:6). Elsewhere Genesis records Rebekah's distress over Esau's Hittite wives, "I am weary of my life because of these Hittite women" (27:46; compare 26:34-35). That concerns two marriages, not anyone's honesty. The rabbinic sources above judge Ephron as one man, on the wording of 23:15-16, and Calvin and Henry defended him. Whatever a reader concludes about Ephron, it is a conclusion about him, not about Arabs, Jews, Hittites or "the East."

**2. Why insist on buying, and what did the purchase mean?** Abraham refuses a gift twice (23:6, 23:11) and pays in full. Readers explain why in four ways.

- **An act of faith in the promise and in resurrection.** Keil: "The fact that Abraham purchased a burying-place in strictly legal form as an hereditary possession in the promised land, was a proof of his strong faith in the promises of God and their eventual fulfilment." Calvin says Abraham bought the grave in the land promised him "for the purpose of bearing testimony to posterity, that the promise of God was not extinguished either by his own death, or by that of his family." Matthew Henry: "It was a token of his belief and expectation of the resurrection." Bengel, on Acts 7:16: "As they were pilgrims, the first land which they bought was land for a sepulchre; for they were seeking after the heavenly land, their true native country."
- **A legal claim, made politely.** Genesis Rabbah 58:6, followed by Rashi, reads *ger* and *toshab* as two legal postures, tenant and landlord. In the midrash's voice: "If you prefer, I will act as a tenant, but if not, I will act as a landlord, as this is what the Holy One blessed be He has said to me: 'To your descendants I have given this land.'" Calvin, giving two possible senses of 23:4, allows as one of them that Abraham wished "to avert the odium which might attach to him as a purchaser," as if saying, "it is enough for me to have a place where we may be buried."
- **God's kindness shown, and a trial endured.** Ramban: "This section was written to inform us of G-d's kindnesses to Abraham." Abraham was honored as a prince of God, and God "fulfilled His promise to him: And I will make thy name great." Ramban also reports a rabbinic reading that "this also was one of the trials of Abraham: he desired a place to bury Sarah but did not find it until he purchased it."
- **A founding tradition for a later people.** The Cambridge Bible explains the chapter's detail by "The veneration of the cave of Machpelah as the traditional burying-place of the patriarchs." It reads the purchase as setting "an anticipatory seal of possession upon the land of promise." On 23:20 it calls the transaction one "which for all subsequent ages symbolized to the Israelites their ancestral connexion with, and sacred rights in, the land of Canaan."

*Objections each reading faces.* Against the faith reading, the Cambridge Bible accounts for the chapter's detail by later "veneration of the cave," not by Abraham's own state of mind; the chapter itself gives Abraham no motive beyond "so that I may have a burial site" (23:9). Against the legal-claim midrash, Calvin reads the same words as disclaiming any wish for possessions. Against Ramban's reading of kindness, Ibn Ezra, as Ramban reports him, located the point in "the superiority of the land of Israel" and the fulfilment of the land promise. Against the Cambridge reading, Keil and Calvin hold that the motive was Abraham's own faith, not a later people's founding story. What the land promise itself guarantees, and to whom, is the subject of Positions 2 in the Genesis 13 lesson. It is not reopened here.

**3. Stephen's account (Acts 7:16).** Stephen says that "Their bones were carried back to Shechem and placed in the tomb that Abraham had bought from the sons of Hamor at Shechem for a price he paid in silver" (Acts 7:15-16). Genesis gives Abraham one purchase, at Machpelah near Hebron from Ephron (23:16-20), and Jacob a purchase at Shechem "from the sons of Hamor" for "a hundred pieces of silver" (33:19). Joseph's bones were buried at Shechem (Joshua 24:32); Jacob, at Machpelah (50:13). Every Greek edition shown on Bible Hub reads Ἀβραάμ in Acts 7:16. The editions differ only in whether Shechem is a place (ἐν Συχέμ, Nestle, Westcott-Hort, Tischendorf) or a person (τοῦ Συχέμ, the Byzantine, Greek Orthodox and Textus Receptus texts). Readers explain the difficulty in four ways.

- **A slip in Stephen's speech, recorded as spoken.** Meyer: "Stephen has here evidently fallen into a mistake, and asserted of Abraham what historically applied to Jacob, being led into error by the fact that something similar was recorded of Abraham." He adds that admitting "the mistake so easily possible in the hurry of the moment" would spare expositors "all strange and forced expedients."
- **An error introduced in the report or in copying.** The Cambridge Bible supposes that Stephen spoke correctly of both burials and that "into the report of what he said a confusion has been introduced by the insertion of Abraham's name for Jacob's in the abbreviated narrative." Barnes judges that "the error has arisen since; but how, is not known." The Pulpit Commentary thinks it "much more probable that this word should have been interpolated by some early transcriber."
- **Two purchases deliberately compressed into one sentence.** Bengel quotes Flacius: Stephen "compresses into one two different sepulchres, places, and purchases," naming "the true purchaser" from the first and "the true seller" from the second. Bengel concludes that "the reading Ἀβραὰμ remains intact." Gill reads the verse the same way: "the words refer to both places and purchases."
- **A purchase by Abraham at Shechem that Genesis does not record.** Ellicott argues that Jacob's claim at Shechem "presupposes a previous purchase by some ancestor of Jacob's—i.e., by Abraham." He thinks Stephen followed a Samaritan tradition and calls this "the most probable solution of the difficulty." The Pulpit Commentary reports Bishop Wordsworth as holding that Abraham "really did buy a field of Ephron in Sychem" (Wordsworth's own work was not read).

*Objections each reading faces.* Against the slip reading, Barnes finds it "not at all probable that either Stephen or Luke would have committed such an error," and the Pulpit Commentary finds it unlikely that Stephen, who "shows such thorough knowledge of the Bible history," "should have made a gross mistake in such a well-known and famous circumstance." Against the copying reading, every edition Bible Hub shows reads "Abraham." Barnes concedes that "All the ancient versions agree in reading 'Abraham,'" and Meyer calls the omission "against all external and internal critical evidence." Against the compression reading, Meyer lists "a combining of two purchases" among the "forced expedients." Against the unrecorded-purchase reading, Meyer counts it as having "assumed a purchase not mentioned at all in the O. T." Ellicott and Barnes also discuss what the difficulty implies for how Acts records Stephen's speech; that wider question is not taken up here. This lesson reports these readings and does not choose among them.

*If you are grieving.* Genesis records that Abraham went "to mourn and to weep for her" and makes no comment on his grief. The readers quoted in this lesson are describing Abraham, not setting a rule for how anyone should grieve, or for how long. If you are carrying a loss, a pastor, chaplain or grief counselor can walk with you through it. If grief brings thoughts of ending your life or harming yourself, call or text 988 (the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline in the US), or chat with a counselor at 988lifeline.org. Outside the US, contact your local emergency services. Having such thoughts is nothing to be ashamed of, and help is available now.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, as a model of the shape, not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate step.

**Passage element.** Genesis 23:3-4 and 23:13. A newly bereaved man, living where he is "a foreigner and an outsider," asks the local community for what he needs to bury his wife. When a gift is offered, he says, "I will pay you the price of the field. Accept it from me."

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: Abraham names his status, asks in public, and pays the named price before witnesses. Inference: a first audience would likely have heard a dealing no one could later dispute; the Cambridge Bible notes that witnesses were "requisite for the validity of the transaction." Why Abraham refused the gift is disputed (Positions 2).

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** In grief and in an unfamiliar place, a person can ask plainly for help and still deal openly and fairly, so that what is settled stays settled.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a grieving person, an unfamiliar community, a necessary purchase, and a public, honest settlement. Not carried forward: the land promise, the cave, the bargaining customs, and any judgment about the seller's motives.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed. Nothing here promises a fair seller or a smooth arrangement.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family. Someone who moved to a new town for work loses a parent there and must arrange a burial among people they barely know. A neighbor offers a family plot for free; the funeral home presents a contract.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: honest dealing. One faithful response is to thank the neighbor, ask for or pay a fair arrangement in writing, and have someone they trust read the contract before signing.

**Cautions.** Funeral contracts, burial rights and estates raise legal and financial questions; a qualified professional, not a study note, should advise on them. Grief can leave anyone open to pressure, and a trusted friend or pastor can come along to such meetings. The bridge does not say that accepting a gift is wrong. The passage is not a text about funeral arrangements, and the learner may find the analogy does not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. Ephron's two speeches each end "Bury your dead." Read 23:11 and 23:15 aloud. What in the wording, if anything, would you point to before deciding whether he is generous, shrewd or simply polite?
2. The Talmud's reading of Ephron rests partly on one letter and on the phrase "according to the standard of the merchants" (23:16). What kind of evidence is a spelling, and how much weight can it carry? Calvin's objection, and the Hebrew of 23:16-17, are both in this lesson.
3. Genesis tells of Abraham buying a grave in a land promised to him. Which reading in Positions 2 stays closest to what the chapter itself says, and which depends most on other texts?
4. In Acts 7:16, each of the four readings has to explain why every Greek edition reads "Abraham." Which explanation do you find carries the least weight, and why?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 22:20-23:20 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) Give the five moves of the negotiation, with a verse for each. (c) State what 23:4 says Abraham is, and what Hebrew word there first appeared at 15:13.
2. **Five-minute outline.** One minute each: Nahor's family and Sarah's death; the words new here; the negotiation and its refrains; the three disputes, held open; the connections and the boundary.
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: by giving the rabbinic reading of Ephron its own bullet, the lesson risks leaving a reader with a villain the text does not name. Before answering, check how Positions 1 states what the text does and does not record. A second candidate, running the other way: by giving Calvin and Radak a "fair seller" bullet, the lesson may be smoothing over the evidence the rabbis point to, the spelling in 23:16. Ask what each side, as quoted here, can point to in the text.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) Genesis 23 does not state Abraham's motive for paying. Every reason in Positions 2 comes from readers or from other texts. (b) Genesis 23 does not judge Ephron, and it says nothing about any people's character. The text names one seller and records his words. (c) The chapter does not say how long after the binding Sarah died. Ramban's reconstruction and the midrash in the previous lesson are readers' answers.
