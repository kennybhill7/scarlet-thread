Genesis 4:17-26 closes out the chapter that began with the murder of Abel, and it does so by running two genealogies side by side: nine generations descending from Cain (4:17-18), which widen at the seventh generation into the chapter's second extended scene -- a two-wife household, three sons each credited with a distinct cultural "first," and a short poem (4:19-24) -- and then four verses that restart the count from Adam through a different son, Seth, ending on a one-line notice about calling on the LORD's name (4:25-26). The passage is short, but several of its details are checkable before they are debatable: whether Lamech's speech is actually built as Hebrew poetry and what features make it so; what the Hebrew verb behind "began" at 4:26 is doing, and whether it carries only one sense; and what, if anything, the text itself says about how to weigh Cain's line's achievements. This lesson works through those in order, labeling each claim as a textual observation, a named tradition's or scholar's own stated reading, or this lesson's own inference -- the same three-way sort this course has used since Genesis 3.

## Context

**What happens, in order (4:17-26).** Cain has relations with his wife; she bears Enoch, and Cain builds a city and names it after his son (4:17). The text then lists Enoch's descendants in a single unbroken line -- Irad, Mehujael, Methushael, Lamech (4:18) -- with no further narrative until Lamech. Lamech marries two women, Adah and Zillah (4:19). Adah bears Jabal, "the father of those who dwell in tents and raise livestock" (4:20), and his brother Jubal, "the father of all who play the harp and flute" (4:21). Zillah bears Tubal-cain, "a forger of every implement of bronze and iron," and a daughter, Naamah, named but given no stated occupation (4:22). Lamech then speaks -- the first extended human speech recorded since 3:12-13 -- addressing his two wives by name and boasting that he has killed a man for wounding him, and that "if Cain is avenged sevenfold, then Lamech seventy-sevenfold" (4:23-24, BSB). The text pivots without transition: "Adam again had relations with his wife, and she gave birth to a son and named him Seth, saying, 'God has granted me another seed in place of Abel, since Cain killed him'" (4:25, BSB). A son is born to Seth, Enosh, and the unit closes: "At that time men began to call upon the name of the LORD" (4:26, BSB). This is a plain retelling in the text's own order; what the chapter's structure and harder verses are doing is the rest of this lesson's work.

**A name that recurs, worth flagging before it causes confusion.** Cain's son is named Enoch (4:17). Genesis 5:18-24 names a different Enoch, five generations later in Seth's line, of whom 5:24 says, "Enoch walked with God, and then he was no more, because God had taken him away" (BSB). These are two different people who happen to share an English transliteration of two different Hebrew spellings in some editions' convention, or the same consonants read as the same name in others; either way, nothing in 4:17 connects this Enoch to the man later described as walking with God. A reader tracking the chapter should keep the two straight before drawing any further conclusion from either one.

**Where Cain's city sits.** Genesis 4:16, immediately before this lesson's passage, places Cain in "the land of Nod, east of Eden" (BSB) after he leaves "the presence of the LORD." This app's place data (OpenBible.info's geocoding dataset) marks Nod "unlocated," with a single, non-confident identification in the dataset; no marker is placed on a map for it here, the same honest limit this course has already noted for Eden itself. Cain's city-building at 4:17 is the next recorded act after that departure; the text states the act and the name but does not say where the city stood beyond that general region.

**Two genealogies, one shared literary shape.** Richard S. Hess, in a short technical note in the *Bulletin for Biblical Research*, observes that Lamech sits at a structural hinge in both genealogies he appears in: in Cain's line (Gen 4:17-24) he is, by Hess's count, "sixth in the sequence" of named descendants and, by the number attached to his own song, "seventh in the line from Adam" counting Adam himself as first (Adam-Cain-Enoch-Irad-Mehujael-Methushael-Lamech). Hess notes that the names before Lamech appear in a strict, one-line (linear) genealogical formula, while the names after him -- his two wives and three sons -- appear in a different, branching (segmented) form built around Lamech as the connecting figure; Lamech, in Hess's words, "serves as the single genealogical link between the earlier genealogy of v 17 and that of Lamech's generation in vv 18-24. He alone is mentioned in both." That is a checkable, structural fact about how the text is organized, independent of anything the chapter does or does not say about Lamech's character.

**The number seven, twice.** Two sevens frame this passage on either side. At 4:15, just before this lesson's passage begins, God states a term of protection over Cain: "if anyone slays Cain, then Cain will be avenged sevenfold" (BSB). At 4:24, Lamech closes his own song with "if Cain is avenged sevenfold, then Lamech seventy-sevenfold" (BSB) -- the same number, raised by a further multiple of ten, now attached not to a divine statement of protection but to Lamech's own announced policy for anyone who wounds him. The text does not state that Lamech's claim carries the same standing as the one God made about Cain; it only records that Lamech makes it, in his own voice, as a boast to his household. Hess's article observes that this device ties the two figures together deliberately: "the poem serves to tie together Cain and Lamech, not only by the deed of vengeful murder which each commits, but also by the parallel structure of the lines themselves. From the perspective of the poetic parallelism, Lamech is a new Cain." That reading -- "a new Cain" -- is Hess's own stated conclusion about the poem's design, reported here as his, not independently adjudicated by this lesson.

**Is Lamech's speech actually Hebrew poetry, and how can a reader check?** Several independent lines of evidence say yes, and each is checkable on its own terms rather than taken on authority. First, parallelism: the UBS *Handbook on Genesis* (Reyburn and Fry, 1997) describes 4:23-24 as containing "balanced beats and parallelism of lines," with each pair built so that "the second line always goes beyond the first line by adding something to it" -- an intensifying, not merely repetitive, parallelism. Second, Hess reports the same structure from a different angle, citing Umberto Cassuto, D. K. Stuart, and especially S. Gevirtz's dedicated 1963 study of the poem: "The names of Adah and Zillah parallel the reference to them as wives of Lamech in the first couplet. The man corresponds to the 'boy' in the second couplet. Both designations indicate the same individual" -- that is, the Hebrew itself signals that "a man" and "a young man" (4:23, BSB) are the same person described twice, a feature of synonymous parallelism a reader can verify by comparing the two half-lines directly, not an inference drawn from outside the verse. Third, the numbers: the Handbook notes "the numerical intensification is seen in the increase from seven times in the first line to seventy-seven times in the second" -- a checkable escalation, matching the 4:15-to-4:24 pattern already noted above. None of this settles what the poem means; it is reported here only to establish, on checkable grounds, that it is built as poetry.

**The verb behind "began" (4:26) -- a genuine lexical ambiguity, not just a translation choice.** The Hebrew form behind "began" at 4:26 is a Hoph'al of the root חלל (*chalal*). Brown-Driver-Briggs's full entry (via Bible Hub) catalogues this root's range across roughly 143 Old Testament occurrences under three separate headwords: a first verb glossed "bore, pierce"; a second, denominative verb glossed "play the pipe"; and a third verb glossed "pollute, defile, profane," whose Hiph'il BDB glosses "begin (literally untie, loosen, open...)" -- BDB's own entry derives both the "begin" sense and the "profane" sense of this third verb from that same underlying "untie, loosen, open" idea, rather than treating them as two unrelated words that happen to share a spelling. At Genesis 4:26 itself, BDB's entry glosses the exact form found here, אָז הוּחַל, as "then it was begun (= men began)" -- the "begin" sense, specifically. That is the lexicon's own call at this verse. But the same root, in its more common Pi'el and Niph'al stems, is the Hebrew Bible's standard verb for profaning something holy -- including, repeatedly, "the name" of God (for example Leviticus 18:21; 19:12; 20:3; 22:32; Ezekiel 20:39; 36:20-23). That second sense is not a stretch invented to make 4:26 say something unexpected; it is this same root's dominant use everywhere else it appears with "the name" as its object. The lexical fact, stated carefully: the form at 4:26 is most directly glossed "began," and a different, well-attested sense of the identical root is "profane," specifically of a name. Which sense a reader takes at 4:26 is the subject of real, named disagreement -- see Positions, below.

**"Call upon the name of the LORD" -- a phrase that recurs later, differently.** The specific collocation at 4:26 -- calling on the LORD's name -- recurs nearly word for word four more times in Genesis: Abram at Bethel, "There he built an altar to the LORD, and he called on the name of the LORD" (12:8, BSB); Abram again at the same altar (13:4); Abraham at Beersheba, "he called upon the name of the LORD, the Eternal God" (21:33, BSB); and Isaac at Beersheba, "called on the name of the LORD" (26:25, BSB). Ephraim A. Landau, in a 2026 *Jewish Bible Quarterly* article treating this exact phrase across the Hebrew Bible, groups all five of these occurrences together and notes a real difference among them: the four patriarchal instances each follow a specific act -- building an altar, in three of the four cases -- while 4:26 names the practice generally, with no altar, offering, or other act attached to it in the verse itself. Landau's own conclusion is deliberately unresolved: he finds "the lack of evidence in all five of these verses allows for various interpretations involving either 'invoking' Him or 'worshipping' Him," and identifies three live possibilities across the set (invoking divine aid, public worship, or proclaiming God to others) without judging which applies at any one of the five verses. That caution is reported here as his, not overridden by this lesson.

## Literary Design

**A shift in genealogical form at the midpoint.** As Context noted, Hess documents a formal change in how the genealogy is told once it reaches Lamech: a strict linear list (one name per generation) gives way to a segmented, branching one (one man, two wives, four named children). This is a structural fact about the text's own form, checkable by comparing how 4:18 is written against how 4:19-22 is written; what it signals about the passage's larger design is a separate, more interpretive question this section does not resolve on its own.

**Two genealogies and their shared number.** Genesis 5 -- the next passage in the book, not part of this lesson -- lists ten named generations from Adam through Noah, continuing Seth's line. Hess's article (read independently for this lesson) notes that this second genealogy shows the identical structural feature already seen in Cain's: Lamech's own position, seventh from Adam in Cain's line, is mirrored by Enoch's position, seventh from Adam in Seth's line (Adam-Seth-Enosh-Kenan-Mahalalel-Jared-Enoch), a correspondence Hess attributes in turn to two earlier commentators, B. Jacob and Cassuto. The two seventh-generation figures could hardly be narrated more differently -- one is a boastful killer; of the other it is said, "Enoch walked with God, and then he was no more, because God had taken him away" (Gen 5:24, BSB) -- but that full contrast belongs to Genesis 5, outside this lesson's passage; it is flagged here only as a structural fact worth carrying forward, not developed.

**What the chapter does and does not say about Cain's line's achievements.** Genesis 4:20-22 names three "firsts" -- livestock-based tent-dwelling, musical instruments, and metalworking -- without attaching any stated praise or blame to any of them. That absence of an explicit verdict is itself a checkable textual fact: the verses are constructed exactly like the genealogical notices around them ("X was the father of Y"), with no adjective, no divine response, and no narrator's comment of the kind 4:1-16 supplies for Cain's own act ("sin is crouching at your door," 4:7, BSB). Whether that silence should be read as neutral reporting, as an implicit commendation of genuinely good gifts, or as damning by association with the violent figure who speaks immediately afterward, is a real, named disagreement -- see Positions, below.

## Positions

**What does "began to call upon the name of the LORD" (4:26) mean?** The lexical ambiguity documented in Context produces two real, named readings, not a settled consensus with one outlying translation choice.

The majority reading among the classic Protestant commentators collected by Bible Hub takes "began" at face value, as the start (or public resumption) of invoking God by name. Matthew Poole reads it as the start of "a more public and solemn manner" of worship. Matthew Henry's *Concise Commentary* describes this point in the narrative as when "God gave Adam and Eve to see the revival of religion in their family," adding, "the worshippers of God began to do more in religion; some, by an open profession of true religion, protested against the wickedness of the world around." Albert Barnes glosses the clause "then was it begun to call upon the name of the Lord" and describes it as "the solemn invocation of God by his proper name in audible and social prayer and praise." Ellicott's commentary reads it as a shift in how the divine name itself was used and regarded: "the notion of Divinity began now to be attached to this name, and even in their worship men called upon God as Jehovah." Benson's commentary allows for degree rather than a hard beginning -- "perhaps not more than had been done at first, but more than had been done since the defection of Cain" -- while still landing on an intensification of true worship. A 2024 survey article (wholereason.com) additionally reports this same general reading in John Calvin, Charles Hodge, and the twentieth-century Genesis commentator Umberto Cassuto, each treating the verse as marking organized, public invocation of God; that report is secondhand here and has not been independently checked against those three authors' own texts.

A second, older reading, attested in classical Jewish sources and resting on the same root's "profane" sense, reads the verse in the opposite moral direction. Rashi comments directly: "The word הוחל must be connected in meaning with חולין 'profane matters,' viz, calling the names of men and the names of idols after the name of the Holy One, blessed be He -- making them the objects of idolatrous worship and calling them Deities," citing Genesis Rabbah 23:7 for the reading. Targum Onkelos's own rendering of the clause is itself ambiguous in the form this lesson consulted, bracketing two glosses alongside its base translation: "Then men began to call [idols] [men stopped praying] by the name Adonoy" -- preserving, within one translation, both a "began misusing the name" sense and a "stopped invoking the name" sense, rather than committing to either. On this family of readings, 4:26 does not describe revival; it describes the point at which God's name started to be attached to things that were not God. The same 2024 survey cited above reports that this reading, or at least the acknowledgment that it exists in the history of interpretation, also appears in modern evangelical commentators including John H. Sailhamer, Kenneth A. Mathews, and Allen P. Ross; again, that attribution is reported secondhand here and not independently verified against those commentators' own books.

These two readings do not simply disagree about a translation nuance; they disagree about whether 4:26 is the chapter's one clearly hopeful note or a continuation of its decline. Both readings start from the same lexical fact established in Context -- that the verb's root legitimately carries both senses, and that BDB's own default gloss at this specific verse is "begin." This lesson reports both positions and resolves neither.

**How should Cain's line's cultural achievements (4:20-22) be read?** Maarten J. Paul's 1996 *Tyndale Bulletin* survey of the passage's interpretive history (consulted here through the article's own published summary, not its full text -- see Sources and Limits) states as its own finding that evaluations of Jabal, Jubal, and Tubal-cain's "firsts" have historically tracked the interpreter's own social and cultural position, that a long stretch of earlier interpretation read them negatively, and that Calvin was the first to establish a different, more positive outlook -- a shift Paul reports as then carried into a live modern debate between the commentators J. Gabriel and Claus Westermann.

One named position reads Cain's city-building specifically, not the inventions, as the founding act of a particular kind of human community. Augustine, in Book XV of *The City of God*, states it directly: "it is recorded of Cain that he built a city, but Abel, being a sojourner, built none," and develops this into his larger "two cities" framework: earlier in the same chapter he describes the pattern that "the citizen of this world was the first-born, and after him the stranger in this world, the citizen of the city of God" -- so that Cain, the city-builder, stands for what Augustine elsewhere in the chapter calls the earthly city, and Abel, who built nothing, for the City of God. This is Augustine's own theological reading of the narrative detail, built from the plain fact that the text records a city built by Cain and no city built by Abel; it goes beyond what 4:17 states on its own.

A second position, associated in Paul's survey with Westermann's commentary approach, reads 4:17-24 with a more reserved eye: Genesis 1-11 as a whole, on this view, includes material that simply catalogues the origins of various aspects of human culture without assigning the kind of value judgment a reader may be tempted to supply from the surrounding narrative's mood. This lesson has not independently verified Westermann's own wording on this specific passage against his commentary text; the characterization here is carried from secondary reporting (see Sources and Limits) rather than a direct quotation.

This lesson reports both readings. Nothing in 4:20-22 itself states which is correct; the two positions differ in how much interpretive weight they are willing to place on the surrounding narrative (the murder in 4:1-16, Lamech's boast immediately following) versus the bare content of the three verses naming the inventions.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key: the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step, and a different passage element, situation, or response would be equally legitimate.

**Passage element.** Genesis 4:23-24. Lamech turns a personal grievance -- being wounded by a man -- into an announced, standing policy of escalated retaliation, stated not privately but publicly, to his own wives: "if Cain is avenged sevenfold, then Lamech seventy-sevenfold" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: Lamech does not simply retaliate once; he composes a poem -- a memorable, repeatable form -- and addresses it to the people closest to him, his household, rather than to the man he struck. Inference: for a first hearer of the genealogy, the poem functions as a stated household policy, not a private act; what is being modeled and handed down to Lamech's own family is the expectation that any future offense against him will be met with an unlimited, self-declared multiple of the original harm.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Announcing a policy of disproportionate retaliation to the people who will hear it as a standard -- rather than simply reacting once, in private -- normalizes escalation as the expected response to any future offense, for everyone who hears the announcement.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a real grievance, a decision to make the response larger than the offense, and a public announcement of that decision to people who will take it as the new standard. Not carried forward: polygamy, the specific violence described, Lamech's household, and anything specific to the ancient setting. The bridge holds only where a modern case actually matches this shape -- a real grievance answered by an announced, escalated policy, publicly stated to people who will internalize it as normal.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy (a pattern to notice and resist in oneself), not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed; nothing in 4:23-24 is treated here as a promise about what will happen to anyone who imitates Lamech.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. A department head who was publicly undermined by a colleague tells her own team, in a meeting, that she will "make sure" anyone who crosses her "regrets it" far more than whatever they did to her -- establishing, out loud, an expectation of disproportionate response among the people who report to her.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: restraint and proportionality (the opposite pattern from the one modeled in the text, not an imitation of it). A faithful response addresses the real grievance through a proportionate, appropriate channel (direct conversation, a manager, an HR process) and does not announce an escalated personal policy to subordinates who will otherwise learn, from watching, that disproportionate retaliation is the department's norm.

**Cautions.** This example is not about suppressing legitimate grievances or declining to hold anyone accountable; proportionate consequences through a real process are not what Lamech's boast describes. It does not apply to someone genuinely defending themselves from ongoing harm, and it is not a tool for telling a person who has been wronged that naming the wrong is itself the problem. Anyone in a situation involving real danger needs real protection -- a qualified person or authority, not a study note.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. BDB glosses the verb at 4:26 "began," while the same root's dominant sense elsewhere is "profane" -- specifically of a name. What, if anything, in the immediate context of 4:25-26 itself (not later tradition, not the surrounding chapters) would help a reader decide between the two senses at this specific verse?
2. Genesis 4:20-22 states the three "firsts" with no explicit praise or blame; Lamech, from the same genealogical line, speaks the chapter's most violent words two verses later. Does the text's silence about the achievements function as neutral reporting, or does their placement right before Lamech's boast do some of the evaluative work the verses themselves do not state outright? What kind of evidence would actually distinguish those two possibilities?
3. The name Enoch appears in both genealogies (Cain's son at 4:17; Seth's descendant five generations later in Genesis 5), and so does the name Lamech (Cain's descendant here; Noah's father in Genesis 5:28-31). What should a reader make of two names recurring across both lines -- coincidence, a deliberate authorial echo, or something else -- and what would be needed to tell which?
4. The phrase "called on the name of the LORD" recurs at 4:26 and then differently -- each time following a specific act -- at 12:8, 13:4, 21:33, and 26:25. If a reader wanted to argue that 4:26 describes the same kind of act as Abram's altar-building, what would the text need to say that it does not currently say?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate what happens in Genesis 4:17-26 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) State, in the text's own words, what Lamech's song says about Cain's and Lamech's respective "avenging," before saying what any tradition concludes from the fact that the numbers increase. (c) Name one place elsewhere in Scripture where this passage's language (a word, a number, a phrase) visibly reappears, and say what kind of connection it is: a verbal echo, a motif, or something else.

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** Outline how you would teach this passage in five minutes, with at least one point in each segment you could cite to a source or a verse. A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the plain sequence of the two genealogies and the shift from a linear to a segmented form at Lamech (see Context); (1:00-2:00) Lamech's song as checkable Hebrew poetry -- the parallelism, the "man"/"boy" identity, the 7-to-77 escalation -- read first on its own terms (see Context); (2:00-3:30) the two named disputes, the meaning of "began" at 4:26 and how to weigh Cain's line's achievements, reported as real disagreements rather than resolved (see Positions); (3:30-4:30) where this passage's language travels later in Scripture, naming the evidence label of each example, not just listing it (see the table in prompt 5); (4:30-5:00) one open question, stated on purpose as the last thing taught (prompt 1 or 2, above).

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first; the point of this prompt is that you do the forming, not that you pick from a list. If you are stuck, here are two worked candidates running in opposite directions. First: the "profanation" reading of 4:26 (Rashi, Targum Onkelos) is a retrojection from later Jewish anxiety about idolatry and the misuse of the divine name onto a verse whose own Hebrew form BDB glosses, at this exact occurrence, as "begin" -- the lexicon's own call at 4:26 itself favors the plain reading, and the "profane" sense has to be imported from how the root is used elsewhere, not from this verse's own grammar. Before you answer it, note that BDB's gloss is a lexicographer's judgment, not a grammatical proof, and that the same Hoph'al stem is not independently attested elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible with unambiguous "profane" force the way the Pi'el and Niph'al are -- so the "plain reading favored by the lexicon" claim rests partly on there being no closer grammatical parallel to compare against, not on a settled rule. Second, running the other direction: reading 4:20-22's silence about Cain's line's achievements as carrying any evaluative force at all -- positive (Westermann's more reserved reading, as reported) or negative (Augustine's two-cities reading) -- imports a judgment the text does not state, since the same unadorned genealogical form ("X was the father of Y") is used throughout Genesis 5 for Seth's line without anyone reading moral content into it there. Before you answer it, note that Augustine's own argument does not rest on the inventions themselves but specifically on the one stated act -- Cain built a city, Abel built none -- which is a textual asymmetry between the two genealogies' openings, not merely an argument from silence about the three later inventions.

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Genesis 4:20-22 does not state, in its own words, whether raising livestock, making music, or forging metal tools is good, bad, or morally neutral; no verb of blessing, cursing, or evaluation is attached to any of the three notices, unlike 4:11-12's explicit curse on Cain himself. (b) Genesis 4:26 does not specify, in its own words, what act or acts "calling upon the name of the LORD" refers to; unlike the later patriarchal instances of the same phrase (12:8; 13:4; 21:33; 26:25), which each follow a stated act such as building an altar, 4:26 names the practice with no accompanying act in the verse itself.

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection from this passage and defend it: name its type (for example quotation, explicit reference, allusion, motif), give its evidence label (explicit, strong, plausible, or devotional), and point to the words in both texts that support that grade. The reviewed connections attached to this lesson sit at different tiers, which is the point of the exercise:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matthew 18:21-22 | allusion | strong | The Greek of Matthew's "seventy-seven times" matches the Septuagint's rendering of Lamech's number at Genesis 4:24 word for word, though no quotation formula names Genesis |
| Genesis 12:8 | motif | strong | The identical Hebrew phrase "called on the name of the LORD" recurs, though Genesis 4:26 lacks the stated act (altar-building) that accompanies every later occurrence |

Choose one row and defend it in your own words: name its type, its evidence label, and point to the words in both texts that support that grade. Or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` instead: Luke 3:38 (BSB) reads "the son of Enosh, the son of Seth, the son of Adam, the son of God" -- Luke lists the same father-son relationship Genesis 4:25-26 records (Seth, then his son Enosh), simply in the reverse direction, descending from Jesus back to Adam -- a real, checkable textual fact -- but say what further evidence would be needed to argue that this is a meaningful connection to *this specific passage's* content (the naming of Seth and Enosh, and 4:26's notice about calling on the LORD) rather than simply what is true of every other name shared between a genealogy in Genesis and a genealogy in Luke 3.

- **The footnoted scholars in the two Redemptive History and Theology pages used for the Matthew 18 connection** (Hamilton, Wenham, Walton, Waltke, and Fredricks on the Genesis side; France, Hagner, and Keener on the Matthew side) were not independently read; only Redemptive History and Theology's own reporting of them was consulted.
- **Luke 3:36-38 and Genesis 5 were both considered and not authored as formal connections.** Luke's genealogy names Seth and Enosh in the same father-son relationship Genesis records, just listed in reverse (descending from Jesus to Adam), which is a checkable fact, but this lesson did not locate a scholarly treatment tying that specifically to 4:25-26's content (as opposed to Luke simply reproducing an inherited genealogy); Genesis 5 is the next passage in the same book and was judged better left as a forward-pointer (see Literary Design and Questions to Carry) than a formal connection to itself.
- **Naamah (4:22) is named with no stated role**, unlike her three half-brothers. This lesson located no named scholarly treatment of this specific asymmetry that could be verified against its own primary source within this research pass; it is flagged here as a genuine open textual observation, not resolved.
- **Targum Onkelos's rendering of 4:26 is quoted from a modern bracketed translation** (Metsudah Chumash, 2009, via Sefaria) that itself preserves two different glosses inside one clause; this lesson reports that bracketed ambiguity rather than resolving it into a single English sentence, and a reader checking the underlying Aramaic directly may find the translator's two bracketed options reflect two different manuscript or interpretive traditions this lesson did not independently trace.
- **Hess (1991) was read in full** and is the most directly verified source in this lesson; every claim attributed to it above was checked against the complete article text, not a summary.
