Genesis 19:30-38 is nine verses long, and it is one of the hardest passages in Genesis to read. After Sodom is destroyed, Lot leaves Zoar for a cave in the mountains with his two daughters. The older daughter says to the younger, "Our father is old, and there is no man in the land to sleep with us, as is the custom over all the earth" (19:31). On two nights they get their father drunk with wine and each sleeps with him; twice the narrator says "he was not aware when she lay down or when she got up" (19:33, 35). Their sons are named as the fathers of the Moabites and the Ammonites "of today" (19:37-38). The narrator reports the daughters' reasoning and gives no verdict on anyone, and God is not named anywhere in the nine verses. This is an account of incest in which the father is made drunk. The lesson does not soften it, and it does not use it to excuse or explain abuse in anyone's life today. It sets out what the text says and leaves unsaid, reports how named readers have judged the daughters and Lot and whether the story was told against Moab and Ammon, and follows the line from Moab to Ruth, to David, and to Matthew 1:5. ## Context

**What happens, in order (19:30-38).** "Lot and his two daughters left Zoar and settled in the mountains—for he was afraid to stay in Zoar—where they lived in a cave" (19:30). The older daughter proposes: "Come, let us get our father drunk with wine so we can sleep with him and preserve his line" (19:32). The two nights follow in nearly the same words (19:33-35), and "Thus both of Lot's daughters became pregnant by their father" (19:36). The sons are named (19:37-38).

**Where this unit sits.** Stage 5 of this app's eleven-stage lens ("Israel: The Twelve Tribes," `chapters: ["1.12"]`). The passage follows directly on 19:29, "He remembered Abraham, and He brought Lot out of the catastrophe". The destruction of Sodom, the long dispute over its sin, and Lot's offer of these same daughters to the crowd (19:8) belong to this curriculum's previous lesson; this one refers back to 19:8 only where readers of 19:30-38 do.

**What the text states, and what it leaves unstated.**

- *The motive is given in the daughters' own words, not the narrator's.* The older daughter gives two reasons: "Our father is old" and "there is no man in the land" (19:31). Both speeches end with the same aim, to "preserve his line" (19:32) and "preserve our father's line" (19:34). Whether "no man in the land" means the whole earth or only their region is disputed (Position 1). The text itself records that Zoar was spared, "I will grant this request as well, and will not demolish the town you indicate" (19:21), and that Lot left it out of fear (19:30).
- *Lot's state is stated twice and not explained.* The clause "he was not aware when she lay down or when she got up" occurs only at 19:33 and 19:35 in the BSB (script count). In the Hebrew of 19:33 a scribal dot stands over the word for "when she got up." Genesis Rabbah 51:8 and the Talmud (Nazir 23a; Horayot 10b) read the dot as a sign of partial knowledge; in Nazir's words, "that when she lay down he did not know; however, when she arose he knew." Keil and Delitzsch, after reporting Jerome's account of a rabbinic reading of the dot, conclude that Lot, "though not entirely unconscious," acted "without clearly knowing what he was doing." The dot is a feature of the Hebrew text; what it means is interpretation.
- *No verdict, and no mention of God.* Unlike 19:13 or 19:29, these nine verses contain no word of God and no narrator's judgment (checked by script: neither "God" nor "LORD" occurs in 19:30-38). Readers who condemn, excuse, or simply record the act are all filling that silence (Position 1).
- *Nobody is named but Lot and the sons.* The daughters are never named, here or elsewhere. After 19:36, Lot's name does not occur again in Genesis (script count). Outside Genesis he is named only in Deuteronomy 2:9 and 2:19 ("the descendants of Lot"), Psalm 83:8 ("the sons of Lot"), Luke 17:28-32, and 2 Peter 2:7, which calls him "a righteous man." Matthew Henry draws a moral from the silence; Benson infers that Lot "lived to repent of his sin, otherwise St. Peter would not have spoken so honourably of him" (Benson adds that there is no proof the daughters repented). Both are inferences from what the text does not say.
- *The names.* The Cambridge Bible reads "Moab" as a play on the Hebrew for "from a father," calling it "an instance of derivation by folk-etymology"; Keil notes that the Greek Septuagint adds a clause spelling it out, and the Pulpit Commentary records a different derivation (Rosenmüller). "Ben-ammi" is glossed "son of my people" or "son of my father's kin" (Cambridge). The name "Ben-ammi" occurs only at Genesis 19:38 in the BSB. The phrase "of today" in 19:37-38 marks the narrator's own day; Barnes and the Pulpit Commentary note the phrase can cover anything from a few years to centuries.

**Where Moab and Ammon go next.** A script scan of the BSB finds "Moab" as a word (Moab, Moabite(s), Moabitess) in 174 verses and "Ammon" as a word (Ammon, Ammonite(s), Ammonitess) in 118, in both cases only in the Old Testament; neither word occurs in the New Testament. The major places:

- *Kinship and land.* Deuteronomy 2:9 and 2:19 forbid Israel to attack either people, because "I have given it to the descendants of Lot as their possession" (2:19).
- *Hostility and exclusion.* Moab's king hires Balaam (Numbers 22:4-6), and at Shittim "the men began to indulge in sexual immorality with the daughters of Moab" (Numbers 25:1). Deuteronomy 23:3 rules that "No Ammonite or Moabite or any of their descendants may enter the assembly of the LORD, even to the tenth generation," giving as its reason that "they did not meet you with food and water" and "hired Balaam" (23:4). Nehemiah 13:1-3 reads this law aloud and "excluded from Israel all of foreign descent" (13:3).
- *Ruth.* Elimelech's sons "took Moabite women as their wives, one named Orpah and the other named Ruth" (Ruth 1:4); "Ruth the Moabitess" is her title in five verses, and "the Moabitess" alone in a sixth (2:6) (script count); her son Obed "became the father of Jesse, the father of David" (4:17). David later asks "the king of Moab" to shelter his parents (1 Samuel 22:3).
- *An Ammonite queen mother.* Solomon marries women "of Moab, Ammon" (1 Kings 11:1), and his son Rehoboam's mother is "Naamah the Ammonite" (1 Kings 14:21, 31; 2 Chronicles 12:13). Matthew 1:7 names Rehoboam in Jesus' line but not his mother.
- *Judgment and restoration.* Zephaniah turns the chapter's own geography on Lot's descendants: "surely Moab will be like Sodom and the Ammonites like Gomorrah" (Zephaniah 2:9). Jeremiah ends his oracles against both with a promise: "Yet in the latter days I will restore Moab from captivity" (Jeremiah 48:47), and "Yet afterward I will restore the Ammonites from captivity" (49:6).

## Literary Design

**Two drunk fathers.** By script count, the only verses in Genesis that use "drunk" or "drunkenness" are 9:21 and 9:24 (Noah) and 19:32-35 (Lot). Both scenes follow a judgment, involve a father, wine and his children, and each closes on a neighbor of later Israel (a curse on Canaan, 9:25-27; the birth of Moab and Ammon, 19:37-38). The connection below grades the parallel and names the two readers who draw it.

**Doubled scenes.** The two nights are told in nearly the same words (19:33, 35), and the daughters' aim is said twice (19:32, 34). Rashi, citing Numbers Rabbah, notices one difference: of the older daughter the text says she "slept with her father" (19:33), of the younger only "slept with him" (19:35), and he reads the difference as Scripture sparing the younger, who "did not originate" the plan. The difference is checkable in the BSB; the reason Rashi gives is his reading.

**An echo of 19:8.** At Sodom, Lot offered "two daughters who have never slept with a man" (19:8); here the same daughters act toward him. Wil Gafney and Ilan Kutz (Position 1) and Chizkuni, to the daughters' discredit (Position 4), read the two scenes together. Whether the narrator intended the reversal the text does not say.

## Positions

Four questions follow. Each is reported through named readers in their own words, and the order is not a ranking. Positions report what readers conclude; the Context section reports what the text states.

**1. How does the account regard the daughters and Lot?** The text reports the daughters' words and acts and Lot's state and passes no verdict. Readers fall into four broad groups.

- **Both are at fault.** Matthew Henry: "No excuse can be made for the daughters, nor for Lot." Calvin (CCEL's English rendering; see Sources and Limits) judges that the daughters "were not so much moved through sensual lusts, as through a foolish desire for the procreation of their family," rejects the view that they thought the whole world destroyed ("as many explainers drivel"), and concludes that "in no wise they are to be excused," since getting their father drunk shows "they guess, that he cannot be made willing." Of Lot he writes: "Though Lot not sinned knowingly, yet, because his drunkenness was the cause of his sin, his guilt is diminished, but not annulled." Keil and Delitzsch call the act an "accursed crime" impelled "by the desire to preserve their family," and add: "If it was not lust, therefore, which impelled them to this shameful deed, their conduct was worthy of Sodom." Matthew Poole calls it "a great sin, not only in them, but also in Lot himself, not to be excused by ignorance of the virtue of wine."
- **The daughters' intent is credited and Lot is faulted.** The Talmud (Nazir 23a, with a parallel at Horayot 10b) contrasts the daughters, "who intended to engage in sexual intercourse with him for the sake of a mitzva, as they thought that the entire world was destroyed and wished to preserve the human race," with Lot, who "intended to act for the sake of a transgression." When it asks whether Lot, "asleep at the time," can be a sinner, it answers from the scribal dot: he knew when the older daughter arose, so "on the other, following, night, he should not have drunk wine again." Genesis Rabbah 51:9 reports two sages saying "We do not know whether Lot lusted after his daughters or his daughters lusted after him," and concluding from Proverbs 18:1 "that Lot lusted after his daughters, but his daughters did not lust after him." Ramban suggests the daughters hoped "that G-d should have mercy" and the world be rebuilt, and that "they were modest and did not want to tell their father to marry them"; he then allows that "the matter was extremely repulsive in the eyes of the people of those generations and was never done," and notes that the Rabbis "discredit Lot very much." Among Christian readers, Gill reports Bishop Patrick's view that the daughters acted from "an eager desire after the Messiah," and answers it in the same breath: "let the intention be ever so good, it will, not justify an action so monstrously vile." The Jewish sources are not of one voice, however: in Genesis Rabbah 51:10 Rabbi Huna says Moab's conception was "not for the sake of licentiousness, but rather, for the sake of Heaven," and Rabbi Simon says the opposite.
- **The act is recorded, not commended, and read for its meaning.** Augustine (*Reply to Faustus* 22.42-45) answers a critic who used the story against the Old Testament: "In the story of Lot's daughters the action is related, not commended," and "It is God's writing, but not His doing; He does not propose these things for our imitation, but holds them up for our warning." He weighs motive ("the natural desire for offspring in order to preserve the race"), states that "It would have been better for them never to have been mothers, than to have become mothers by their own father," and concludes of Lot: "His guilt therefore is not that of incest, but of drunkenness." He asks whether the daughters had used some art "so that in his ignorance he might sin, or rather be sinned against," and sets the idea aside: "it is not likely that the Scripture would have omitted all notice of this." Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* IV.31), reporting an earlier presbyter, goes further: with actions "on which the Scriptures pass no censure," readers "ought not to become the accusers" but "should search for a type." He holds that Lot acted without "any knowledge or thought of anything of the kind," that the daughters "are to be held excusable, since they supposed that they only, along with their father, were left," and that the two daughters figure "the two churches."
- **The story reverses how incest happens.** Two modern writers read the narrative itself as placing on daughters what fathers do. Ilan Kutz, a psychiatrist writing in the *BMJ* (2005), asks: "How is it that father-daughter incest is universally perpetrated by the father, while in Genesis the roles are reversed?" He argues from Lot's offer at 19:8, from the inhabited town of Zoar and Abraham's nearby tents (against "no man in the land"), and from the implausibility of two pregnancies from two nights, that Lot is the more likely abuser, and that the narrators chose "to shift the blame on to the women" to protect the family name. Wil Gafney, writing at TheTorah.com (2021), holds that "the crafting of the story as a case of daughters as the initiators of incest is problematic, as, in the real world, the reverse would be the case." Building on Genesis Rabbah 51:9, she reads Lot's move from Zoar to the cave as a wish to get his daughters alone. She draws on Ilona Rashkow's list of features the story shares with reports of incest, including "Blame placement on younger persons in the relationship," and then offers a "womanist midrash," an openly imaginative retelling in which Lot plans the whole affair. She cites Genesis Rabbah 51:9 (above) as an ancient precedent for suspecting Lot.

*Objections each reading faces.* The first reading has to account for the narrator's silence: Augustine's point that the act is "related, not commended" cuts against any claim that the text itself condemns, and Kutz argues the narrators shifted the blame on to the women; on his reading, faulting the daughters equally repeats that shift. The second rests heavily on the scribal dot and on midrash; Keil accepts partial awareness but not the Talmud's inference that Lot knew and chose to drink again, and Rabbi Simon's dissent shows the credit given the daughters was disputed among the rabbis themselves. The third has to face what Calvin and Poole press, that the drunkenness was itself a choice, and the Pulpit Commentary rejects Marcus Kalisch's similar inference that the narrative "exempts him from all serious reproach." The fourth reads against the text's twice-stated "he was not aware," which Irenaeus and Augustine take at face value; Kutz's case rests partly on paintings and clinical patterns as well as on 19:8 and Zoar, and Gafney presents her retelling as midrash, not as what the text says.

**2. Was the account told against Moab and Ammon?** Genesis names the sons as ancestors of two of Israel's neighbors, and readers disagree about why it tells their origin this way.

- **Yes, or possibly.** The Pulpit Commentary reports that De Wette regarded the story as "the invention of later Jewish hatred to the Moabites and Ammonites," a view it says Rosenmüller thought "not improbable." The Cambridge Bible allows that "the story, as we have it in Gen., reflects the feelings of bitterness and hatred which were felt among the Israelites towards their neighbours," pointing to Deuteronomy 23:3 and Nehemiah 13:1-2. Gafney is direct: "On its face, the text is a slur, a hyperbolic parable to explain that the Ammonites and Moabites were literally bad from the seed." Kutz holds that the narrators "cast an ancient blot of shame on their origins." Keil reports that Knobel traced Deuteronomy 23's exclusion to the incestuous origin.
- **No.** Keil and Delitzsch: "This account was neither the invention of national hatred to the Moabites and Ammonites, nor was it placed here as a brand upon those tribes," arguing from Deuteronomy 2:9 and 2:19, and on Deuteronomy 23:3 that the exclusion was "not, however, because their forefathers were begotten in incest... but on account of the hostility they had manifested." Ellicott, noting that "Several modern commentators see in this recital a mark of Jewish hatred," replies that in Deuteronomy 2 "the relationship of these two nations to Israel is used as a ground for kindly feelings," and points to Ruth and to David's friendship with the king of Moab. The Pulpit Commentary reports Hävernick's objection: why would the same writer show Abraham full of "compassionate tenderness towards the cities of the plain"?
- **Possibly an older story told to their honor.** The Cambridge Bible also finds "plausibility in this theory of Gunkel's": that the story may preserve a Moabite and Ammonite tradition of "some stupendous catastrophe" after which only Lot and his daughters survived, so that it reflected "not ignominy, but honour, upon those who were the means of preserving the whole human race." It adds at once: "Our ignorance of Moabite early traditions forbids us to go further than to admit the possibility." Gunkel's own work was not read for this lesson.

*Objections each reading faces.* The polemic reading has to account for Deuteronomy 2's protection of Lot's descendants and for Ruth, as Keil and Ellicott argue. The "no" reading has to account for the later hostility the Cambridge Bible cites (Deuteronomy 23:3; Nehemiah 13:1-2), which the Cambridge Bible offers as a possible setting for the story. The honor-tradition reading rests, by the Cambridge Bible's own admission, on traditions no one has.

*Readings that disparage the peoples.* Some classic readers turned the story into a verdict on the peoples. The Geneva Bible's note on 19:37 says that as they "were born in most horrible incest, so were they and their posterity vile and wicked"; Poole calls the Moabites infamous and marked by God's displeasure; Chizkuni extends the charge of seduction to later women he counts as their descendants. Gill, citing Strabo, says incest was "usual among" the Arabs of his sources, offering it as what the daughters "might think lawful." Set beside Barnes's and Ellicott's purely historical note that both peoples later "merged" into the Arabs, that claim turns a story about ancient ancestors into a remark about a living people. These readings are reported as a documented feature of how the passage has been used, not as descriptions of anyone. Checkable against the text: Genesis 19:37-38 names two ancient peoples "of today," meaning the narrator's day; it says nothing about the character of their descendants and names no later people. The rest of the Bible protects their land (Deuteronomy 2), excludes them from the assembly for named acts of hostility, not for their origin (Deuteronomy 23:4), places a Moabite woman in David's line (Ruth 4:17) and an Ammonite woman as mother of a king of Judah (1 Kings 14:21), and promises both peoples restoration (Jeremiah 48:47; 49:6).

**3. Deuteronomy 23:3 and Ruth the Moabitess.** If "No Ammonite or Moabite or any of their descendants may enter the assembly of the LORD" (Deuteronomy 23:3), how is a Moabite woman David's great-grandmother? Readers answer differently.

- **A Moabite man, not a Moabite woman.** The Mishnah (Yevamot 8:3) rules that Ammonite and Moabite men are barred, but "their female counterparts, even the convert herself, are permitted immediately." The Talmud (Yevamot 76b-77a) tells the ruling as a dispute over David himself: Doeg objects that David "descends from Ruth the Moabite"; Abner answers that the verse means "a Moabite man... but not a Moabite woman," a reason drawn from Deuteronomy 23:4's charge that they did not meet Israel "with bread and with water," since "it is the way of a man to go forth to meet guests"; and the study hall confirms it. The same page has David give thanks that the rule covers "Ruth the Moabite woman and Na’ama the Ammonite woman," and reads Lot's daughters "found here" (Genesis 19:15) beside "I have found David, My servant" (Psalm 89, in the Talmud's translation): "The lost article that was found among the daughters of Lot... is David and his royal house." Gill on Deuteronomy 23:3 reports the same distinction from Ibn Ezra, and Ellicott cites Rashi's reading of the verse as a marriage ban, that an Ammonite or Moabite man "shall not marry an Israelitish woman."
- **The law concerns naturalization, not marriage.** Barnes says the law "forbids only the naturalization of those against whom it is directed" and "was not understood at any rate to interdict marriage with a Moabitess," adding that Ruth was "doubtless" a proselyte. Keil (on Ruth 1:4): "Marriage with daughters of the Moabites was not forbidden in the law, like marriages with Canaanitish women... it was only the reception of Moabites into the congregation of the Lord that was forbidden."
- **An exception, not a rule.** Poole thinks the law covered women as well, citing Ezra 10 and Nehemiah 13, and answers the cases of Rahab and Ruth this way: "these were extraordinary instances, and that God when he pleased might exempt any particular person of them from this curse, though the Israelites might not do so."

*Objections each reading faces.* The rabbinic reading has to meet Doeg's challenge (the men should have met the men and the women the women), the challenge Abner could not answer (76b), which the Talmud later answers (77a) from Psalm 45 and Genesis 18:9. The naturalization reading has to meet Nehemiah 13:1-3, where the law was applied to exclude "all of foreign descent." Poole's exception has to explain why the book of Ruth itself contains, as the Pulpit Commentary notes of the marriages, "not the least hint of blame."

**4. This passage and abuse today.** This section is not one more reading to weigh against those above. It names a use of the passage and states what the text does and does not say.

- **The passage has been read to put blame on daughters.** Chizkuni, commenting on 19:8, explains Lot's offer of his daughters to the crowd by reading forward to the cave: he holds that "the daughters of Lot were not chaste and did not shy away from engaging in seducing men, as we know from later when they initiated carnal relations with their own father." Abby Eisenberg, writing for the Jewish Theological Seminary (2021), quotes this and states: "This victim-blaming interpretation is deeply offensive and unacceptable for us today." Kutz argues that the story's shape, with daughters as initiators and a father who knew nothing, matches the excuse abusers give ("It wasn't my fault; it was she who seduced me... I was too drunk"), and he reports a woman whose father abused her "while whispering in her ear that 'the Bible itself does not condemn this sort of thing.'" On that last claim the text can be checked: Leviticus 18 does not list one's own daughter by name, but it opens with "None of you are to approach any close relative to have sexual relations" (18:6) and forbids a man "both a woman and her daughter" (18:17).
- **What the text does and does not say.** Genesis 19:30-38 reports an act and does not approve it; it addresses no reader's situation and gives no one permission for anything. It does not give the daughters' ages, and it says nothing about who is responsible when a child or a vulnerable adult is harmed today. Whatever a reader concludes about Lot and his daughters in Position 1, the passage cannot be used to excuse abuse or to place its blame on the person harmed. Abuse is never the fault of the person who was abused.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge; it models the shape and is not an answer key. It builds only on what the narrator says about Lot, an adult, before anything happens in the cave. Nothing about the daughters or the act in 19:32-36 is carried forward.

**Passage element.** Genesis 19:21 and 19:30. Zoar is the town spared at Lot's own request: "I will grant this request as well, and will not demolish the town you indicate" (19:21). Yet Lot leaves it and settles in the mountains, "for he was afraid to stay in Zoar" (19:30).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text sets a spared, inhabited town (19:21) beside the narrator's statement that Lot left it out of fear (19:30); the text gives fear as the reason and no other. Inference: for hearers who knew Zoar still stood, the account showed fear after a catastrophe drawing Lot away from the place of safety he had himself asked for.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Fear after a disaster can pull a person away from help that is actually there.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: an adult survivor of a catastrophe, fear, and withdrawal from an inhabited place of refuge. Not carried forward: anything about the daughters or the act in the cave, any judgment on Lot beyond the narrator's one stated reason, and any claim that a modern survivor's fear resembles his in kind.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed.

**Modern domain and situation.** Community. After a flood destroys his home, an adult survivor turns down a place in the shelter set up in the next town, sure that town will flood too, and moves alone to a remote property with no phone signal.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: reconnection. One faithful response is for a neighbor or disaster caseworker to visit, listen to the fear without dismissing it, and help him reach the aid that exists in the next town, at a pace he can accept.

**Cautions.** This is not mental-health or disaster-safety guidance. If someone after a disaster seems at risk of harming themselves, contact local emergency services or, in the US, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline); follow local authorities in a real emergency. The learner may find the analogy does not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose.

1. The narrator gives no verdict, and the daughters' reasoning is reported in their own words. When a text reports a motive without judging it, what in the wording would you need to see before saying the text approves, condemns, or neither?
2. The words "he was not aware" are said twice (19:33, 35), and a scribal dot sits over one word in 19:33. How much should a reader rest on a mark in the manuscript tradition, as against the sentence the mark sits in?
3. Genesis names the sons as ancestors of Moab and Ammon; Deuteronomy 2 protects their land, Deuteronomy 23 excludes them, Ruth brings a Moabite into David's line, and Jeremiah promises both restoration. Read together, do these texts look like a polemic, a family history, or something else?
4. In Hebrew, the second element of "Ben-ammi" (19:38) and the word "my people" in "your people will be my people" (Ruth 1:16) are the same written form, *ʿammi*, tagged on BibleHub's interlinear as part of a proper name at 19:38 (Strong's 1151) and as the common noun at Ruth 1:16 (5971). This pairing is this lesson's own observation; no source read for this lesson connects the two, and Ruth is a Moabite, not an Ammonite. Is a shared common word evidence of anything?

## Teach-Back Prompts

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 19:30-38 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) State in the text's own words what the older daughter gives as her reason (19:31-32), what the narrator says of Lot's state (19:33, 35), and what the sons are said to be (19:37-38). (c) Name one place elsewhere in Scripture where Moab or Ammon reappears, and say what kind of connection it is.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) the sequence and what the text leaves unstated; (1:00-1:45) where Moab and Ammon go next; (1:45-3:30) Positions 1-3, each with its objection; (3:30-4:15) the line to Ruth, David and Matthew 1:5, with its grades; (4:15-5:00) the misuse named plainly, and the boundary (prompt 4).
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own first. If you are stuck, here are two, running in opposite directions. One: setting Kutz's and Gafney's readings beside the Talmud and Augustine treats a modern retelling that contradicts the text's "he was not aware" as if it had the standing of a reading of the text. Two: reporting Calvin's and Keil's blame of the daughters calmly, beside readings that see them as the harmed party, gives a reading that has been used against victims the same standing as one that names that harm. How far does separating Context (the text) from Positions (readers' conclusions) answer each?
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one. (a) Genesis 19:30-38 states no verdict on the daughters or on Lot; every judgment above, condemning or excusing, goes beyond its words. (b) The passage says nothing about the character of the Moabites' or Ammonites' descendants, names no later people, and gives Deuteronomy 23's exclusion no reason; Deuteronomy itself gives the reason as hostility in the wilderness. (c) Matthew 1:5 names Ruth, not Moab; it does not cite Genesis 19, and no New Testament verse names Moab or Ammon.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Name its type and label and point to the words in both texts that support the grade.

The thread here is genealogical and narrative, not prophetic: no text calls Genesis 19 a prophecy or a type of anything in the New Testament. Candidates considered and not authored: Deuteronomy 23:3-6 (it names both peoples but gives a different reason for their exclusion and does not refer to Genesis 19; it is handled in Position 3); Zephaniah 2:9 ("Moab will be like Sodom"), whose tie to 19:30-38 is this lesson's own observation; Irenaeus's reading of the two daughters as "the two churches," a typology no New Testament text states and so a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`; and Matthew 1:7's Rehoboam, whose Ammonite mother Matthew does not name.
