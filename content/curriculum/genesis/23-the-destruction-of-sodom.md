Genesis 19:1-29 is the night two angels spend in Sodom and the morning the city is destroyed: "all the men of the city of Sodom, both young and old" surround Lot's house and demand his guests (19:4-5); Lot offers his daughters (19:8); the visitors strike the crowd blind and lead Lot's family out; "the LORD rained down sulfur and fire on Sodom and Gomorrah" (19:24); Lot's wife looks back; and Abraham sees the smoke rising "like smoke from a furnace" (19:28). Readers disagree, with real consequence for people's lives, about what Sodom's sin was, and the city's name became a legal word used for centuries to punish people. This lesson sets out what the text says and does not say, lists every later biblical reference to Sodom, and reports the main named readings -- a traditional Christian reading, a violence-and-injustice reading (drawing heavily on Jewish sources), and affirming readings -- each in its own words and at comparable strength. It does not decide the modern ethical question about same-sex relationships. The chapter contains sexual violence, threatened and offered; resources are in Position 2. Every BSB quotation here and in the five connection files was checked by script against this app's shipped corpus; see Sources and Limits.

## Context

**What happens, in order (19:1-29).** The two angels reach Sodom at evening; Lot, "sitting in the gateway of the city," presses them to stay and "prepared a feast for them" (19:1-3). "Before they had gone to bed, all the men of the city of Sodom, both young and old, surrounded the house" (19:4) and demand the visitors; Lot offers his daughters; the crowd moves "to break down the door" (19:5-9). The visitors strike the crowd "with blindness" and tell Lot, "we are about to destroy this place" (19:10-13); his sons-in-law "thought he was joking" (19:14). At dawn, "when Lot hesitated," the visitors take the family by the hand, "because of the LORD's compassion for them" (19:15-16); Lot is granted the small town of Zoar (19:17-23). The LORD rains down "sulfur and fire" (19:24-25); "Lot's wife looked back, and she became a pillar of salt" (19:26); Abraham sees the smoke (19:27-28); and the narrator closes: "He remembered Abraham, and He brought Lot out of the catastrophe" (19:29).

**Where this unit sits.** Stage 5 of this app's eleven-stage lens ("Israel: The Twelve Tribes," `chapters: ["1.12"]`). Chapter 19 is the visit announced in 18:20-21 -- "The outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah is great. Because their sin is so grievous, I will go down to see if their actions fully justify the outcry that has reached Me" -- after Abraham bargains from fifty righteous people down to ten (18:23-32). Lot's settling "toward Sodom" (13:12) and 2 Peter 2:6-8's verdict on Lot were covered in this curriculum's Genesis 13 lesson; Lot's daughters in the cave (19:30-38) belong to a later lesson.

**What 19:4-9 says, and what it does not.** The crowd is "all the men of the city of Sodom, both young and old" (19:4). Their demand: "Where are the men who came to you tonight? Send them out to us so we can have relations with them!" (19:5). Lot calls it "such a wicked thing" (19:7), offers "two daughters who have never slept with a man," and gives his reason: "they have come under the protection of my roof" (19:8). The crowd replies, "This one came here as a foreigner, and he is already acting like a judge! Now we will treat you worse than them," and moves "to break down the door" (19:9). The verses do not describe the men's desires apart from this night, do not list the city's sin, and do not say which element of the night -- the sexual act, its being forced, its target being male, its target being guests, or contempt for the outsider who sheltered them -- is the reason for the destruction. Genesis gives the reason earlier and generally: an "outcry" and a sin "so grievous" (18:20; again 19:13), whose content it does not state. Every reading below is, in part, an answer to that silence.

**The word behind "have relations with" (19:5).** The BSB phrase renders the Hebrew verb usually translated "know"; the same English phrase appears in the Gibeah parallel at Judges 19:22. Most commentators read for this lesson take it as sexual because of 19:8, where the daughters "have never slept with a man" (the same verb): Matthew Poole glosses 19:8 "to wit, carnally," and Victor Hamilton, quoted by Melvin Otey (*Themelios* 49.1), says the offer makes "clear that the issue is intercourse and not friendship." Poole also names the alternative before rejecting it: "Either know who they are; or rather abuse them." Otey reports John Boswell crediting the view that the men "meant no more than to 'know' who they were"; Scott Morschauser (*JSOT*, 2003) arguing that the implication of "know" (Otey cites it at 19:8) "is that the men be produced for interrogation"; and John Calvin, who rejects Boswell's conclusion, as reading the verb as "something akin to making acquaintance or discovering identity" (Otey's paraphrase, n. 25). The main sources for all three readings below take 19:5 as a demand made by force, and most take it as sexual; Boswell, Morschauser and, on the verb alone, Calvin as Otey reports him are the dissenters.

**Every later reference to Sodom, and what each names.** A script scan of the whole BSB finds "Sodom" in 47 verses: 20 in Genesis and 27 across 13 other books. Both sides argue from these, so they are set out here; where a verse names no sin, that is stated.

- **Deuteronomy 29:23; Isaiah 13:19; Jeremiah 49:18, 50:40; Lamentations 4:6; Amos 4:11; Zephaniah 2:9; Romans 9:29 (quoting Isaiah 1:9).** Sodom's overthrow as the measure of total ruin -- "a burning waste of sulfur and salt... just like the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah" (Deuteronomy 29:23). No sin of Sodom's own is named. (Zephaniah 2:10's "their pride, for taunting and mocking the people of the LORD" is said of Moab and Ammon.)
- **Deuteronomy 32:32.** "the vine of Sodom" as an image of bitter fruit; no specific sin.
- **Isaiah 1:9-10; 3:9.** Jerusalem's leaders are "you rulers of Sodom," and "like Sodom they flaunt their sin; they do not conceal it." The sins those chapters name are Jerusalem's, among them "seek justice and correct the oppressor" (1:17) and "the plunder of the poor is in your houses" (3:14).
- **Jeremiah 23:14.** Of Jerusalem's prophets: "They commit adultery and walk in lies. They strengthen the hands of evildoers, so that no one turns his back on wickedness. They are all like Sodom to Me." The sins are the prophets'; the comparison is to Sodom.
- **Ezekiel 16:46-56.** The fullest statement: "Now this was the iniquity of your sister Sodom: She and her daughters were arrogant, overfed, and complacent; they did not help the poor and needy. Thus they were haughty and committed abominations before Me. Therefore I removed them, as you have seen" (16:49-50); God "will restore Sodom and her daughters" (16:53). The BSB's plural "abominations" in 16:50 renders a Hebrew singular (*to'evah*) by BibleHub's interlinear; 16:47's "their abominations" is plural in Hebrew, and the singular at 33:26 is rendered "detestable acts." Peterson suggests translators were "perhaps following the plural" in the Greek Septuagint. The passage sits in a chapter built on the image of Jerusalem as an unfaithful wife (16:15-34), a setting Brian Neil Peterson makes central to his reading.
- **Matthew 10:15; 11:23-24; Luke 10:12.** It will be "more bearable for Sodom" on the day of judgment than for towns that reject Jesus' messengers or miracles. The sin these verses name is the rejecting towns' -- not welcoming (Matthew 10:14) and, of Chorazin, Bethsaida and Capernaum, not repenting (11:20). They name no sin of Sodom's in so many words; Matthew Vines reads them as associating Sodom's sin with inhospitality (see Positions).
- **Luke 17:28-29.** "People were eating and drinking, buying and selling, planting and building. But on the day Lot left Sodom, fire and sulfur rained down from heaven and destroyed them all." The point is unpreparedness; no sin of Sodom's is named.
- **2 Peter 2:6-8.** God "condemned the cities of Sodom and Gomorrah to destruction" and rescued Lot, "distressed by the depraved conduct of the lawless." The BSB's "depraved conduct" translates *aselgeia*, which other versions render "sensual conduct"; Vines counts the verse among the New Testament texts that "describe Sodom as sexually immoral," while calling the word "a general term."
- **Jude 1:7.** "Sodom and Gomorrah and the cities around them, who indulged in sexual immorality and pursued strange flesh, are on display as an example of those who sustain the punishment of eternal fire." In the BSB's wording, Jude 1:7 is the only later reference that names a sexual sin of Sodom's own in so many words; 2 Peter 2:7's "depraved conduct" is counted as sexual by some readers, and Ezekiel 16:50's "abomination" is disputed (see Positions). Some versions render Jude's phrase "perversion" or "unnatural desire"; what "strange flesh" means is itself disputed.
- **Revelation 11:8** calls Jerusalem "figuratively called Sodom and Egypt," naming no specific sin.

The data cut both ways: most references name no sin; Ezekiel's list leads with arrogance, excess and neglect of the poor and then adds "abominations"; the prophets use Sodom as a byword for a society's whole corruption; Jude names sexual sin. What weight each carries is what the positions below dispute.

## Literary Design

**Two hospitality scenes.** Abraham, "at the entrance of his tent," runs to meet three men, bows, offers water to "wash your feet," and stands by them "as they ate" (18:1-8); Lot, "sitting in the gateway of the city," rises to meet two of them, bows, and offers to let them "wash your feet and spend the night" before preparing "a feast" (19:1-3). Then the scenes diverge: at Abraham's tent the visitors eat in peace; at Lot's house the city surrounds the door. Vines reads the pairing as the chapter's frame; Peterson argues the frame is wider (see Positions).

**Looking back.** "Do not look back" (19:17); "Lot's wife looked back" (19:26); two verses later Abraham "looked down toward Sodom and Gomorrah" (19:28). Luke 17:32's "Remember Lot's wife!" picks up the middle verse.

**Rescue by the hand.** The narrator gives God's motive twice: "because of the LORD's compassion for them" (19:16) and "He remembered Abraham" (19:29). Neither credits Lot's own righteousness; 2 Peter 2:7-8 supplies that word from outside Genesis.

## Positions

Three questions are reported below. The first is the long-running dispute over what Sodom's sin was, reported through its named readings without a ruling. The second is Lot's offer of his daughters. The third is the history of the passage's use against gay people, which this lesson names in its own section, as this curriculum's Genesis 9 lesson did for the "Curse of Ham." Positions report what readers conclude; the Context section above reports what the text states.

**1. What was Sodom's sin?** Three families of reading are reported, each from its own named sources, followed by each side's reply to the other. The order is not a ranking.

- **A traditional Christian reading: the attempted same-sex act is central, alongside the violence and inhospitality.** Brian Neil Peterson, an Old Testament scholar writing in the *Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society* (2016), agrees that attempted gang rape and "the contravention of hospitality" are "part of the overall problem in Sodom," but argues that reading Genesis 19 "in light of Torah" -- especially the sexual laws of Leviticus 18 and 20 -- shows "the pervasive sin problem in Sodom: homosexuality, which in this case is perpetrated against strangers, infidelity in marriage, and a rejection of God's moral" standards. In a second article (*JETS*, 2018) he argues that Ezekiel 16:50's "abomination" is singular in Hebrew, that the singular corresponds to Leviticus 18:22 (BSB: "You must not lie with a man as with a woman; that is an abomination"), and that Ezekiel, a priest, was "drawing upon the Holiness Code of Leviticus... in order to highlight Sodom's main sin of homosexuality." Peterson reports Robert Gagnon (*The Bible and Homosexual Practice*, 2001) reaching the same conclusions on Ezekiel; Gagnon's own book was not read for this lesson. Melvin Otey (*Themelios* 49.1) adds a historical argument: that the sexual reading is not a medieval invention. He cites earlier Jewish texts that charge Sodom with fornication (Jubilees, the Testament of Benjamin), first-century Jewish writers who specify same-sex acts -- Josephus (the Sodomites "resolved themselves to enjoy these beautiful boys by force and violence") and Philo ("men ha[ving] intercourse with males") -- and Jude 7. His conclusion keeps the other elements in view: "it is better to acknowledge all of the prominent elements in the ancient narrative, including the attempt to transgress natural and supernatural boundaries, rape, and inhospitality, without dismissing the emphasis on divine condemnation of homosexuality." Among classic commentators, Matthew Poole on 19:5 cross-references Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13 and Romans 1:26-27, and John Gill on Ezekiel 16:50 reads "committed abomination" as "perhaps referring to that sin, which has its name from them." A confessional statement takes the same line: the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §2357 states that "Sacred Scripture... presents homosexual acts as acts of grave depravity," citing Genesis 19:1-29 first in its footnote (n. 140), and adds that such acts are "contrary to the natural law" and "Under no circumstances can they be approved." §2358 calls the inclination itself "objectively disordered" and in the same paragraph says such persons "must be accepted with respect, compassion, and sensitivity. Every sign of unjust discrimination in their regard should be avoided." §2359 adds: "Homosexual persons are called to chastity."

- **A reading centered on violence, cruelty to strangers, and injustice.** On this reading, the night at Lot's door is an act of collective violence against vulnerable outsiders, and it is the symptom, not the whole, of a city whose "outcry" (18:20) had reached heaven. Its textual anchor is Ezekiel 16:49: "arrogant, overfed, and complacent; they did not help the poor and needy." Jewish tradition has long emphasized Sodom's injustice and cruelty. The Babylonian Talmud (Sanhedrin 109a-b, read directly) teaches that "The people of Sodom became haughty and sinned due only to the excessive goodness" God gave them, and that they said, "Why do we need travelers, as they come only to divest us of our property? Come, let us cause the proper treatment of travelers to be forgotten from our land." It catalogues crimes against the poor and the stranger, and identifies the "outcry" of Genesis 18:20 with "a young woman who would take bread out to the poor people in a pitcher," and who was killed for it: "It is due to that sin that the fate of the people of Sodom was sealed." The Mishnah (Pirkei Avot 5:10) names a character type after the city: the one who says "mine is mine, and yours is yours": "this is a commonplace type; and some say this is a sodom-type of character." Ramban (Nachmanides, 13th century; Chavel's translation) reads 19:5 itself this way: "Their intention was to stop people from coming among them... and they despised charity." He reads Ezekiel 16:50's "abomination" as summarizing the sins just listed -- they "continued provoking and rebelling against Him with their ease and the oppression of the poor. It is this thought which Scripture expresses by saying, And they were haughty, and committed abomination before Me" -- and, while noting that in the Rabbis' view "all evil practices" "were rampant among them," holds that the city's fate was sealed because "they did not strengthen the hand of the poor and needy." The Jewish sources do not speak with one voice, however: they hold both elements. Peterson cites Genesis Rabbah and the Targums on Genesis 13:13 for the sexual reading; Targum Jonathan (Etheridge's translation) says the men of Sodom "sinned with open nakedness, and the shedding of innocent blood, and practiced strange worship," and Genesis Rabbah 50:7 puts the two elements together in the townspeople's own mouths: "Any guest who comes here, we will have sexual relations with them and take their possessions." Among modern scholars, Otey reports David M. Carr reading the episode as God's "condemnation of a town that violated hospitality by trying to rape guests in one of its homes" rather than a judgment on homosexual behavior.

- **Affirming readings: the text concerns sexual violence, not consensual relationships.** Matthew Vines, in "Was Homosexuality the Sin of Sodom and Gomorrah?" (The Reformation Project, read directly), grants that "in Genesis 19:5, the men of Sodom demand that Lot bring out his guests so that they can have sex with them," that "this would at least ostensibly have been a same-sex act, and it certainly is presented very negatively," and argues: "First, the men are not expressing romantic interest in Lot's guests. They are threatening to gang rape them." He reads the threat against ancient male-on-male rape "as a tactic of aggression, domination, and humiliation," and against the Gibeah parallel in Judges 19, concluding: "Gang rape and murder. That's what the men of Sodom threatened to do, and it's what the men of Gibeah both threatened and carried out. This is indeed evil, but it has nothing to do with same-sex relationships that are consensual, much less those that are marked by long-term commitment, faithfulness, and love." He reads the women offered in both stories as evidence of patriarchy, not of a teaching about gender: "women were thought to be less important than men." On the later texts he states that "When Sodom's evil is specified, the focus is generally on injustice, oppression, and idolatry," and, in an earlier talk, that "In Matthew 10 and Luke 10, Jesus associates the sin of Sodom with inhospitable treatment of his disciples." On the history, he dates the first same-sex reading of Sodom to Philo in the first century -- "The first writer who interpreted Sodom's destruction as punishment for same-sex behavior was the Hellenistic Jewish philosopher Philo of Alexandria" -- and argues that for Philo, "same-sex behavior wasn't an expression of a sexual orientation. It was an expression of out-of-control lust," the product, as Vines summarizes Philo, of "Goods in excess." Peterson cites James Brownson (*Bible, Gender, Sexuality*, 2013, p. 268) and Ken Wilson among affirming interpreters of the same kind; Otey quotes the theologian Shannon Craigo-Snell: "when some Christians in the medieval period were convinced that same sex relations were wrong, they reinterpreted the story of Sodom and Gomorrah in line with their beliefs."

- **The traditional reply to the affirming readings.** Peterson's 2016 article is written as that reply, naming Vines and Brownson in its first footnote. He answers on three points. First, context: "Long before the men of Sodom failed to offer hospitality to the angels/men, the reader is told twice by the author that the 'men'... of the city of Sodom were exceedingly wicked and sinners (13:13), and that the cry of its people had come up before YHWH (18:20)." Second, Ezekiel: the singular "abomination" in 16:50 is distinct from the plural "abominations" of 16:51, so "it is not a matter of pitting inhospitality against homosexual activity; it is, as Davidson puts it, not 'either-or' but 'both-and'" (quoting Richard Davidson, *Flame of Yahweh*, 2007); at the heart of Sodom's actions, he adds, was, in Robert Gagnon's words, "human arrogance in relation to God." Third, the rest of the chapter: he argues that 19:15-38, including the incest of Lot's daughters, "sheds just as much light on the sin of Sodom as do the attempted rape of the angels and the refusal of hospitality." Otey's reply is historical: against the claim that the sexual reading is late, he answers that "Second Temple literature demonstrates that the view predates the Christian church."

- **The affirming reply to the traditional reading.** Vines answers Peterson's two textual arguments in the same article. On Ezekiel 16:50, the Hebrew word *toevah* "is used 117 times in the Old Testament, and only five of those uses refer to or encompass the prohibitions of male same-sex intercourse in Leviticus 18 and 20... In Ezekiel 16 specifically, toevah is used seven other times to describe idolatry and adultery, not same-sex behavior." On Jude 7: "the Greek phrase that is used here is sarkos heteras, which literally means 'other' or different flesh," so "Jude 7 indicts the men of Sodom for pursuing flesh that was too different from their own, not too similar" -- that is, angels. He quotes Richard Hays (*The Moral Vision of the New Testament*, 1996): "it is impossible to construe this passage as a condemnation of homosexual desire," and reports that Preston Sprinkle, who does not hold the affirming view, wrote that "'strange flesh' refers not to people of the same sex, but to angels." (Hays and Sprinkle are reported here as Vines quotes them; their books were not read.) On Otey's historical point, Vines agrees the reading begins with Philo but argues it "didn't become the consensus Christian view until the fifth century," and that "even then, same-sex behavior was understood as lustful excess, not loving orientation."

The sourcing behind these readings is uneven, and a reader should know it: the traditional Christian reading is represented by two peer-reviewed articles, a scholarly journal article and a Catechism, all read directly; the violence-and-injustice reading by the Talmud, Mishnah, Midrash, Targum and Ramban read directly, with its one modern scholar (Carr) reported through a critic; and the affirming reading by Vines's own published article and talk read directly, with its academic voices (Brownson, Craigo-Snell, Boswell, Hays) reported through others (see Sources and Limits).

Each reading has to answer something. The traditional Christian reading has to account for the fact that, of the 27 verses outside Genesis that name Sodom, only Jude 7, and arguably 2 Peter 2:7, name sexual sin of Sodom's own (Ezekiel's "abomination" is itself disputed), and that Ezekiel's explicit list ("Now this was the iniquity...") leads with arrogance, excess and neglect of the poor; it answers with the singular "abomination," with Jude 7, and with early Jewish readers. The violence-and-injustice reading has to account for Jude 7's "sexual immorality" and for the sexual reading's presence in Jewish sources on 19:5 -- Rashi glosses the demand "with homosexuality," in the Sefaria translation, citing Genesis Rabbah 50:5, which reads it as a demand "for sexual relations" and treats that demand as the point at which Lot's pleading for the city ends: "from now on, you are no longer permitted to advocate on their behalf"; the reading answers that the night's sexual demand was itself the violence. The affirming readings have to account for the same evidence and, further, for the claim that a text about forced sex has no bearing on consensual relationships; they answer that the absence of consent is the very thing the narrative depicts. Two further disputes run underneath all three. One is what Jude's "strange flesh" means: Poole reads it as "male flesh"; Barnes as "the unusual sin which, from the name Sodom, has been called 'sodomy'"; the *Expositor's Greek Testament* as "departure from the natural use"; Otey, citing Jeremy Hultin, as the Sodomites seeking "to have sexual relations with the angels," adding that "Since they do not know the visitors are angels rather than mere men, their intentions, at least, are homoerotic"; and Vines, Hays and Sprinkle as angelic flesh, not same-sex desire. The other is what Ezekiel's singular "abomination" refers to. Peterson ties it to Leviticus 18:22 and lists Ezekiel's singular uses as 16:50, 18:12, 22:11 and 33:26, arguing they occur "when sexual sins are in view" (BSB 22:11: "One man commits an abomination with his neighbor's wife"; 18:12, which the BSB renders as a plural, sits in a list that begins "He oppresses the poor and needy"). He anticipates the opposing view -- "Some may argue" that the word is "merely an encapsulating term for all the listed sins that come before it" -- and answers it from the plural in 16:51; Ramban, quoted above, reads it as that summary, and Vines points to the word's wider use. A reader can check each of the four verses in context.

This lesson reports these readings and does not resolve them. Above all, it does not decide the question many readers bring to this chapter -- whether same-sex relationships are right or wrong -- which turns on many other texts and on each tradition's whole reading of Scripture, not on Genesis 19 alone, and which the sources above answer differently. A reader working this out for their own life, or for someone they love, needs a pastor or counselor who knows them, not a study note.

**2. Lot's offer of his daughters (19:8).** "Look, I have two daughters who have never slept with a man. Let me bring them to you, and you can do to them as you please." The text records the offer and does not comment on it. It is not presented in Genesis as an example to follow, and this lesson does not present it as one. The commentators quoted here divide mainly over how to account for it.

- **Condemned, with or without extenuation.** Ramban: "From the praise of this man Lot we have come to his disgrace... This bespeaks nothing but an evil heart for it shows that the matter of prostitution of women was not repugnant to him." Keil and Delitzsch: Lot "committed the sin of seeking to avert sin by sin," and "the offer was a grievous violation of his paternal duty." Benson, Gill and Poole also condemn the offer, each allowing some extenuation from Lot's agitation of mind (Gill: "the hurry of spirit, and confusion of mind that he was in").
- **Explained by ancient custom.** The Cambridge Bible: "Lot's proposal, so atrocious in our ears, may have been deemed meritorious in an Eastern country, where no sacrifice was considered too great to maintain inviolate the safety of a stranger who had been received in hospitality," and it attributes Lot's choice "not so much to the weakness of the man as to the terribly low estimate of womanhood which prevailed at that time." Ellicott reports two church fathers taking a similar line -- "Granting with St. Ambrose that it was the substitution of a smaller for a greater sin, and with St. Chrysostom that Lot was bound by the laws of hospitality to do his utmost to protect his guests" -- and then rejects the excuse: "yet he was also bound as a father equally to protect his daughters to the last extremity." The Geneva Bible's note holds both halves: "He deserves praise for defending his guests, but he is to be blamed for seeking unlawful means."

The daughters are not consulted, are not named, and say nothing in 19:1-29. The crowd refuses the offer (19:9), and the visitors' intervention (19:10-11) is what prevents harm. Judges 19:24-25, the parallel at Gibeah, shows the same offer made and the harm then done (see the reviewed connection). These are things the text records; the lesson draws no lesson from Lot's conduct here.

Elsewhere, contact local sexual-assault services, and if you are in immediate danger, call your local emergency number. **3. The passage's use against gay people.** This section is not one more reading to weigh against those in Position 1. It names a history, and states what the sources on every side read for this lesson say about it. Reporting any reading above is not endorsing any such use.

- **From the city's name to a crime punishable by death.** In 559 the Emperor Justinian issued a law (Novel 141, read directly in S. P. Scott's translation) against "the corruption of males," grounding it in Genesis 19: "We know, from the study of the Holy Scriptures, that God, in order to punish such persons, visited His wrath upon those who formerly inhabited the City of Sodom, and caused its territory to be consumed, even to this day, by an inextinguishable fire." An earlier law (Novel 77), which grouped such acts with blasphemy and swearing by God's head, blamed "famine, earthquake, and pestilence" on "crimes of this description" and ordered the city prefect to arrest those who persisted "and inflict upon them the punishment of death." The city's name passed into the law of Christian Europe as "sodomy." The United States Supreme Court's opinion in *Lawrence v. Texas* (2003) traces American sodomy laws to "the English criminal laws passed in the first instance by the Reformation Parliament of 1533." In *Bowers v. Hardwick* (1986), which upheld a Georgia statute authorizing up to 20 years' imprisonment, Chief Justice Burger's concurrence wrote that condemnation of such conduct "is firmly rooted in" Judeo-Christian "moral and ethical standards," cited Roman capital law, and repeated Blackstone's language of disgust; Burger did not cite Genesis, but the legal category he defended bore the city's name. *Lawrence* reversed that decision: "Bowers was not correct when it was decided, and it is not correct today... Bowers v. Hardwick should be and now is overruled." The same opinion found the historical grounds relied on in *Bowers* "more complex than the majority opinion and the concurring opinion by Chief Justice Burger indicate," and found that early American sodomy laws were not "directed at homosexual conduct as a distinct matter" and that "It was not until the 1970's that any State singled out same-sex relations for criminal prosecution."
- **What the sources on each side say about that use.** The affirming side names it as harm: Craigo-Snell, quoted by Otey, says such readings "have caused concrete harm to God's beloved LGBTQ children." One traditional confessional text, the Catholic Catechism, quoted in Position 1, rejects "unjust discrimination" in its own words: "Every sign of unjust discrimination in their regard should be avoided" (CCC §2358). Justinian's law rested on the reading that Sodom was destroyed for same-sex acts; holding that reading does not entail endorsing such laws, and none of the modern authors quoted in Position 1 argues for them in the texts read for this lesson.
- **What the text itself does and does not say.** Genesis 19 narrates a judgment of God on a city; it gives no instruction to any human court, ruler, or crowd to punish anyone. That is a checkable fact about the narrative, not a ruling on the dispute in Position 1.

This lesson does not caricature the traditional Christian reading as hateful: its sources state their own reasons, from Scripture and, in the Catechism's case, from natural law as well, and the Catechism's next paragraph after the one that cites Genesis 19 (§2358) calls for respect. Nor does it let the history above stand unremarked: the passage was used to justify death sentences and imprisonment, and that use is named here so that a reader can see it plainly. A reader who has been hurt by how this passage was used against them or someone they love deserves to be heard by a pastor or counselor who knows them, not handed a study note.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge; it models the shape and is not an answer key. It deliberately does not use 19:4-9.

**Passage element.** Genesis 19:12-17. The visitors warn that "we are about to destroy this place" (19:13); the sons-in-law "thought he was joking" (19:14); "when Lot hesitated," the visitors take the family by the hand (19:16); the command is "Run for your lives! Do not look back" (19:17).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text records three responses to one warning -- dismissal, hesitation, looking back. Inference: for hearers who knew the ending, the account made the cost of delay vivid without stating a moral; the text gives God's compassion, not Lot's promptness, as the reason for the rescue (19:16).

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** A credible warning of real danger calls for action, not debate; people we love may dismiss or delay it, and sometimes the faithful response is to take someone by the hand.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: real, imminent danger; a clear warning; the tendency to delay. Not carried forward: the divine judgment, the messengers, and any suggestion that people who suffer in a modern disaster are being judged.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed; the rescue in 19:16 and 19:29 is told of Lot, not promised to anyone who heeds a warning.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family. An evacuation order is issued ahead of a wildfire; an elderly parent says the warnings are always exaggerated and wants to wait and see.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: protective action. One faithful response: an adult child takes the warning seriously, makes the plan concrete (where to go, what to bring, who drives), and stays with the parent until they leave -- persistence, not argument.

**Cautions.** Follow local authorities in a real emergency; this is not safety guidance. Persistence is not coercion; where someone's safety or capacity is in doubt, emergency services or a qualified professional is the right next step. The bridge does not read disasters as judgments, and the learner may find it does not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose.

1. Genesis names Sodom's sin only as an "outcry" and a sin "so grievous" (18:20); the Talmud fills in the outcry with a young woman killed for feeding the poor. What in 19:1-11 alone would you point to as evidence of what the outcry was about?
2. Of the 27 verses outside Genesis that name Sodom, most name no sin of Sodom's own, Ezekiel 16:49-50 names several, and Jude 1:7 names sexual immorality. How should a reader weigh many references that name nothing against a few that name something?
3. Ezekiel 16:50 has a singular "abomination" in Hebrew where the BSB has a plural. Peterson ties it to Leviticus 18:22; Ramban reads it as a summary of 16:49's sins; Vines points to the word's wider use. What would you need to see to be confident what it refers to?
4. 2 Peter 2:7-8 calls Lot "righteous," yet he offers his daughters (19:8) and hesitates (19:16), and Genesis gives "the LORD's compassion" and "He remembered Abraham" as the reasons he was rescued. What does the narrator's choice of reasons suggest about how Genesis wants Lot to be seen?
