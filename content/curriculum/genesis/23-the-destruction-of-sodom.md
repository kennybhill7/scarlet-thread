Genesis 19:1-29 is the account of the night two angels spend in Sodom and the morning the city is destroyed. Lot takes the visitors in; "all the men of the city of Sodom, both young and old" surround his house and demand them (19:4-5); Lot offers his two daughters instead (19:8); the visitors strike the crowd blind, warn Lot's family, and drag them out of the city by the hand; "the LORD rained down sulfur and fire on Sodom and Gomorrah" (19:24); Lot's wife looks back; and the next morning Abraham sees the smoke rising "like smoke from a furnace" (19:28). It is among the most argued-over passages in Scripture. Readers disagree, with real consequence for people's lives, about what Sodom's sin was; the city's name became a legal word, and that word was used for centuries to punish people. This lesson does three things and declines a fourth. It sets out exactly what the text says and does not say. It lays out every later biblical reference to Sodom so a reader can see the evidence every side argues from. It reports the main named readings -- traditional, Jewish, and affirming -- each in its own words and at comparable strength. It does not decide the modern ethical question about same-sex relationships, which no study note can decide for a reader. The chapter also contains sexual violence, threatened and offered; a reader for whom that is close to home will find a resource in Position 2. Every quotation attributed to the BSB below, here and in this lesson's five connection files, was printed from this app's own shipped corpus by script before drafting, and a second script confirmed each fragment is a literal substring of the verse it is attributed to; see Sources and Limits.

## Context

**What happens, in order (19:1-29).** "Now the two angels arrived at Sodom in the evening, and Lot was sitting in the gateway of the city" (19:1). Lot rises, bows, and presses them to stay: "please turn aside into the house of your servant; wash your feet and spend the night" (19:2). They say they will spend the night in the square; "Lot insisted so strongly that they followed him into his house," where he "prepared a feast for them and baked unleavened bread" (19:3). "Before they had gone to bed, all the men of the city of Sodom, both young and old, surrounded the house" (19:4) and demand the visitors (19:5). Lot goes out, shuts the door behind him, pleads with them, and offers his daughters (19:6-8). The crowd answers with a threat and a sneer at Lot as an outsider, and moves "to break down the door" (19:9). The visitors pull Lot inside and strike the men at the entrance "with blindness" (19:10-11). They tell Lot to gather anyone who belongs to him, "because we are about to destroy this place" (19:12-13). His sons-in-law "thought he was joking" (19:14). At dawn the angels urge him on; "when Lot hesitated, the men grabbed his hand and the hands of his wife and his two daughters," and the text gives the reason: "because of the LORD's compassion for them" (19:15-16). Told to flee to the mountains, Lot asks for a small town instead, and is granted Zoar (19:17-23). The LORD rains down "sulfur and fire" and destroys "these cities and the entire plain" (19:24-25). "But Lot's wife looked back, and she became a pillar of salt" (19:26). Abraham returns to the place where he had stood before the LORD and sees the smoke (19:27-28), and the narrator closes the account: "He remembered Abraham, and He brought Lot out of the catastrophe" (19:29). That is the sequence; what it means is the rest of this lesson's work.

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens (`content/lens/eleven-stages.json`) places Genesis 12 through Malachi in stage 5, "Israel: The Twelve Tribes" (`chapters: ["1.12"]`). Genesis 19 is the second half of a two-chapter unit. In chapter 18 the LORD tells Abraham, "The outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah is great. Because their sin is so grievous, I will go down to see if their actions fully justify the outcry that has reached Me" (18:20-21), and Abraham bargains down from fifty righteous people to ten (18:23-32). Chapter 19 is the visit chapter 18 announced, and 19:29's "He remembered Abraham" ties the rescue back to that bargaining. Lot's choice to settle "toward Sodom," and the narrator's early notice that "the men of Sodom were wicked, sinning greatly against the LORD" (13:12-13), were treated in this curriculum's Genesis 13 lesson, which also covered 2 Peter 2:6-8's verdict on Lot; that ground is not re-walked here. Genesis 19:30-38, Lot's daughters in the cave, is a later lesson's territory and is not treated here.

**What 19:4-9 says, and what it does not.** Read slowly, the five verses state the following. The crowd is "all the men of the city of Sodom, both young and old" (19:4). Their demand is: "Where are the men who came to you tonight? Send them out to us so we can have relations with them!" (19:5). Lot calls it "such a wicked thing" (19:7). He offers "two daughters who have never slept with a man" and gives his reason for protecting the visitors: "they have come under the protection of my roof" (19:8). The crowd replies, "This one came here as a foreigner, and he is already acting like a judge! Now we will treat you worse than them," and "pressed in on Lot and moved in to break down the door" (19:9). What the verses do not state: they do not describe any relationship between the men of Sodom other than this demand; they do not say what the men's desires were apart from this night; they do not name the city's sin in a list; and they do not say, in Genesis 19 itself, which element of the night -- the intended sexual act, its being forced, its target being male, its target being guests, or the crowd's contempt for the outsider who sheltered them -- is the reason for the destruction. The reason Genesis itself gives is earlier and general: an "outcry" and a sin "so grievous" (18:20), named again in 19:13, "the outcry to the LORD against its people is so great." Genesis does not say what the outcry was about. Every reading in Positions below is, in part, an answer to that silence.

**The word behind "have relations with" (19:5).** The BSB's "have relations with" renders the Hebrew verb usually translated "know." The same English phrase appears at Judges 19:22, in the parallel story at Gibeah (see the reviewed connection). The verb's sense here is the first thing readers dispute. Most commentators read for this lesson take it as sexual because of 19:8, where Lot offers daughters "who have never slept with a man" (the same verb in Hebrew): Matthew Poole glosses 19:8's phrase "to wit, carnally," and Victor Hamilton is quoted by Melvin Otey (*Themelios* 49.1) as saying that when Lot offers his daughters, "it becomes clear that the issue is intercourse and not friendship." A minority have read 19:5 differently. Otey reports John Boswell crediting the argument that the men "meant no more than to 'know' who they were," and Scott Morschauser (*JSOT*, 2003) arguing that the implication of the verb "know" (Otey cites it at 19:8) "is that the men be produced for interrogation." Matthew Vines, an affirming writer, does not take that route; he reads the demand as a threat to "gang rape Lot's angel visitors" (see Positions). The sources quoted below for each of the three readings (Peterson and Otey; Carr and the rabbinic texts; Vines) all take 19:5 as a sexual demand made by force, and disagree about what follows from that; Boswell and Morschauser are the dissenters on the verb itself.

**Every later reference to Sodom, and what each names.** A script scan of this app's whole BSB text finds the name "Sodom" in 47 verses: 20 in Genesis and 27 across 13 other books. Both sides of the dispute below argue from these verses, so they are set out here. Where a verse names no sin, that is stated.

- **Deuteronomy 29:23; Isaiah 13:19; Jeremiah 49:18, 50:40; Lamentations 4:6; Amos 4:11; Zephaniah 2:9; Romans 9:29 (quoting Isaiah 1:9).** These use Sodom's overthrow as the measure of total ruin -- "a burning waste of sulfur and salt... just like the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah" (Deuteronomy 29:23). They name no sin of Sodom's own. (Zephaniah 2:10 gives Moab's and Ammon's sin as "their pride, for taunting and mocking the people of the LORD"; that is said of Moab and Ammon, not of Sodom.)
- **Deuteronomy 32:32** uses "the vine of Sodom" as an image of Israel's enemies' bitter fruit; it names no specific sin.
- **Isaiah 1:9-10.** Isaiah calls Jerusalem's leaders "you rulers of Sodom" and its people "you people of Gomorrah." The sins the same chapter names are Jerusalem's: "Your hands are covered with blood" (1:15); "seek justice and correct the oppressor. Defend the fatherless and plead the case of the widow" (1:17); "They all love bribes" (1:23).
- **Isaiah 3:9.** "like Sodom they flaunt their sin; they do not conceal it." The likeness named is open, unashamed sin; the charge brought a few verses later is "the plunder of the poor is in your houses" and "Why do you crush My people and grind the faces of the poor?" (3:14-15).
- **Jeremiah 23:14.** Of Jerusalem's prophets: "They commit adultery and walk in lies. They strengthen the hands of evildoers, so that no one turns his back on wickedness. They are all like Sodom to Me." The sins listed are the prophets'; the comparison is to Sodom.
- **Ezekiel 16:46-56.** The fullest statement: "Now this was the iniquity of your sister Sodom: She and her daughters were arrogant, overfed, and complacent; they did not help the poor and needy. Thus they were haughty and committed abominations before Me. Therefore I removed them, as you have seen" (16:49-50). The same passage says God "will restore Sodom and her daughters" (16:53). One textual detail matters to the dispute: the BSB has the plural "abominations" in 16:50, but the Hebrew interlinear parses the noun there (*to'evah*) as feminine singular, "abomination." What that singular refers to is argued in Positions below. The comparison sits inside a chapter built on the image of Jerusalem as an unfaithful wife (16:15-34), a setting Brian Neil Peterson makes central to his reading.
- **Matthew 10:15; 11:23-24; Luke 10:12.** Jesus says it will be "more bearable for Sodom" on the day of judgment than for towns that reject his messengers or his miracles. The sin these verses name is the Galilean towns' -- not welcoming or heeding (Matthew 10:14) and not repenting (11:20). They name no sin of Sodom's in so many words; Matthew Vines reads them as associating Sodom's sin with inhospitality (see Positions).
- **Luke 17:28-29.** "It was the same in the days of Lot: People were eating and drinking, buying and selling, planting and building. But on the day Lot left Sodom, fire and sulfur rained down from heaven and destroyed them all." The point named is unpreparedness in ordinary life; no sin of Sodom's is named.
- **2 Peter 2:6-8.** God "condemned the cities of Sodom and Gomorrah to destruction, reducing them to ashes as an example of what is coming on the ungodly," and rescued Lot, "distressed by the depraved conduct of the lawless." The phrase "depraved conduct" is general; 2:10 goes on to name "those who indulge the corrupt desires of the flesh and despise authority" among the ungodly being warned.
- **Jude 1:7.** "Sodom and Gomorrah and the cities around them, who indulged in sexual immorality and pursued strange flesh, are on display as an example of those who sustain the punishment of eternal fire." This is the one later reference in the BSB that names a sexual sin of Sodom's own. (Some English versions render the phrase "perversion" or "unnatural desire"; the BSB has "pursued strange flesh.") What "strange flesh" means is itself disputed (see Positions and the reviewed connection).
- **Revelation 11:8** calls Jerusalem "figuratively called Sodom and Egypt," naming no specific sin.

The data cut both ways, and both sides know it. A reader can see that most references name no sin at all; that Ezekiel's list leads with arrogance, excess, and neglect of the poor and then adds "abominations"; that the prophets use Sodom as a byword for a society's whole corruption; and that Jude names sexual sin. What weight each item carries is exactly what the positions below dispute.

## Literary Design

**Two hospitality scenes, side by side.** Chapter 18 opens with Abraham sitting "at the entrance of his tent," seeing three men, running to meet them, bowing, and offering water to "wash your feet" and bread (18:1-5); he has a calf prepared and stands by them "as they ate" (18:8). Chapter 19 opens with Lot "sitting in the gateway of the city," rising to meet two of them, bowing, and offering to let them "wash your feet and spend the night" before preparing "a feast" (19:1-3). The two openings can be checked against each other line by line. Then the scenes diverge: at Abraham's tent the visitors eat in peace; at Lot's house the city surrounds the door. Matthew Vines reads the pairing as the frame for the whole chapter (see Positions); Brian Neil Peterson argues the frame is wider than hospitality (also Positions). That the parallel opening exists is checkable; what it is for is part of what they dispute.

**Looking back, looking down.** The command to Lot's family is "Do not look back, and do not stop anywhere on the plain!" (19:17). "Lot's wife looked back, and she became a pillar of salt" (19:26). Two verses later Abraham "looked down toward Sodom and Gomorrah... and he saw the smoke rising from the land like smoke from a furnace" (19:28). The verse that forbids looking, the verse that records looking, and the verse where looking is the patriarch's witness to the judgment sit within eleven verses of each other. Luke 17:32's "Remember Lot's wife!" picks up the middle one (see the reviewed connection).

**Rescue by the hand.** The narrator states God's motive for the rescue twice, in two ways: "because of the LORD's compassion for them" (19:16) and "He remembered Abraham" (19:29). Neither verse credits Lot's own righteousness; 2 Peter 2:7-8 supplies that word from outside Genesis, as this curriculum's Genesis 13 lesson noted.

## Positions

Three questions are reported below. The first is the long-running dispute over what Sodom's sin was, reported through its named readings without a ruling. The second is Lot's offer of his daughters. The third is the history of the passage's use against gay people, which this lesson names in its own section, as this curriculum's Genesis 9 lesson did for the "Curse of Ham." Positions report what readers conclude; the Context section above reports what the text states.

**1. What was Sodom's sin?** Three families of reading are reported, each from its own named sources. The order is not a ranking.

- **A traditional Christian reading: the attempted same-sex act is central, alongside the violence and inhospitality.** Brian Neil Peterson, an Old Testament scholar writing in the *Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society* (2016), agrees that attempted gang rape and "the contravention of hospitality" are "part of the overall problem in Sodom," but argues that reading Genesis 19 "in light of Torah" -- especially the sexual laws of Leviticus 18 and 20 -- shows "the pervasive sin problem in Sodom: homosexuality, which in this case is perpetrated against strangers, infidelity in marriage, and a rejection of God's moral" standards. In a second article (*JETS*, 2018) he argues that Ezekiel 16:50's "abomination" is singular in Hebrew, that the singular corresponds to Leviticus 18:22 (BSB: "You must not lie with a man as with a woman; that is an abomination"), and that Ezekiel, a priest, was "drawing upon the Holiness Code of Leviticus... in order to highlight Sodom's main sin of homosexuality." Peterson reports Robert Gagnon (*The Bible and Homosexual Practice*, 2001) reaching the same conclusions on Ezekiel; Gagnon's own book was not read for this lesson. Melvin Otey (*Themelios* 49.1) adds a historical argument: that the sexual reading is not a medieval invention. He cites earlier Jewish texts that charge Sodom with fornication (Jubilees, the Testament of Benjamin), first-century Jewish writers who specify same-sex acts -- Josephus (the Sodomites "resolved themselves to enjoy these beautiful boys by force and violence") and Philo ("men ha[ving] intercourse with males") -- and Jude 7. His conclusion keeps the other elements in view: "it is better to acknowledge all of the prominent elements in the ancient narrative, including the attempt to transgress natural and supernatural boundaries, rape, and inhospitality, without dismissing the emphasis on divine condemnation of homosexuality." Among classic commentators, Matthew Poole on 19:5 cross-references Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13 and Romans 1:26-27, and John Gill on Ezekiel 16:50 reads "committed abomination" as "perhaps referring to that sin, which has its name from them." A confessional statement takes the same line: the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §2357 states that "Sacred Scripture... presents homosexual acts as acts of grave depravity," citing Genesis 19:1-29 first in its footnote (n. 140, with Romans 1:24-27, 1 Corinthians 6:10 and 1 Timothy 1:10). The same paragraph adds that such acts are "contrary to the natural law" and "Under no circumstances can they be approved." The next paragraph, §2358, calls the inclination itself "objectively disordered" and in the same paragraph says such persons "must be accepted with respect, compassion, and sensitivity. Every sign of unjust discrimination in their regard should be avoided."

- **A reading centered on violence, cruelty to strangers, and injustice.** On this reading, the night at Lot's door is an act of collective violence against vulnerable outsiders, and it is the symptom, not the whole, of a city whose "outcry" (18:20) had reached heaven. Its textual anchor is Ezekiel 16:49: "arrogant, overfed, and complacent; they did not help the poor and needy." Jewish tradition has long emphasized Sodom's injustice and cruelty, as the texts that follow show. The Babylonian Talmud (Sanhedrin 109a-b, read directly) teaches that "The people of Sodom became haughty and sinned due only to the excessive goodness" God gave them, and that they said, "Why do we need travelers, as they come only to divest us of our property? Come, let us cause the proper treatment of travelers to be forgotten from our land." It catalogues their crimes against the poor and the stranger: judges named for lying and forgery, a bed on which guests were cut or stretched to fit, a poor man given marked coins but refused bread "so that he could not spend the money and would die of hunger." It identifies the "outcry" of Genesis 18:20 with "a young woman who would take bread out to the poor people in a pitcher," and who was killed for it; "It is due to that sin that the fate of the people of Sodom was sealed." The Mishnah (Pirkei Avot 5:10) names a character type after the city: the one who says "mine is mine, and yours is yours": "this is a commonplace type; and some say this is a sodom-type of character." Genesis Rabbah 50:7 puts the two elements of the night together in the townspeople's own mouths: "Any guest who comes here, we will have sexual relations with them and take their possessions." Among modern scholars, Otey reports David M. Carr reading the episode as God's "condemnation of a town that violated hospitality by trying to rape guests in one of its homes" rather than a judgment on homosexual behavior.

- **Affirming readings: the text concerns sexual violence, not consensual relationships.** Matthew Vines (in his 2012 talk "The Gay Debate," read directly from his own transcript; his 2014 book *God and the Gay Christian* was not read) accepts that 19:5 is sexual -- "the men of Sodom threaten to gang rape Lot's angel visitors, who have come in the form of men, and so this behavior would at least ostensibly be same-sex" -- and argues that this "is the only connection that can be drawn between this passage and homosexuality in general, and there is a world of difference between violent and coercive practices like gang rape and consensual, monogamous, and loving relationships." He reads the threatened rape against the background of male-on-male rape as a means of humiliation, so that "The actions of the men of Sodom are intended to underscore their cruel treatment of outsiders," points to the hospitality contrast with chapter 18, and cites Ezekiel 16:49 and Jesus' words: "In Matthew 10 and Luke 10, Jesus associates the sin of Sodom with inhospitable treatment of his disciples." He addresses Jude 7 directly: "even if Jude 7 is taken as specifically referring to the threatened gang rape from Genesis 19:5, that still has nothing to do with the kinds of relationships that we're talking about." Peterson cites James Brownson (*Bible, Gender, Sexuality*, 2013, p. 268) and Ken Wilson among affirming interpreters who read the account as attempted gang rape and a breach of hospitality; Brownson's own pages were not read for this lesson. Otey quotes the theologian Shannon Craigo-Snell's version of the historical claim: "when some Christians in the medieval period were convinced that same sex relations were wrong, they reinterpreted the story of Sodom and Gomorrah in line with their beliefs." A reader should know the sourcing is uneven: the traditional Christian reading is represented here by two peer-reviewed articles and a Catechism read directly, while the affirming reading is represented by one popular talk read directly and otherwise through its critics' quotations (see Sources and Limits).

- **The traditional reply to the affirming readings.** Peterson's 2016 article is written as that reply, naming Vines and Brownson in its first footnote. He answers on three points. First, context: "Long before the men of Sodom failed to offer hospitality to the angels/men, the reader is told twice by the author that the 'men'... of the city of Sodom were exceedingly wicked and sinners (13:13), and that the cry of its people had come up before YHWH (18:20)." Second, Ezekiel: the singular "abomination" in 16:50 is distinct from the plural "abominations" of 16:51, so "it is not a matter of pitting inhospitality against homosexual activity; it is, as Davidson puts it, not 'either-or' but 'both-and'" (quoting Richard Davidson, *Flame of Yahweh*, 2007), adding: "Put simply, at the heart of Sodom's actions was 'human arrogance in relation to God.'" Third, the rest of the chapter: he argues that 19:15-38, including the incest of Lot's daughters, "sheds just as much light on the sin of Sodom as do the attempted rape of the angels and the refusal of hospitality." Otey's reply is historical: against Boswell's and Craigo-Snell's claim that the sexual reading is late, he answers that "Second Temple literature demonstrates that the view predates the Christian church." Vines's own talk had said that the destruction "was not originally thought to have anything to do with sexuality at all, even if there is a sexual component to the passage we just read," and that the sexual reading took hold "starting in the Middle Ages"; that historical claim is the one Otey's article disputes.

Each reading has to answer something. The traditional Christian reading has to account for the fact that, of the 27 verses outside Genesis that name Sodom, only Jude 7 names a sexual sin of Sodom's own in so many words (Ezekiel's "abomination" and 2 Peter's "depraved conduct" are themselves disputed), and that Ezekiel's explicit list ("Now this was the iniquity...") leads with arrogance, excess and neglect of the poor; it answers with the singular "abomination," with Jude 7, and with early Jewish readers. The violence-and-injustice reading has to account for Jude 7's "sexual immorality" and for the sexual reading's presence in Jewish sources on 19:5 -- Rashi glosses the demand "with homosexuality," in the Sefaria translation, citing Genesis Rabbah 50:5, which reads it as a demand "for sexual relations"; the reading answers that the night's sexual demand was itself the violence. The affirming readings have to account for the same evidence and, further, for the claim that a text about forced sex has no bearing on consensual relationships; they answer that the absence of consent is the very thing the narrative depicts. Two further disputes run underneath all three. One is what Jude's "strange flesh" means: Poole reads it as "male flesh"; the *Expositor's Greek Testament* as "departure from the natural use"; Jeremy Hultin, whom Otey quotes in support, as the men of Sodom seeking "sexual relations with the angels," parallel to the angels of Jude 1:6; Otey adds that "Since they do not know the visitors are angels rather than mere men, their intentions, at least, are homoerotic." The other is what Ezekiel's singular "abomination" refers to. Peterson ties it to Leviticus 18:22 and lists Ezekiel's singular uses as 16:50, 18:12, 22:11 and 33:26, arguing they occur "when sexual sins are in view" (BSB 22:11: "One man commits an abomination with his neighbor's wife"; 18:12, which the BSB renders as a plural, sits in a list that begins "He oppresses the poor and needy"). He anticipates the opposing view -- "Some may argue" that the word is "merely an encapsulating term for all the listed sins that come before it" -- and answers it from the plural in 16:51. A reader can check each of the four verses in context.

This lesson reports these readings and does not resolve them. Above all, it does not decide the question many readers bring to this chapter -- whether same-sex relationships are right or wrong -- which turns on many other texts and on each tradition's whole reading of Scripture, not on Genesis 19 alone, and which the sources above answer differently. A reader working this out for their own life, or for someone they love, needs a pastor or counselor who knows them, not a study note.

**2. Lot's offer of his daughters (19:8).** "Look, I have two daughters who have never slept with a man. Let me bring them to you, and you can do to them as you please." The text records the offer and does not comment on it. It is not presented in Genesis as an example to follow, and this lesson does not present it as one. Named readers divide over how to account for it, not over whether it was wrong.

- **Condemned, with or without extenuation.** Keil and Delitzsch: "In his anxiety, Lot was willing to sacrifice to the sanctity of hospitality his duty as a father, which ought to have been still more sacred, 'and committed the sin of seeking to avert sin by sin,'" adding that "the offer was a grievous violation of his paternal duty." Benson: "This was unadvisedly and unjustifiably offered, probably through the great discomposure and perturbation which his mind was in. It is true, of two evils we must choose the less, but of two sins we must choose neither." Gill: "this was a very great evil in Lot... contrary to parental love and affection," while allowing that "nothing can be said to excuse this good man, but the hurry of spirit, and confusion of mind that he was in." Matthew Poole calls it "A most imprudent and sinful motion, whereby he yielded to one sin to prevent another," while allowing "some extenuation" from Lot's "great charity and kindness to strangers" and "perturbation" of mind.
- **Explained by ancient custom.** The Cambridge Bible: "Lot's proposal, so atrocious in our ears, may have been deemed meritorious in an Eastern country, where no sacrifice was considered too great to maintain inviolate the safety of a stranger who had been received in hospitality," and it attributes Lot's choice "not so much to the weakness of the man as to the terribly low estimate of womanhood which prevailed at that time." Ellicott reports two church fathers taking a similar line -- "Granting with St. Ambrose that it was the substitution of a smaller for a greater sin, and with St. Chrysostom that Lot was bound by the laws of hospitality to do his utmost to protect his guests" -- and then rejects the excuse: "yet he was also bound as a father equally to protect his daughters to the last extremity." The Geneva Bible's note holds both halves: "He deserves praise for defending his guests, but he is to be blamed for seeking unlawful means."

The daughters are not consulted, are not named, and say nothing in 19:1-29. The crowd refuses the offer (19:9), and the visitors' intervention (19:10-11) is what prevents harm. Judges 19:24-25, the parallel at Gibeah, shows the same offer made and the harm then done (see the reviewed connection). These are things the text records; the lesson draws no lesson from Lot's conduct here.

*If this part of the chapter is close to your own experience.* The chapter describes sexual violence threatened against guests and offered against daughters. **3. The passage's use against gay people.** This section is not one more reading to weigh against those in Position 1. It names a history, and states what the sources on every side read for this lesson say about it. Reporting any reading above is not endorsing any such use.

- **From the city's name to a crime punishable by death.** In 559 the Emperor Justinian issued a law (Novel 141, read directly in S. P. Scott's translation) against "the corruption of males," grounding it in Genesis 19: "We know, from the study of the Holy Scriptures, that God, in order to punish such persons, visited His wrath upon those who formerly inhabited the City of Sodom, and caused its territory to be consumed, even to this day, by an inextinguishable fire." An earlier law (Novel 77), which grouped such acts with blasphemy and swearing by God's head, blamed "famine, earthquake, and pestilence" on "crimes of this description" and ordered the city prefect to arrest those who persisted "and inflict upon them the punishment of death." The city's name passed into the law of Christian Europe as "sodomy." The United States Supreme Court's opinion in *Lawrence v. Texas* (2003) traces American sodomy laws to "the English criminal laws passed in the first instance by the Reformation Parliament of 1533." In *Bowers v. Hardwick* (1986), which upheld a Georgia statute authorizing up to 20 years' imprisonment, Chief Justice Burger's concurrence wrote that condemnation of such conduct "is firmly rooted in" Judeo-Christian "moral and ethical standards," cited Roman capital law, and repeated Blackstone's language of disgust; Burger did not cite Genesis, but the legal category he defended bore the city's name. *Lawrence* reversed that decision: "Bowers was not correct when it was decided, and it is not correct today... Bowers v. Hardwick should be and now is overruled." The same opinion found the historical grounds relied on in *Bowers* "more complex than the majority opinion and the concurring opinion by Chief Justice Burger indicate," and found that early American sodomy laws were not "directed at homosexual conduct as a distinct matter" and that "It was not until the 1970's that any State singled out same-sex relations for criminal prosecution."
- **What the sources on each side say about that use.** The affirming side names it as harm: Craigo-Snell, quoted by Otey, says such readings "have caused concrete harm to God's beloved LGBTQ children." The traditional side's own confessional text, quoted in Position 1, rejects "unjust discrimination" in its own words: "Every sign of unjust discrimination in their regard should be avoided" (CCC §2358). Justinian's law rested on the reading that Sodom was destroyed for same-sex acts; holding that reading does not entail endorsing such laws, and none of the modern authors quoted in Position 1 argues for them in the texts read for this lesson.
- **What the text itself does and does not say.** Genesis 19 narrates a judgment of God on a city; it gives no instruction to any human court, ruler, or crowd to punish anyone. That is a checkable fact about the narrative, not a ruling on the dispute in Position 1.

This lesson does not caricature the traditional Christian reading as inherently hateful: its sources state their own reasons, from Scripture and, in the Catechism's case, from natural law as well, and the Catechism's next paragraph after the one that cites Genesis 19 (§2358) calls for respect. Nor does it let the history above stand unremarked: the passage was used to justify death sentences and imprisonment, and that use is named here so that a reader can see it plainly. A reader who has been hurt by how this passage was used against them or someone they love deserves to be heard by a pastor or counselor who knows them, not handed a study note.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from the passage in its own setting to a modern situation. It models the shape; it is not an answer key, and the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate step. It deliberately does not use 19:4-9.

**Passage element.** Genesis 19:12-17. The visitors warn Lot that "we are about to destroy this place" (19:13). Lot passes the warning to his sons-in-law, who "thought he was joking" (19:14). At dawn, "when Lot hesitated," the visitors take the family by the hand and lead them out (19:16), and the command is "Run for your lives! Do not look back" (19:17).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text records three different responses to the same warning -- dismissal (the sons-in-law), hesitation (Lot), and looking back (Lot's wife) -- and it gives the warning itself in plain, urgent words. Inference: for hearers who knew how the story ended, the account would have made the cost of delay vivid without stating a moral; the text states God's compassion as the reason for the rescue (19:16), not Lot's promptness.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** A credible warning of real danger calls for action, not debate; people who love us may hesitate or dismiss it, and sometimes the faithful response is to take someone by the hand.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a real and imminent danger, a clear warning, and the human tendency to dismiss or delay. Not carried forward: the divine judgment in the story, the identity of the messengers, and any suggestion that people who suffer in a modern disaster are being judged.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed; the rescue in 19:16 and 19:29 is told of Lot and is not treated here as a promise that anyone who heeds a warning will be spared.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family. An official evacuation order is issued ahead of a wildfire. An elderly parent, who has lived in the house for forty years, says the warnings are always exaggerated and wants to wait and see.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: protective action. One faithful response is for an adult child to take the warning seriously, make the plan concrete (where to go, what to bring, who will drive), and stay with the parent until they leave -- persistence, not argument.

**Cautions.** In a real emergency, follow the instructions of local authorities; this bridge is not safety guidance. Persistence is not coercion: an adult's decisions about their own life and home deserve respect, and where someone's safety or capacity is in real doubt, emergency services or a qualified professional is the right next step. This bridge does not read natural disasters as judgments on the people they strike. The passage is not a disaster-planning text; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing and may find does not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Genesis itself names Sodom's sin only as an "outcry" and a sin "so grievous" (18:20), and the Talmud fills in that outcry with a young woman killed for feeding the poor. Before reading any later text, what in 19:1-11 alone would you point to as evidence of what the outcry was about?
2. Of the 27 verses outside Genesis that name Sodom, most name no sin of Sodom's own, Ezekiel 16:49-50 names several, and Jude 1:7 names sexual immorality. How should a reader weigh many references that name nothing against a few that name something -- and does the answer change depending on which reading in Position 1 you start from?
3. Ezekiel 16:50 has a singular "abomination" in Hebrew where the BSB has a plural. Peterson argues the singular points to Leviticus 18:22; others read it as a summary of the sins just listed in 16:49. What would you need to see to be confident what the singular refers to?
4. Lot is called "righteous" in 2 Peter 2:7-8, yet offers his daughters to a mob (19:8) and hesitates when told to flee (19:16). Genesis 19 itself gives "the LORD's compassion" and "He remembered Abraham" as the reasons he was rescued. What does the narrator's choice of reasons suggest to you about how Genesis wants Lot to be seen?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate Genesis 19:1-29 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) State in the text's own words what the men of Sodom demand (19:5), what Lot offers (19:8), and what reason Genesis gives for Lot's rescue (19:16, 19:29), before saying what any tradition concludes. (c) Name one place elsewhere in Scripture where this chapter's people, places or images visibly reappear, and say what kind of connection it is.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the plain sequence, and where the chapter sits after Abraham's bargaining in 18:16-33 (Context); (1:00-2:00) what 19:4-9 says and does not say, including the verb behind "have relations with" (Context); (2:00-3:00) the whole-canon list of references to Sodom and what each names (Context); (3:00-4:00) the three named readings of Sodom's sin, the traditional reply to the affirming readings, and the line this lesson does not cross (Positions 1), with the history of misuse named plainly (Positions 3); (4:00-4:30) where the chapter travels later in Scripture, naming each connection's evidence label (prompt 5); (4:30-5:00) the boundary, stated last (prompt 4).
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then answer it. Form your own first. If you are stuck, here is one: by setting the traditional reading beside affirming readings at equal length, the lesson treats a minority modern interpretation as if it had the same standing as the reading held by most of the church for most of its history, and by giving the history of legal misuse its own section, it implies the traditional reading is responsible for that history. A second candidate, running the other direction: by reporting the traditional reading's arguments in detail, alongside a law that put people to death, the lesson gives a reading many gay readers experience as condemnation the same calm treatment as a scholarly footnote. Ask whether a study note that reports positions without ruling can avoid both failures, and how.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) Genesis 19 does not, in its own words, say which element of the night at Lot's door is the reason for the destruction; it gives the reason as an earlier "outcry" (18:20; 19:13) whose content it does not state. Every specific account of Sodom's sin draws on later texts or tradition. (b) Genesis 19 narrates a judgment by God; it gives no instruction to any human authority to punish anyone. Whether its account bears on consensual relationships is what the readings in Position 1 dispute, and those who decide it do so from a wider reading of Scripture, not from this chapter alone.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend it: name its type, give its evidence label, and point to the words in both texts that support the grade. The reviewed connections attached to this lesson are:
