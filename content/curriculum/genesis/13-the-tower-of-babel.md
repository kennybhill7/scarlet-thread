Genesis 11:1-9 narrates a short, complete story: a unified humanity, speaking one language, settles on a plain in Shinar and sets out to build "a city with a tower that reaches to the heavens" (11:4); God comes down, confuses their language, and scatters them "over the face of all the earth" (11:9). Nine verses carry a surprising amount of real, checkable content -- the builders' own stated twin motives ("make a name for ourselves" and avoid being "scattered," both in 11:4); a specific, distinctive building technique the text itself flags as unusual (fired brick and tar instead of stone and mortar, 11:3); a Hebrew wordplay on the place-name itself (11:9); and a genuinely disputed question about what, exactly, God's own words in 11:6-7 say He is responding to. It also sits next to a real textual puzzle this lesson does not invent: Genesis 10:5, 10:20, and 10:31 each describe the sons of Japheth, Ham, and Shem already divided "according to their languages" -- language, plural, already a fact about the nations -- in the chapter that comes immediately before this one's own account of when the nations' one language was first confused. Every quotation attributed to the BSB below, including in both of this lesson's connection files, was printed directly from this app's own shipped corpus (`web/public/bible/BSB/1.json` for Genesis, `36.json` for Zephaniah, `44.json` for Acts) by a Node script before any of it was drafted, and a second automated pass confirmed every quoted fragment is a literal substring of the real verse text -- see Sources and Limits for the exact method and result.

## Context

**What happens, in order (11:1-9).** "The whole world had one language and a common form of speech" (11:1). As people journey eastward, they find a plain in the land of Shinar and settle there (11:2). They say to each other, "Come, let us make bricks and bake them thoroughly" -- using brick instead of stone, and tar instead of mortar (11:3). They propose building "a city with a tower that reaches to the heavens," stating their own reasons: "that we may make a name for ourselves and not be scattered over the face of all the earth" (11:4). The LORD comes down to see the city and tower (11:5) and states His own reasoning: "If they have begun to do this as one people speaking the same language, then nothing they devise will be beyond them" (11:6). He proposes, "Come, let Us go down and confuse their language, so that they will not understand one another's speech" (11:7). The LORD scatters them over the face of all the earth, and they stop building the city (11:8). The unit closes with the place-name etymology: "it is called Babel, for there the LORD confused the language of the whole world, and from that place the LORD scattered them over the face of all the earth" (11:9). This is a plain retelling of what happens, in the text's own order; what each piece means is the rest of this lesson's work.

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens (`content/lens/eleven-stages.json`) names stage 4 "Genesis 10-11 -- The Nations and the Tower at Babel," with a machine-readable `chapters` field of `["1.10"]`, pairing this stage with Revelation 1-18 ("Babylon and the Beast") and carrying this exact note in its own summary field: "at Pentecost (Acts-Jude -- The Early Church) the language division is pictured in reverse." That note is this app's own lens, not an external source, but it names the same Acts 2 connection this lesson's own research independently located and checks below (Positions and the reviewed connection to Acts 2). This lesson's own passage boundary is 11:1-9, the unit Genesis 10's Table of Nations leads into; a companion unit covering Genesis 10 itself sits immediately before this one and is not this lesson's own territory.

**A note on this lesson's neighbor.** Genesis 10:1-32 traces Noah's sons' descendants into named nations, and three separate verses inside that chapter -- 10:5 ("according to their languages"), 10:20 ("according to their clans, languages, lands, and nations"), and 10:31 (the identical formula for Shem's line) -- state plainly that these nations are already divided by language. This lesson's own passage, which follows immediately after in the text's own order, narrates the event that first produces that division (11:1, "the whole world had one language," confused at 11:7-9). This is a real, checkable textual feature -- not an assumption brought to the text from outside it -- and Positions (3) below reports named, non-adjudicated explanations for it rather than resolving it.

**Key words, read as observations.**

- **מִגְדָּל** (*migdal*, "tower," 11:4-5) is an ordinary Hebrew word used elsewhere mainly for military watchtowers; it is not a specialized term for the kind of stepped Mesopotamian temple-tower called a ziggurat, because, as John H. Walton's direct study of the ziggurat's Mesopotamian background argues, "the Israelites did not have ziggurats" and so had no ready specialized term to borrow or coin one from. That the text uses a generic word rather than a technical one is independently checkable from the word's own range of use elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible; what, if anything, follows from that for identifying the structure the text has in mind is part of the dispute in Positions (1) below.
- **שֵׁם** (*shem*, "name," 11:4) is the noun behind "that we may make a name for ourselves" -- the builders' own stated first reason for building, independently checkable as their own direct speech rather than the narrator's description of their motive. The aggregated classic commentaries on 11:4 read this phrase consistently, if with different emphasis: Keil and Delitzsch state "the real motive therefore was the desire for renown"; Gill's Exposition says they intended "to spread their fame, and perpetuate their name to the latest posterity"; Matthew Poole states they acted "merely out of pride and vain-glory"; and Ellicott reads a harder edge, that building together gave them "a distinctly ambitious object, for by remaining as one nation they would be able to reduce to obedience" other groups. What these five sources agree on, independently checkable by comparing their own wording side by side, is that none of them reads "make a name for ourselves" as a neutral statement; the question this lesson does not resolve is exactly what about that self-naming project God's own stated response in 11:6-7 is answering (Positions 2).
- **בָּלַל / בָּבֶל** (*balal*, "to confuse, to mix," and *Bavel*, "Babel") form the wordplay the text names explicitly at 11:9: "it is called Babel, for there the LORD confused [*balal*] the language." This consonantal pun is independently checkable by comparing the two Hebrew words side by side. It is also, on the available evidence, not the city's own real etymology: the native Akkadian name was *Bab-ilim*, "gate of god(s)" -- a different root entirely -- and the Hebrew wordplay is a deliberate, meaning-bearing association the text makes with the place-name, not a report of its historical derivation. That the narrator uses a sound-based pun rather than the real Akkadian etymology is itself worth sitting with as a Question to Carry, below, rather than something this lesson resolves for the reader.
- **בְּנֵי הָאָדָם** ("the sons of men," 11:5) describes who is building, in the LORD's own observation as the text reports it -- an ordinary phrase for humankind in general, not a title or a named group; nothing in the word itself narrows the builders to any particular nation beyond what 11:1-4 has already stated (that they are, at this point in the story, one undivided people).

**A real, checkable building-technique detail (11:3), with a directly-read Mesopotamian-background source.** The text states the builders used "brick instead of stone, and tar instead of mortar" -- a detail John H. Walton's 1995 study (read directly, in full, for this lesson, not through a secondary report) argues reflects a genuine and checkable distinction between Israelite/Palestinian and Mesopotamian building practice: "In Palestine, mud bricks (sun-dried) are first found in levels designated pre-pottery Neolithic A... Kiln-fired brick is unattested. The practice was rather to use stone for the foundations and sun-dried brick for the superstructure," while in Mesopotamia, "kiln-fired bricks are first noted during the late Uruk period," with "bitumen... the usual mortar used with kiln-fired bricks" -- an expensive combination "normally used only for palaces, temples, and other official buildings." Walton reads Genesis 11:3's own second half -- "So they used brick instead of stone, and tar instead of mortar" -- as the narrator's own explanatory aside "to those who might be unaware of the details of this 'foreign' practice," which matches an Israelite audience for whom stone-and-mud construction was the ordinary technique. This is a directly-read, checkable piece of ancient Near Eastern background, not a secondary report of what Walton argues; what it is evidence for -- a ziggurat specifically, or simply an expensive public building project in the Mesopotamian style generally -- is part of Positions (1) below.

## Literary Design

**A proposed symmetrical (chiastic) structure, reported with its own hedge.** A page on ChiasmusXChange proposes reading 11:1-9 as a nine-part symmetrical structure turning on 11:5 (the LORD coming down to see) as its center: the one-language opening of 11:1 is paired with the language confused "of all the earth" at 11:9; the people's journey and settlement in Shinar (11:2) is paired with their building stopping (11:8); the builders' own proposal (11:3-4) is paired with the LORD's own proposal to go down and confuse their language (11:7); and 11:6 (God's own stated reasoning about their unity and power) is paired, as the structure's near-center counterpart, with 11:5 itself. (The page's own verse labels use the King James Version's wording for its outline, not this app's BSB text; this lesson restates the same structural claim in its own words rather than quoting that page's KJV phrasing as if it were this app's own Bible text, which it is not.) The page itself does not present this as a settled, uncontested reading -- its own comment thread notes a similarly shaped proposal in E. W. Bullinger's *Companion Bible* with a different verse-by-verse assignment -- and this lesson reports it with the same caution this curriculum's Genesis 3 lesson review already required for chiasm claims generally: a real, checkable textual symmetry (the repeated "over the face of all the earth," the twin "Come, let" speeches, the "whole world" language of both 11:1 and 11:9) is not by itself proof that the author built it on purpose as opposed to a reader's flexible labeling finding a pattern that fits.

**A twin "Come, let us" address, independently checkable without relying on the chiasm proposal above.** 11:3-4 record the builders' own words, "Come, let us make bricks... Come, let us build," and 11:7 records the LORD's own words, "Come, let Us go down and confuse their language" -- the identical opening formula ("Come, let [us/Us]...") used once for the human plan and once for the divine response to it. This recurrence is directly checkable by comparing the two speeches side by side; whether it is the narrator's deliberate mirroring of the human proposal in the divine response, or simply how two plural-subject proposals in the same short unit will tend to be worded, is not settled by the recurrence alone. A reader who already knows this curriculum's earlier Genesis units may also notice that "Let Us" (the same first-person-plural divine self-reference) occurs at 1:26 ("Let Us make man in Our image") and again at 3:22 ("the man has become like one of Us"); whether 11:7's own use of the same formula is doing anything with that earlier pattern, or is simply the ordinary way this text represents God speaking of Himself, is left open here rather than built into a claim this lesson has not sourced to a named scholar.

**An inclusio on "the face of all the earth."** The builders state their own fear in 11:4 -- "not be scattered over the face of all the earth" -- and the narrator reports the outcome in 11:8-9 using close variants of the identical phrase twice: "the LORD scattered them from there over the face of all the earth" (11:8) and again at the chapter's close, "from that place the LORD scattered them over the face of all the earth" (11:9). The thing the builders named as the one outcome they were building to prevent is, in the text's own repeated wording, exactly what happens -- a real, checkable feature of the passage's own vocabulary, not an interpretive gloss added to it.

## Positions

Three separate questions. Each is reported through its own named positions, from their own stated reasoning; none is adjudicated, and no position's statement here should be read as this lesson's own conclusion.

**1. Can the tower of 11:1-9 be identified with a specific historical structure -- most often proposed as the Etemenanki ziggurat of Babylon -- or is that identification better read as a later, defensible-but-unprovable association rather than a settled fact?** This is a real, live dispute among the sources consulted for this lesson, not a question with one side simply uninformed.

- **A widely held identification with Etemenanki, reported with its own named limits.** Wikipedia's survey article states, "According to modern scholars, the biblical story of the Tower of Babel was likely influenced by Etemenanki," citing Stephen L. Harris as proposing the association arose during the Babylonian captivity and Isaac Asimov as speculating the account's authors "were inspired by the existence of an apparently incomplete ziggurat at Babylon." The same article states this is not a unique or uncontested identification: "Other proposals in the past have been the ziggurats of Borsippa, Dur-Kurigalzu, Dur-Sharrukin, or the Tower of Esagil" -- a real, named spread of candidate structures, not a single settled referent.
- **A historical-background argument that a literal, single-site identification with the archaeologically known Etemenanki is likely anachronistic.** John H. Walton's 1995 study (read directly in full for this lesson) argues the narrative's own details -- the building materials, the pattern of early Mesopotamian urbanization, the origin of the ziggurat as an architectural form -- place the kind of project described "perhaps centuries prior to the actual development of urbanization as attested by archaeological records," tentatively locating it against "the latter part of the fourth millennium" or earlier. Walton separately notes that excavation at the historical city of Babylon itself "cannot inform us of its history prior to the second millennium, because the shifting water table of the Euphrates has obliterated the strata," and that "historical records do not mention Babylon prior to meager references in the Ur III period" -- centuries after the span Walton's own argument assigns to the events of Genesis 11. On Walton's own reading, the text's naming of the place "Babel" at 11:9 functions rhetorically, identifying "the contemporary example of what was wrought in that initial incident" rather than asserting that the specific tower built in Genesis 11 is the same physical structure later excavated or described as Etemenanki, which is itself a Neo-Babylonian-era structure in its best-known form.
- **A linguistic objection to the ziggurat identification generally, answered by Walton on three stated grounds.** Walton's own article reports and answers an objection "frequently" raised against identifying the *migdal* of 11:4-5 as a ziggurat at all: that the Hebrew word "is used primarily in military contexts or as a watch tower, but never used of a ziggurat." Walton's answer, stated as his own three-part argument rather than a report of someone else's reply, is that Israelites had no ziggurats of their own, had no occasion to develop a specialized term for one, and would naturally extend an existing word ("tower") to a foreign structure they had no better word for -- which does not, on Walton's own framing, settle whether this specific text has a ziggurat in view at all, only that the objection from vocabulary alone does not rule it out.

This lesson does not decide between a specific Etemenanki identification and a more general or representative reading; each position above answers a real question the others leave open (why the text names "Babel" specifically, whether the *migdal* must be a ziggurat at all, and whether the narrative's own details fit a much earlier period than the archaeologically attested Etemenanki).

**2. What does God's own speech in 11:6-7 state He is responding to -- and does the popular one-word summary "pride" match what the text itself says?** The text's own words at 11:6 are: "If they have begun to do this as one people speaking the same language, then nothing they devise will be beyond them." Named readings differ on what to make of that sentence.

- **A traditional reading centered on pride and defiance of the command to fill the earth.** The aggregated classic commentaries on 11:4 read the builders' project in exactly these terms: Jamieson, Fausset, and Brown call the attempt "foolish, wicked, and justly offensive" as a scheme "to defeat the counsels of heaven by attempting to prevent emigration"; Matthew Poole states the builders acted "merely out of pride and vain-glory" to "erect an everlasting monument." On this reading, 11:6's "nothing they devise will be beyond them" names the scale of the threat pride and self-sufficient unity pose, consistent with the builders' own stated "make a name for ourselves."
- **A reading that locates the specific danger in what the building project represented theologically, not in architecture, urbanization, or ambition as such.** Walton's own study, after a lengthy argument that the ziggurat in Mesopotamian religious practice represented humanity "trying to bring deity down to the level of man," states his own conclusion directly: "The danger of the action of the builders then has nothing to do with architecture or with urbanization. Nothing was wrong with towers or with cities. The danger is found in what this building project stood for in the minds of the builders." On this reading, 11:6's "nothing they devise will be beyond them" is read together with what immediately follows (the rhetorical naming of the place as "Babel" at 11:9, which Walton reads as the narrator pointing forward to "the offense" the Babylonians themselves would later commit, not an offense already complete in the builders' own stated intentions) rather than as a verdict on the builders' hearts delivered at 11:6 itself.
- **A third, more cautious reading that treats 11:6 as stating a concern about unchecked collective capability, independent of either account of its moral cause.** Read on its own, apart from either tradition above, 11:6's clause names a capability ("nothing they devise will be beyond them") as the thing the LORD responds to, not a stated sin-word; this is a directly checkable feature of the verse's own grammar -- it names a consequence of unified language and purpose, not an accusation. This lesson does not attribute this narrower reading to a named scholar consulted for this lesson; it is flagged here as an observation about what the verse's own words do and do not say, to be weighed against the two sourced positions above, not as a third sourced position with equal standing to them.

This lesson does not rank the first two positions, and flags the third as the lesson's own unsourced observation rather than a position reported from a named source.

**3. Genesis 10:5, 10:20, and 10:31 each describe the nations already divided "according to their languages," apparently before this chapter's own account of when language was first confused (11:1, 11:7-9). How is that order best explained?** This is a real textual feature, checked directly against this app's own shipped BSB text of both chapters for this lesson, not an assumption imported from outside the text. Named, non-exclusive explanations are in print.

- **A harmonizing reading: Genesis 10 is organized by descent, not by strict chronological sequence, and describes an end state reached only after Babel.** Don Stewart's survey states the point directly: "the events are not listed in chronological order. Genesis tells of the disbursement of the different language groups throughout the world. It is a broad outline of where the various language groups settled. Genesis 11, on the other hand, explains how these different languages came about -- through the events at the tower of Babel." On this reading, 10:5, 10:20, and 10:31's "according to their languages" describe the nations as they stood once the nations existed as described -- which, in the text's own larger story, is after Babel, even though the chapter recording it precedes Genesis 11 in the book's own order. This reading can point to 10:25's own wording for support: "Two sons were born to Eber: One was named Peleg, because in his days the earth was divided" -- a notice inside Genesis 10 itself that something called "division" happens within the genealogy's own timeframe, read on this view as a forward-pointing reference to the very event Genesis 11 goes on to narrate.
- **A documentary-hypothesis explanation: Genesis 10 interweaves two originally separate sources with two different accounts of how the nations came to be divided.** John Day's academic treatment (read directly for this lesson) states that Genesis 10 "is clearly composite, combining material from the P and J sources that sometimes overlaps," distinguished by consistent formulas ("the descendants of A" for P, "A begot B" for J), and that the two sources differ in exactly the relevant respect: "For P, the spread of humanity all over the earth, leading to the formation of different nations, constituted a gradual process following the flood. In contrast, J attributed the spread of humanity over the earth to God's scattering human beings following the building of the tower and city of Babel (Gen 11:8-9)." On this reading, 10:5, 10:20, and 10:31's "according to their languages" belongs to P's own account of a gradual post-flood differentiation that does not require, or know of, a single Babel-confusion event at all; the apparent tension is a seam between two source-traditions preserved side by side in the final text, not one author contradicting himself.
- **A minimal, text-internal observation that does not adjudicate between the two explanations above.** Whatever its ultimate cause, 10:32's own closing summary -- "From these the nations of the earth spread out after the flood" -- and 10:25's Peleg notice both read as the chapter's own forward-looking or summarizing statements rather than a strict verse-by-verse chronology; a table of descent naming a later state of affairs before the chapter that narrates how that state came about is a recognized literary move elsewhere in narrative generally, independent of which source-critical or harmonizing account a reader finds persuasive.

This lesson does not decide among these explanations. Each addresses a real feature of the text (10:25's Peleg notice, the P/J stylistic markers Day identifies, and Genesis 10's own position in the book's final order) that the others do not fully account for on their own.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key: the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step, and a different passage element, situation, or response would be equally legitimate.

**Passage element.** Genesis 11:3-4. The builders state their own twin reasons for the project in their own words: "that we may make a name for ourselves and not be scattered over the face of all the earth."

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text gives the builders' motive in their own direct speech, not the narrator's summary of it, and that speech names two linked goals -- reputation ("a name for ourselves") and self-protection against dispersion ("not be scattered") -- without any stated reference to God, a command, or a larger purpose beyond the group itself. Inference: for an audience familiar with the surrounding chapters' own repeated command to "fill the earth" (9:1, 9:7, echoing 1:28, as this curriculum's Genesis 9:1-17 lesson already traced), hearing a project whose own stated purpose is explicitly to avoid the very spreading-out that command names would likely register as the group's own purpose substituted for a purpose beyond the group, independent of whatever specific theological reading of 11:6-7 a hearer ultimately held (Positions 2). The Context and Literary Design sections above supply what the text itself gives; nothing in them lets this lesson say more than that about what first hearers took from the builders' own stated reasons.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** A group's stated reasons for a shared undertaking -- building something, staying together, protecting a name or a position -- can be entirely truthful about what the group actually wants, and still name no purpose beyond the group's own continuation and reputation; noticing when a project's own stated "why" stops at the group itself is a different, and prior, question from whether the project succeeds or is skillfully executed.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a group states its own real reason for a shared undertaking; that reason names self-continuation and reputation and nothing beyond them; a person assessing the undertaking has to decide what to make of that, apart from how well the undertaking itself is carried out. Not carried forward: the specific theological content of 11:6-7 (whatever a reader concludes about what it identifies as the actual danger, Positions 2), the scale of the ancient building project, and any claim that every instance of organizational self-preservation or reputation-building is the same kind of thing this passage narrates.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed; nothing in 11:3-4 is treated here as a promise that every self-protective or reputation-seeking group project will be confused or scattered the way this one specific narrated project was.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. A department inside a larger organization has, over several years, built its own specialized internal process, vocabulary, and tooling that works well for its own members but is increasingly hard for other departments to understand or interact with. When leadership proposes folding the department's functions into a cross-functional structure, the department's own stated objection is that the change will "break what we've built" and "scatter" people who have worked well together for years.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: examining a group's own stated reasons for resisting change, where the reasons given (continuity, cohesion, protecting what works) are genuinely true and not merely a cover story, but may still stop short of naming the organization's own larger purpose. One faithful response is for someone in the department, or a leader over it, to ask honestly whether "what we've built" and "not being scattered" serve the organization's own stated mission or have become ends in themselves -- without assuming the answer in advance, and without treating every instance of a team wanting to stay together as the same thing this passage narrates.

**Cautions.** This example does not say every organizational structure, specialized process, or desire for team continuity is a modern tower of Babel; most such things are ordinary and good. It should not be used to dismiss legitimate concerns about disruptive change as if raising them were itself the offense -- the passage's own content is about a project's stated purpose, not about change-resistance as such. The passage is not an organizational-change text; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing, and the learner may find it does not hold.

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

(b) State, in the text's own words, what the builders say their own reasons are (11:4) and what the LORD says His own reasoning is (11:6-7), before saying what any tradition concludes about either statement. (c) Name one place elsewhere in Scripture where this passage's language or imagery (one language versus many, scattering, a city and a tower, the wordplay on a place-name) visibly reappears, and say what kind of connection it is.

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** Outline how you would teach this passage in five minutes, with at least one point in each segment you could cite to a source or a verse. A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the plain sequence of 11:1-9 and where it sits at the start of stage 4, immediately after a companion unit on Genesis 10 (see Context); (1:00-2:00) the builders' own stated motive, the brick-and-tar construction detail, and the Babel/*balal* wordplay, each independently checkable (see Key Words); (2:00-3:00) the real, named dispute over identifying the tower with Etemenanki, and over what 11:6-7 itself says God is responding to, held open on purpose (see Positions 1-2); (3:00-3:45) the real chronological puzzle Genesis 10 creates for this chapter, and the two named, non-adjudicated explanations for it (see Positions 3); (3:45-4:30) where this passage's own language travels later in Scripture, naming the evidence label of each reviewed connection, not just listing it (see the table in prompt 5); (4:30-5:00) the boundary, stated on purpose as the last thing taught (prompt 4).

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first; the discipline of this prompt is that you do the forming, not that you pick from a list. If you are stuck, here is one worked candidate, stated at full strength: naming a specific, named candidate ziggurat (Etemenanki) at all, when Walton's own directly-read argument concludes the narrative's details more likely fit a period centuries earlier than that specific structure's attested history, risks giving a popular but probably anachronistic identification more credibility than the evidence supports -- a lesson that reports "Etemenanki" prominently in its own Positions heading, even while reporting the counter-argument, may leave a learner with the popular identification as the one that sticks. Before you answer it, weigh what Positions (1) actually does: it names Etemenanki because that is the identification most sources in print actually propose and dispute by name, not because this lesson adopts it, and it reports Walton's own counter-argument in comparable depth and in his own words, including his own specific archaeological reasoning (the water table, the absence of early historical references to Babylon) rather than a one-line dismissal. A second worked candidate, running the other direction: Positions (2)'s third, unsourced reading (that 11:6 names a capability rather than a stated sin) risks reading too much into a single verse's grammar without any named scholar's support, when the surrounding context -- the builders' own "make a name for ourselves," the aggregated commentaries' consistent reading of pride and self-glorification -- may already settle the question in the traditional reading's favor, making the third reading an invented complication rather than a genuine open question.

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Genesis 11:1-9 does not state, anywhere in its own wording, that the tower built here is the same physical structure later known as the Etemenanki ziggurat of Babylon, or any other specifically named, archaeologically identified building. The text names the place "Babel" (11:9) and describes building materials and a general kind of structure; any further identification with a specific excavated site is an inference from outside this text's own four corners, documented as a live, disputed question in Positions (1). (b) Genesis 11:6-7 does not use the word "pride," or any single Hebrew word scholars agree translates as "pride," to state what God is responding to. The verse states a concern about unified capability ("nothing they devise will be beyond them"); what moral category that concern belongs to is a reading brought to the verse, documented as a real, named dispute in Positions (2), not a word the verse itself supplies.
