Genesis 15 is two conversations held on one night and the following dusk. In the first (15:1-6), Abram answers a promise of reward with a complaint -- he is still childless -- and is taken outside to count the stars; the scene ends with one of the most quoted sentences in the Old Testament: "Abram believed the LORD, and it was credited to him as righteousness" (15:6). In the second (15:7-21), Abram asks how he can know he will possess the land, is told to prepare animals, cut them in half, and wait; at nightfall he hears a forecast of four hundred years of his descendants' slavery, and then "a smoking firepot and a flaming torch" pass between the halves (15:17), and "On that day the LORD made a covenant with Abram" (15:18). This lesson reports four real questions without settling any of them: how Paul and James, both quoting 15:6, are related to each other; who, in the Hebrew of 15:6, credits what to whom; what the cut-animal rite signified; and how the "four hundred years" of 15:13 fits the "430 years" of Exodus 12:40 and Galatians 3:17. Every quotation attributed to the BSB below, here and in this lesson's five connection files, was printed from this app's shipped corpus before drafting and checked afterward by a second script -- see Sources and Limits.

## Context

**What happens, in order (15:1-21).** "After these events" -- the battle and the meeting with Melchizedek in chapter 14 -- "the word of the LORD came to Abram in a vision: 'Do not be afraid, Abram. I am your shield, your very great reward'" (15:1). Abram answers with a question, not thanks: "O Lord GOD, what can You give me, since I remain childless, and the heir of my house is Eliezer of Damascus?" (15:2), adding that "a servant in my household will be my heir" (15:3). The LORD replies that "one who comes from your own body will be your heir" (15:4), takes him outside -- "Now look to the heavens and count the stars, if you are able" -- and says, "So shall your offspring be" (15:5). Then the narrator, not any speaker, reports: "Abram believed the LORD, and it was credited to him as righteousness" (15:6). The second scene opens with a self-identification: "I am the LORD, who brought you out of Ur of the Chaldeans to give you this land to possess" (15:7). Abram asks, "Lord GOD, how can I know that I will possess it?" (15:8). He is told to bring "a heifer, a goat, and a ram, each three years old, along with a turtledove and a young pigeon" (15:9); he splits the animals "down the middle, and laid the halves opposite each other," but "The birds, however, he did not cut in half" (15:10). Birds of prey come down on the carcasses and Abram drives them away (15:11). At sunset "Abram fell into a deep sleep, and suddenly great terror and darkness overwhelmed him" (15:12), and he hears that his descendants "will be strangers in a land that is not their own, and they will be enslaved and mistreated four hundred years" (15:13); that the oppressing nation will be judged and "afterward they will depart with many possessions" (15:14); that he himself "will go to your fathers in peace and be buried at a ripe old age" (15:15); and that "In the fourth generation your descendants will return here, for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete" (15:16). After dark, the firepot and torch pass between the halves (15:17), the covenant is stated with boundaries "from the river of Egypt to the great River Euphrates" (15:18), and ten peoples then living in the land are listed by name (15:19-21).

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens (`content/lens/eleven-stages.json`) files Genesis 12 through Malachi under stage 5, "Israel: The Twelve Tribes" (machine-readable `chapters` field `["1.12"]`), so this lesson sits in the same stage as this curriculum's Genesis 12 unit. Chapter 12 gave the first land promise in a single clause ("I will give this land to your offspring," 12:7); chapter 15 is the first scene in which the LORD "made a covenant with Abram" (15:18), and the first in which the promised heir is specified as "one who comes from your own body" (15:4). Chapter 17's covenant of circumcision is a later, separate scene and a different lesson's territory.

**A wording check, stated up front.** Genesis 15:6 in this app's BSB reads "Abram believed the LORD." All three New Testament quotations read, in the same BSB, "Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness" (Romans 4:3, Galatians 3:6, James 2:23). The difference is not a BSB inconsistency. The Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation) reads καὶ ἐπίστευσεν Ἀβρὰμ τῷ θεῷ, καὶ ἐλογίσθη αὐτῷ εἰς δικαιοσύνην -- "God," not the divine name, and a passive "it was credited" -- and Meyer's commentary on Romans 4:3 states that Paul quotes "according to the LXX., which renders the active" Hebrew verb "by the passive." Ellicott says the same of Galatians 3:6: "Quoted from the LXX. version of Genesis 15:6."

**Key words, read as observations.**

- **Three first occurrences in one verse.** Checked against Bible Hub's concordance listings, the Hebrew words behind "believed" (*heʾemin*, from *ʾaman*, Strong's 539), "credited" (*ḥashab*, Strong's 2803), and "righteousness" (*ṣedaqah*, Strong's 6666) each make their first appearance in the Hebrew Bible at 15:6. The Cambridge Bible says of *ṣedaqah* that it "occurs here for the first time in Scripture," and Gill that "This is the first time we read of believing." What that clustering signifies is a reader's question; that it exists is checkable.
- **An unnamed subject.** In Hebrew, "it was credited" is an active verb, *wayyaḥshebeha*, parsed by Bible Hub's interlinear as third person masculine singular with a feminine object; Bible Hub's own English gloss, "and He reckoned it," capitalizes the pronoun, which is itself an interpretive choice. The verb does not name its subject. English versions that read "He credited it" or "it was credited" are making, or avoiding, a choice the Hebrew leaves to context. See Positions (2).
- **The same phrase, used once more.** Psalm 106:31 says of Phinehas, "It was credited to him as righteousness for endless generations to come" -- the same two Hebrew words (*ḥashab*, *ṣedaqah*), checked in the same concordance listings, and the same BSB English. Ramban cites this verse in his own comment on 15:6.
- **"Cut" a covenant.** "the LORD made a covenant" (15:18) is, in Hebrew, *karat berit*, literally "cut a covenant" (verb *karat*, Strong's 3772, per Bible Hub's interlinear). This curriculum's Genesis 9 lesson already noted that the Noah covenant uses a different verb. In chapter 15 the idiom stands next to animals that have actually been cut.
- **Two different words for "pieces."** "laid the halves opposite each other" (15:10) uses *bether*; "passed between the halves of the carcasses" (15:17) uses *gezarim* (Strong's 1506), with the verb *ʿabar* ("pass") and *bên* ("between"). Jeremiah 34:18-19 uses *bether* and the same "pass between." See the reviewed connection to Jeremiah 34.

**The four hundred years: what the texts say, and what they do not settle.** Four passages give numbers, and they do not obviously agree:

- Genesis 15:13: descendants "enslaved and mistreated four hundred years."
- Genesis 15:16: "In the fourth generation your descendants will return here."
- Exodus 12:40-41: "Now the duration of the Israelites' stay in Egypt was 430 years," and "At the end of the 430 years, to the very day," the LORD's divisions left Egypt.
- Acts 7:6 (Stephen): "enslaved and mistreated four hundred years." Galatians 3:17 (Paul): "The law that came 430 years later does not revoke the covenant previously established by God."

Named readers handle this in at least two opposite ways. Ellicott, on Genesis 15:13, reads 430 as the length of the stay in Egypt itself: "The exact duration of the sojourn in Egypt was 430 years (Exodus 12:40-41)," with 400 as the round figure. Barnes' Notes on the same verse counts instead from Isaac's birth, giving one hundred ninety years as "strangers in a land not theirs" before Egypt and "the remaining two hundred and ten years in Egypt." Behind the shorter count sits a textual difference: Gill, Matthew Poole, and Jamieson-Fausset-Brown all report that the Septuagint and the Samaritan Pentateuch read Exodus 12:40 as including the land of Canaan, and Jamieson-Fausset-Brown concludes that "the period of sojourn in Egypt did not exceed two hundred fifteen years" -- while Barnes, on Exodus 12:40, holds that "The obvious intention of Moses is to state the duration of the sojourn in Egypt." Ellicott, on Galatians 3:17, judges that "there were two systems of chronology really current," and reports that Josephus uses both (this lesson did not read Josephus). Meyer, on Acts 7:6, reads Stephen's four hundred as given "in round numbers." The "fourth generation" of 15:16 adds a second puzzle: the Cambridge Bible notes it fits the four names Levi, Kohath, Amram, Moses (Exodus 6:16-20) only "If... a generation must have been computed as 100 years," and doubts "whether the mention of 'the fourth generation' comes from the same hand as 'the 400 years'"; the Pulpit Commentary reports that Oort and Kuenen saw a contradiction between the two verses. As this curriculum's Genesis 11:10-32 unit did with Terah's age, this lesson reports the texts and the named harmonizations and does not choose among them.

**"the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete" (15:16).** The clause gives a stated reason for delay: the descendants will not return until a measure of wrongdoing among the land's current inhabitants is filled. The Cambridge Bible paraphrases it as "the wickedness of the people of Canaan must reach a certain degree, before the Divine penalty can be inflicted," and points to Leviticus 18:24-30 and Deuteronomy 9:5. Both are checkable in this app's BSB: Leviticus 18:25 says the land "will vomit out its inhabitants," and Deuteronomy 9:5 tells Israel, "It is not because of your righteousness or uprightness of heart that you are going in to possess their land, but it is because of their wickedness" -- using the same word for "righteousness" (*ṣedaqah*) as 15:6. This verse bears directly on how readers later weigh the conquest narratives of Joshua, a serious moral question on which readers in good faith differ. This lesson does not adjudicate it; it notes only what 15:16 states (a delay, and a reason for it) and leaves the larger question to the lessons whose passages narrate those events.

## Literary Design

**Two scenes, one shape.** Each half opens with a self-identifying word from God (15:1 "I am your shield"; 15:7 "I am the LORD, who brought you out"), continues with a question from Abram that begins "Lord GOD" (15:2, 15:8), and ends with a confirming act (the stars, 15:5; the firepot and torch, 15:17). The parallel is checkable by reading 15:1-2 beside 15:7-8. Rashi draws a contrast inside it: on 15:6 he says Abram "did not ask Him for a sign regarding this" promise of offspring, "but in respect to the promise that he would possess the land he asked for a sign" (15:8).

**An echo of a later formula.** "I am the LORD, who brought you out of Ur of the Chaldeans" (15:7) uses the same shape as the opening of the Ten Commandments, "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt" (Exodus 20:2). The resemblance is checkable in the BSB of both verses. No source read for this lesson discusses it, so it is offered as an observation for testing, not a cited finding.

## Positions

Three questions, each reported through named positions in their own words. None is decided here, and no position below is this lesson's own conclusion.

**1. Paul and James both quote 15:6. How are their uses related?** Paul quotes it immediately after writing, "If Abraham was indeed justified by works, he had something to boast about, but not before God" (Romans 4:2), and goes on to say that faith is credited "to the one who does not work" (4:5). James quotes it, with "the Scripture was fulfilled," to argue that "a man is justified by his deeds and not by faith alone" (James 2:23-24), pointing to Abraham offering Isaac (2:21). Traditions have read this pairing in sharply different ways.

- **A Reformation reading: justification by faith alone, with James describing faith's evidence.** The Westminster Confession (ch. 11.2) states that faith "is the alone instrument of justification: yet is it not alone in the person justified, but is ever accompanied with all other saving graces, and is no dead faith, but worketh by love." Chapter 11.1 adds a point that bears on the wording of 15:6 itself: God justifies "nor by imputing faith itself, the act of believing, or any other evangelical obedience to them, as their righteousness; but by imputing the obedience and satisfaction of Christ unto them." Calvin, on James 2:21, states the two-meaning solution: "Paul means by it the gratuitous imputation of righteousness before the tribunal of God; and James, the manifestation of righteousness by the conduct, and that before men." Readers within this tradition differ on the detail: R. Bruce Compton (*Detroit Baptist Seminary Journal*, 1997), defending the same harmony, reads James's "justified" not as display before others but as "the subsequent act whereby God confirms the righteous standing of the redeemed based on works."
- **A Catholic reading: the Council of Trent.** Trent's Decree on Justification (Session VI, ch. 8) teaches that "we are therefore said to be justified by faith, because faith is the beginning of human salvation, the foundation, and the root of all Justification," and "justified freely" because none of the things that precede justification, "whether faith or works," merit "the grace itself of justification." Chapter 10 cites James 2:24 directly in teaching that the justified, "faith co-operating with good works, increase in that justice which they have received" and "are still further justified." Canon 9 rejects the view that "by faith alone the impious is justified; in such wise as to mean, that nothing else is required to co-operate"; Canon 24 rejects the view that good works "are merely the fruits and signs of Justification obtained, but not a cause of the increase thereof." (Read in an English translation whose translator the source page does not name.)
- **The New Perspective on Paul.** N. T. Wright, in a lecture to the Edinburgh Dogmatics Conference read for this lesson, argues that "in Romans 4 Paul is demonstrably arguing about God's faithfulness to the Abrahamic covenant, not simply using Abraham as an example of someone justified by faith." Of Paul's reckoning language more generally, the same lecture holds that what is reckoned is not God's or Christ's own righteousness "but rather the fresh status of 'covenant member', and/or 'justified sinner'." David Shaw (*Themelios* 40.1) quotes Wright's later article on Romans 4 reading 15:6's phrase as meaning "more or less, 'God reckoned this in terms of covenant membership', or perhaps 'God made a covenant with him on this basis'" -- an argument Wright draws from the verse's position between the promise of 15:1 and the covenant ceremony of 15:7-21. James D. G. Dunn's related argument, as Markus Cromhout's review summarizes it, is that the "works of the law" Paul opposed were chiefly "circumcision, dietary laws and Sabbath observance," important "'badges' of identity" -- a reading that changes what Paul's "not by works" is denying. Shaw writes as a critic of Wright's newer reading; Dunn's own books were not read for this lesson.
- **A fourth stance: the tension is real.** Compton's article reports (fn. 5) readers who do not think the two can be fully harmonized, among them Luther's 1546 preface to James -- "It is flatly against St. Paul and all the rest of Scripture in ascribing justification to works" -- and, as Compton lists them, Ropes, Bultmann, and Dunn's *Unity and Diversity in the New Testament*. These are reported only as Compton cites them.

These readings start from the same two texts and the same quoted verse, and they disagree about what "justified," "works," and "faith" mean in each letter. This lesson reports them and does not rank them. The question underneath -- how a person stands before God -- is not a study-note question. A reader working this out for their own life needs a pastor, priest, or trusted teacher in their own tradition, not a summary of four positions.

**2. In the Hebrew of 15:6, who credits righteousness to whom?** The verb has no named subject (see Key Words), and two medieval Jewish commentators read it differently.

- **God credits Abram's faith as righteousness.** Rashi: "The Holy One, blessed be He, accounted it unto Abraham as a merit, because of the faith with which he had trusted in Him." This is also the direction of the Septuagint's passive and of the New Testament quotations.
- **Abram credits God's promise as God's own righteousness.** Ramban (Nachmanides) quotes Rashi and objects that he does "not understand the nature of this merit," since a prophet who later stood ready "to sacrifice his only son" would hardly need credit for believing "a good tiding." His preferred reading is that Abram "believed in G-d and he considered it due to the righteousness of the Holy One, blessed be He, that He would give him a child under all circumstances, and not because of Abram's state of righteousness and his reward." Ramban then offers a second, God-as-subject reading himself, comparing Psalm 106:31.

Both readings fit the grammar; they differ on context and on which party "righteousness" belongs to. This lesson reports both.

**3. What did the cut-animal rite signify, and who passed between the pieces?**

- **A self-curse.** Gill reads the rite as one in which covenant-makers passed between the parts, "signifying, that should they break the covenant made, they deserved to be cut asunder as that creature was," citing Jeremiah 34:18. An eighth-century BC Aramaic treaty from Sefire, quoted on Wikipedia (a tertiary source; the inscription's edition was not read), contains a comparable curse: "[As] this calf is cut up, thus Matti'el and his nobles shall be cut up."
- **Not a self-curse.** Keil and Delitzsch reply that "there is no sure ground in Jeremiah 34:18" for reading the ancient custom that way, and suggest the division "probably denoted originally the two parties to the covenant, and the passing of the latter through the pieces... their formation into one."
- **Who passed.** Jamieson-Fausset-Brown hold that Abram "did not pass between the sacrifice and the reason was that in this transaction he was bound to nothing"; Gill likewise says "only God passed between the pieces, not Abram." Ellicott disagrees: "Abram had probably passed between them immediately after arranging them, and now Jehovah does the same." The text of 15:17 names only the firepot and torch; it neither states nor rules out an earlier passage by Abram.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from the passage in its own setting to a modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key; the learner's own attempt is a separate step, and a different element or situation would be equally legitimate.

**Passage element.** Genesis 15:2-3 and 15:8. Twice Abram answers God's word with a direct question -- "what can You give me, since I remain childless" and "how can I know that I will possess it?" -- and neither question is rebuked in the text. The first is answered with the stars (15:5), the second with the ceremony (15:9-17).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the narrative places honest questions from Abram immediately before the two high points of the chapter, the crediting of righteousness (15:6) and the covenant (15:18), and records no reproach for either question. Inference: hearers who treasured Abram as their ancestor would have heard that questioning a promise, aloud and to the one who made it, was compatible with the trust 15:6 describes. Rashi's distinction (no sign asked in 15:2-5, a sign asked in 15:8) shows that readers have long noticed the two questions are not identical.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Bringing a doubt directly to the one who made a promise, in plain words, is a different act from withdrawing in silence or abandoning the commitment, and the first can sit alongside real trust.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a promise has been made; its fulfillment is overdue by ordinary standards; the person waiting states the difficulty plainly to the one who promised. Not carried forward: the divine speaker, the specific promises of heir and land, the vision and the ceremony, and any expectation of a supernatural sign.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed; nothing here promises that a question put to a person -- or to God -- will receive the answer Abram received.

**Modern domain and situation.** Church and volunteer life. A volunteer has been told for three years that the congregation will fund the after-school program she runs; each year the budget passes without it. She is tempted either to quit quietly or to stop asking and keep paying for supplies herself.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: honest inquiry to the responsible party. One faithful response is to ask the person who made the commitment, directly and without accusation, what is actually standing in the way and what she can expect, and then to decide about continuing on the basis of the answer rather than on silent resentment.

**Cautions.** This example does not suggest that every delayed human promise deserves continued trust; some promises are broken and should be named as broken. If the situation involves financial hardship, employment law, or a pattern of mistreatment, a qualified person -- a pastor, counselor, or professional adviser -- is the right next step, not a study note. The passage is not a text about church budgets; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. Rashi and Ramban read the same five Hebrew words of 15:6 with different subjects. Using only the chapter itself -- 15:1's "your very great reward" and 15:7-8's request for assurance -- what would you point to as support for each reading, and is anything in the chapter decisive?
2. Paul says the words "it was credited to him" were "written not only for Abraham" but "also for us" (Romans 4:23-24); James says the same Scripture "was fulfilled" when Abraham offered Isaac (James 2:21-23). Before consulting any tradition, what does each writer appear to need the verse to do in his own argument?
3. In Jeremiah 34:20 the covenant-breakers' "corpses will become food for the birds of the air"; in Genesis 15:11 "the birds of prey descended on the carcasses, but Abram drove them away." Is this a meaningful correspondence, or two natural details of the same kind of scene? What evidence would decide it?
4. The texts give 400 years, a "fourth generation," and 430 years. Before reading any harmonization, list what each verse is counting from and to, as far as its own wording says. Where does the wording itself stop telling you?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate Genesis 15 in order -- two scenes, two questions, two confirming acts -- saying what happens, not what it means. (b) Quote 15:6 as this app's BSB gives it, then state how the New Testament wording differs and why (the Septuagint). (c) Name one New Testament passage that quotes this chapter and say whether it quotes or only alludes.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) the two scenes of 15:1-21 (Context); (1:00-2:00) 15:6, its three first-occurring Hebrew words, and its unnamed subject (Key Words; Positions 2); (2:00-3:00) the rite of 15:9-17 and its parallel in Jeremiah 34 (Key Words; Positions 3); (3:00-4:00) the three New Testament quotations and the named Paul-and-James positions, stated without a verdict (Positions 1); (4:00-4:30) the 400/430 puzzle, laid out text by text (Context); (4:30-5:00) the boundary in prompt 4.
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: setting Trent, Westminster, Wright, and Luther side by side as "positions" treats a question about salvation as if it were a matter of taste, when each tradition holds that its answer is true and that the stakes are eternal. Before answering, notice what the lesson actually claims: that these are each tradition's own words, reported, and that the personal question belongs with a pastor. A second candidate, running the other way: the lesson's Key Words section (three first occurrences in one verse; an unnamed subject) may itself nudge a reader toward treating 15:6 as more doctrinally loaded, or more open, than the narrative intends. What would you need to see to decide which?
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) Genesis 15 does not, by its own wording, settle the dispute between Paul's and James's readers; the chapter contains neither the word "works" nor any discussion of Isaac's offering, which comes seven chapters later. Positions (1) reports conclusions drawn from the New Testament letters, not from 15:6 alone. (b) Genesis 15:13 and 15:16 do not, by themselves, fix the length of Israel's stay in Egypt; their numbers must be combined with Exodus 12:40 and with a choice about textual witnesses before any figure results. (c) Genesis 15:17 does not state that Abram did not pass between the pieces; it states only what the firepot and torch did.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** The reviewed connections attached to this lesson:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Romans 4:1-25 | quotation | explicit | "For what does the Scripture say?" introduces 15:6; 4:18 also quotes 15:5 word for word; wording follows the Septuagint |
| Galatians 3:6-7 | quotation | explicit | Same sentence as Romans 4:3, introduced only by "So also," but the full sentence occurs in the Old Testament only at 15:6 (Psalm 106:31 shares only the crediting clause) |

Choose one row and defend it, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` for a candidate. Three candidates this lesson deliberately did not author: (a) **The firepot and torch as a picture of Christ.** Jamieson-Fausset-Brown move from 15:17 to "the only-begotten Son, who passed through between God and us," and devotional writing often reads the rite this way; but no New Testament text cites 15:17, so this is at most a `plausible` type that a named scholar would need to argue -- `no_warrant_yet` is the honest grade here. (b) **Hebrews 11:12's "descendants as numerous as the stars in the sky."** The stars of 15:5 are a real candidate, but the same image recurs at Genesis 22:17 ("like the stars in the sky") and 26:4, so Hebrews cannot be tied to chapter 15 specifically. (c) **Galatians 3:15-17.** Paul speaks of a covenant "previously established by God" and the 430 years, but does not say which Genesis promise-scene he means (12, 15, 17, or 22); the chronology belongs in Context, not in a connection row.

T. Wright's Edinburgh lecture (delivery year not stated on the page); Shaw's *Themelios* article; Cromhout's review; Compton's article.
- **Secondhand only, and labeled as such:** Wright's 2013 article "Paul and the Patriarch" (quoted through Shaw); Dunn's views (through Cromhout and Shaw; his "Unity and Diversity" only through Compton's footnote); Luther's 1546 preface, Ropes, and Bultmann (through Compton); the Sefire treaty (through Wikipedia, a tertiary source); Josephus (through Ellicott); the Septuagint and Samaritan readings of Exodus 12:40 (through Gill, Poole, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, and Ellicott; the Samaritan text itself was not read); Oort and Kuenen (through the Pulpit Commentary).
- **Not found or not used.** No Eastern Orthodox or Lutheran confessional statement on Paul and James was read for this lesson; their absence from Positions (1) is a sourcing gap, not a claim that those traditions are silent. No Ancient Near Eastern text beyond the Sefire curse is cited; other covenant-ritual parallels sometimes mentioned in the literature were not verified and are omitted. The resemblance between 15:7 and Exodus 20:2 is this lesson's own observation, with no source.
- **The conquest question** raised by 15:16 is named and left open on purpose; it belongs to lessons on the passages that narrate the conquest.
