Genesis 29 opens at a well and ends with a mother naming her fourth son. Jacob, sent to his mother's family, meets Rachel, works seven years for her, and on the wedding night is given her older sister Leah instead. Laban explains, "It is not our custom here to give the younger daughter in marriage before the older" (29:26). A week later Jacob marries Rachel too, for seven more years of work. Then the narrator turns to Leah: "When the LORD saw that Leah was unloved, He opened her womb" (29:31). Her four sons are Reuben, Simeon, Levi and Judah, and her words at Judah's birth, "This time I will praise the LORD" (29:35), contain the first "praise" in this app's BSB. The lesson checks the claimed echoes of Jacob's own deception in Genesis 27, follows Levi and Judah forward, and reports four questions on which named Jewish and Christian readers differ. The chapter involves a woman given in marriage in the dark and a man deceived into it. If a situation like that is part of your own life, see the note and resources at the end of Position 2; a pastor or counselor you trust can also help. Every BSB quotation was checked by script against this app's shipped corpus (see Sources and Limits).

## Context

**What happens, in order.** Jacob reaches a well "in the field" (29:2), rolls the stone away for Rachel, waters her father's flock, kisses her and weeps (29:10-11). Laban welcomes him (29:13-14). After a month Laban asks what his wages should be (29:15), and Jacob serves seven years for Rachel (29:18-20). At the feast, "when evening came, Laban took his daughter Leah and gave her to Jacob" (29:23). "When morning came, there was Leah!" (29:25). Laban cites the custom and gives Rachel a week later for seven more years (29:26-30). Leah bears four sons (29:31-35).

**Where this unit sits.** Stage 5 of this app's eleven-stage lens (Genesis 12 through Malachi, machine-readable `chapters: ["1.12"]`). Jacob left home to escape Esau (27:41-45) and on his mother's errand to find a wife among her kin (28:2). The births continue in Genesis 30.

**Key words, read as observations.** Each count was made by this lesson's own script over the app's BSB text, case-insensitively and on word boundaries.

- **Love and its lack.** Words built on "love" ("love", "loved", "unloved") occur in six verses of Genesis 29: 29:18, 29:20, 29:30, 29:31, 29:32 and 29:33. No other chapter of Genesis has more than three such verses. "Unloved" occurs in the whole BSB only at Genesis 29:31 and 29:33, Deuteronomy 21:15-17 and Proverbs 30:23 ("an unloved woman who marries"). Behind "unloved" in 29:31 and 29:33, Bible Hub's interlinear shows *senu'ah* (Strong's 8130), glossed "hated."
- **"Deceived" (29:25).** The Hebrew verb is *rimmitani* (Strong's 7411). Bible Hub's concordance lists 13 occurrences of this verb, the first at Genesis 29:25. Isaac's words about Jacob at 27:35, "Your brother came deceitfully," use *mirmah* (4820), a noun the same lexicon derives "from ramah in the sense of deceiving." So the root recurs; the word does not. (Jacob's own fear at 27:12, "revealed to him as a deceiver," uses a different word, 8591.)
- **"Older" (29:26).** Where the BSB has "the older," the Hebrew has *habbekirah* (1067), "the firstborn," a feminine form related to *bekhor*, the word in Jacob's lie at 27:19: "I am Esau, your firstborn." The BSB's "older" is accurate for a daughter; it hides the echo.
- **"Barren" (29:31).** In Genesis, "barren" describes Sarai (11:30), Rebekah (25:21) and Rachel (29:31), and no one else.
- **The names (29:32-35).** Each name is explained by Leah's words. Reuben: "The LORD has seen my affliction" (the verb *ra'ah*, 7200; Keil and Delitzsch gloss the name "see, a son!"). Simeon: "Because the LORD has heard" (*shama*, 8085). Levi: "my husband will become attached to me" (*yillaweh*, 3867). Judah: "This time I will praise the LORD" (*odeh*, 3034). The words "this time" (*happa'am*) stand in both 29:34 and 29:35. For three sons the Hebrew says "she called his name." For Levi it says *qara*, "he called" or "was called" (BSB: "So he was named Levi"). Rashi notes the change: "Of all of them (the sons) it is written 'And she called', but of this one Scripture writes 'he called!'"
- **"Praise" (29:35).** In this app's BSB, the first verse anywhere with any form of "praise" is Genesis 29:35. The only other in Genesis is 49:8, Jacob's blessing on Judah: "Judah, your brothers shall praise you." Both use the Hebrew verb *yadah* (3034). Bible Hub's concordance lists 114 occurrences of *yadah* and puts Genesis 29:35 first. The claim holds for this verb and for the English word, not for every Hebrew word for praise: *halal* (1984) appears earlier, at 12:15, where Pharaoh's officials "commended" Sarai. The Cambridge Bible adds a caution on the name itself: "The origin of the name is uncertain; but its sound resembles that of the word for 'praise.'"

## Literary Design

**The third well meeting.** The servant met Rebekah at a well (24:11-27), Jacob meets Rachel at one here, and Moses later meets Zipporah's family at a well in Midian (Exodus 2:15-21). The lesson on Genesis 24 reported the "betrothal type-scene" reading and the John 4 connection; neither is repeated here. Differences from Genesis 24 can be checked. Here the man himself does the work: Jacob "rolled the stone away from the mouth of the well and watered his uncle's sheep" (29:10), where Rebekah watered the servant's camels. Here he comes with no gifts. Among the reasons Genesis Rabbah 70:12 and Rashi give for his weeping (another is that he foresaw Rachel would not be buried with him) is this: "Eliezer, my grandfather's servant, had with him rings, bracelets and all good things, whilst I have nothing with me" (Rashi). And Laban runs out in both chapters (24:29; 29:13); Rashi, following Genesis Rabbah 70:13, says he ran "thinking that he was laden with money." Genesis Rabbah 70:11 compares Exodus 2 directly: there seven daughters were driven off by shepherds, "However, here, it was one, and no person touched her."

**The deceiver deceived.** Many readers set this chapter beside Genesis 27. The checkable points:

| | Genesis 27 | Genesis 29 |
|---|---|---|
| Who cannot see | Isaac's "eyes were so weak that he could no longer see" (27:1) | the bride is given "when evening came" (29:23) and known "when morning came" (29:25) |
| The substitution | the younger son in place of the older (27:15-19) | the older daughter in place of the younger (29:23-26) |
| "Firstborn" | "I am Esau, your firstborn" (27:19, *bekhor*) | "the older" (29:26, *bekirah*, "firstborn") |
| Deceit | "Your brother came deceitfully" (27:35, *mirmah*) | "Why have you deceived me?" (29:25, *rimmitani*) |

Genesis Rabbah 70:19 puts the comparison in Leah's mouth. In the morning Jacob says, "What, you are a deceiver, daughter of a deceiver, did I not call you Rachel at night and you answered me?" She answers, "Is there a barber without disciples? Did your father not call you Esau and you answered him?" Christian commentators make the same comparison as a judgment of God. Matthew Henry: "Jacob, who had imposed upon his father, is imposed upon by Laban, his father-in-law, by a like deception. Herein, how unrighteous soever Laban was, the Lord was righteous." Barnes: "This is the first retribution Jacob experiences for the deceitful practices of his former days." Keil and Delitzsch: "Thus the overreacher of Esau was overreached himself, and sin was punished by sin." The Cambridge Bible: "Jacob, who deceived his father and his brother, is made to suffer himself from deception." Calvin, in his comment on 29:25, does not draw the comparison; he discusses Laban's fraud and Laban's pretext. The narrator states no such link. The table above shows what the reading rests on, and the lesson leaves the weighing to the reader.

**Four names in sequence.** Read in order, Leah's words move from what God has done to what she hopes from Jacob and back: "The LORD has seen my affliction. Surely my husband will love me now" (29:32); "Because the LORD has heard that I am unloved" (29:33); "Now at last my husband will become attached to me" (29:34); "This time I will praise the LORD" (29:35). Only the fourth speech does not mention her husband. Ellicott reads the series this way: "And now slowly she parts with her hope of human affection, and finds comfort in Jehovah alone." Jewish readers give the last line a special place. The Talmud (Berakhot 7b) quotes Rabbi Yoḥanan in the name of Rabbi Shimon ben Yoḥai: "From the day the Holy One, Blessed be He, created the world, no one thanked the Holy One, Blessed be He, until Leah came and thanked Him." (The same Hebrew verb can be rendered "thank" or "praise.") Genesis Rabbah 71:4 explains "this time" with a parable of a priest who thanks the giver of a handful more than the giver of a full measure. The matriarchs "believed that this one would have three, and that one would have three"; so "when Leah bore a fourth son, she said: 'This time I will thank the Lord.'" Rashi follows: "because I have assumed more than my share."

**Where the names lead.** Two of the four sons become the lines of priesthood and kingship, and readers in both traditions notice it here, at the births.

- *Levi.* The Cambridge Bible observes that the verb of 29:34, "joined," is the word "especially used of the attachment of the sons of Levi to the service of Jehovah" in Numbers 18:2 and 18:4, where the BSB has "that they may join you." Targum Pseudo-Jonathan expands Leah's words: "thus will it be that my children shall be united to serve before the Lord." Later texts give Levi the priesthood (Deuteronomy 33:8-10) and Hebrews speaks of "the sons of Levi who become priests" (Hebrews 7:5). Barnes: "Levi the reconciler is the father of the priestly tribe."
- *Judah.* Jacob's blessing returns to the verb of 29:35, "Judah, your brothers shall praise you" (49:8), and promises "The scepter will not depart from Judah" (49:10). Chronicles says, "Judah prevailed over his brothers and a ruler came from him" (1 Chronicles 5:2). Ruth traces "the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah" (Ruth 4:12) down to David (4:18-22). Matthew opens, "Jacob the father of Judah and his brothers" (Matthew 1:2), and Hebrews says, "our Lord descended from Judah" (Hebrews 7:14). Targum Pseudo-Jonathan has Leah say, "from this my son kings shall come forth, and from him shall spring David the king, who shall offer praise before the Lord." Matthew Henry: "This was he, of whom, as concerning the flesh, Christ came."
- *Both together.* Genesis Rabbah 71:5 reports Rabbi Levi: "Two tribes ascended to greatness – the tribe of priesthood and the tribe of kingship," listing parallels that include "'this time' for this one and 'this time' for that one" (29:34 and 29:35). Barnes draws a Christian conclusion from the same mother: "It is remarkable that the wife of priority, but not of preference, is the mother of the seed in whom all nations are to be blessed." Ellicott, on 29:17, makes the same point about the sister passed over: "Yet it was not Rachel, with her fair face and well-proportioned figure, and her husband's lasting love, that was the mother of the progenitor of the Messiah, but the weary-eyed Leah."

The other two sons have harder later stories: Reuben's act at 35:22 and his loss of the birthright (49:3-4; 1 Chronicles 5:1), and Simeon and Levi's violence at Shechem (34:25-30; 49:5-7). The Talmud (Berakhot 7b) reads Reuben's name kindly, as Leah's "See [*re'u*] the difference between my son [*beni*] and the son of my father-in-law," since Reuben did not resent losing the birthright and tried to save Joseph (37:21).

## Positions

Four questions follow. Each is reported through named readers in their own words, and none is decided here.

**1. Laban's "custom" (29:26): a real custom or a pretext?** The text gives Laban's words and no comment from the narrator. Readers divide.

- *A real custom, wrongly concealed.* The Pulpit Commentary takes the custom as real and widespread: "The same custom exists among the Indians (Rosenmüller...), Egyptians (Lane), and other Oriental countries (Delitzsch)," and notes Kalisch's view that "the custom has grown into a strong moral obligation." Calvin accepts the custom ("injustice would have been done to the firstborn by disturbing this accustomed order") but holds that "the answer of Laban, though it is not without a pretext, yet forms no excuse for the fraud." He should "have cautioned Jacob himself, in time."
- *A pretext.* Keil and Delitzsch: "A perfectly worthless excuse; for if this had really been the custom in Haran as in ancient India and elsewhere, he ought to have told Jacob of it before." Poole: "This seems to be a false pretence; but if it had been true, custom is to give place to justice." Matthew Henry: "There is reason to believe that Laban's excuse was not true." Ellicott calls it "a pretext which, if not false, was yet overstrained," noting "we have no proof of the existence of any such custom among the Bedaween."
- *A plot with the town.* Genesis Rabbah 70:19 imagines Laban gathering "all the people of his place" and proposing, "If you wish, I will deceive him, and give him Leah... and he will spend another seven years with you," and the guests hinting all evening, "she is Leah, she is Leah."

The readers quoted agree that Jacob was not told in advance, and the text supports that much, since he asks, "Wasn't it for Rachel that I served you?" (29:25).

**2. Leah's part in the substitution.** The text says what Laban did: he "took his daughter Leah and gave her to Jacob" (29:23). It records nothing Leah said, wanted or knew until her first son is born (29:32). Neither Leah's agreement nor Jacob's informed agreement to this marriage is recorded. Readers fill the silence in four ways.

- *A party to the fraud.* Calvin: "It was not without fault on Leah's part that she was despised by her husband; and the Lord justly chastised her, because she, being aware of her father's fraud, dishonorably obtained possession of her sister's husband." He adds at once: "but her fault forms no excuse for Jacob's lust." Ellicott: "Leah must have been a party to the fraud, and therefore Jacob's dislike of her was not altogether without reason." Benson says she bore Jacob's coldness as something "she had reason to bear, because she was consenting to the fraud by which she became his wife." Genesis Rabbah 71:2 reports that people mocked her: "Were she a righteous woman, she would not have deceived her sister."
- *Acting under her father.* Gill finds it "not easy" to excuse her, but says that things "may be said indeed in her favour, as obedience to her father," and suggests, following Schmidt, "that Laban had persuaded her to believe, that the matrimonial contract he had made with Jacob was on her account." The Pulpit Commentary finds it "difficult to understand how Leah could acquiesce," supposes "she must herself have been attached to Jacob," and thinks it "probable that Laban had explained to her his plan."
- *Spared by her sister.* The Talmud (Megillah 13b) says Jacob and Rachel had agreed on secret signs, and Rachel, unwilling to see her sister shamed, gave them to Leah. Gill rejects the story as "beyond belief."
- *Answering Jacob in his own terms.* In Genesis Rabbah 70:19, quoted above, Leah answers his "deceiver" with his own deception of Isaac.

Calvin draws one more conclusion from the night: Jacob "might have dismissed Leah, because she had not been his lawful wife: because the mutual consent of the man and the woman, respecting which mistake is impossible, constitutes marriage." Ellicott judges the other way, that divorcing Leah "would have been worse." The text records only that Jacob stayed married to both.

**3. What does "unloved" mean (29:31, 29:33)?** The Hebrew is the "hated" root, and this curriculum's Genesis 25 lesson (Position 2) met the same question in Malachi 1:3 and Romans 9:13, where the Cambridge Bible cites this chapter. The readings divide in a similar way here.

- *Loved less.* The narrator has just said Jacob "loved Rachel more than Leah" (29:30). Benson: "That is, loved less than Rachel." Barnes ("less loved than Rachel") and Gill say the same; Gill notes "his doing the duty of an husband to her." Jamieson-Fausset-Brown: "not loved so much as she ought to have been." The Cambridge Bible: "By this is meant that Jacob had less affection for Leah than for Rachel," citing Deuteronomy 21:15 (see the connections). Barnes, on 29:30, takes "loved Rachel more" to mean Leah had some share: "This proves that even Leah was not unloved."
- *Less than she was owed, and so a fault.* Calvin: "When Moses asserts that Leah was hated, his meaning is, that she was not loved so much as she ought to have been. For she was not intolerable to Jacob, neither did he pursue her with hatred; but Moses, by the use of this word, amplifies his fault."
- *Not to be softened.* Ellicott: "We must not soften this down too much; for plainly Leah was not the object of love at all. It was her fruitfulness which gave her value in her husband's eyes."
- *A wordplay in the midrash.* Genesis Rabbah 71:2 connects *senu'ah* with her earlier fear that she would marry "the enemy [*lasoneh*]," Esau, and adds that Jacob at first "set his mind to divorce her" until her children came.

Leah's own words keep the stronger word: "the LORD has heard that I am unloved" (29:33).

**4. The text records polygamy and a marriage to two sisters. How do readers judge it?** The chapter narrates the two marriages without comment. Later law forbids one part of what happened: "You must not take your wife's sister as a rival wife and have sexual relations with her while your wife is still alive" (Leviticus 18:18). The chapters that follow record the sisters' rivalry (30:1, 30:15). Christian and Jewish readers explain the patriarch's conduct in different ways.

- *A sin, doubled.* Calvin is the severest. Laban "not only involves his nephew in polygamy, but pollutes both him and his own daughters by incestuous nuptials." He cites Malachi as pronouncing "divorce to be more tolerable than polygamy," and says of Jacob: "although necessity may, in some degree, excuse the fault of Jacob, it cannot altogether absolve him from blame," since he "doubles his fault by polygamy, and trebles it by an incestuous marriage." Against appeals to old practice he writes: "it was not allowable for men, at their own pleasure, to subvert, by a depraved custom, the law of marriage divinely sanctioned from the beginning."
- *A sin of ignorance before the law.* Matthew Henry: "As yet there was no express command against marrying more than one wife. It was in the patriarchs a sin of ignorance; but it will not justify the like practice now," citing Leviticus 18:18 and 1 Corinthians 7:2. Keil and Delitzsch hold that "This bigamy of Jacob must not be judged directly by the Mosaic law... or set down as incest (Calvin, etc.), since there was no positive law on the point in existence then." They add that it "is not to be justified" by its results, and that God "condemned the ungodliness of such a marriage, and prepared the way for the subsequent prohibition in the law."
- *No crime under the custom of its time.* Augustine, answering the Manichaean Faustus: "a plurality of wives was no crime when it was the custom; and it is a crime now, because it is no longer the custom." He holds that Jacob took wives "not for sensual gratification, but for the procreation of children," and adds that anyone who now "despises these restraints... still commits sin."
- *Not lawful, but a figure.* Justin Martyr (*Dialogue with Trypho* 134) writes that "it was not lawful for Jacob to marry two sisters at once," and that "The marriages of Jacob were types of that which Christ was about to accomplish."
- *A moral understanding that developed.* The Catechism of the Catholic Church: "In the Old Testament the polygamy of patriarchs and kings is not yet explicitly rejected" (1610), and in its teaching on marriage today, "polygamy is not in accord with the moral law" (2387).
- *Permitted outside the Land.* Ramban asks how Jacob could "marry two sisters in their lifetime" if the patriarchs kept the commandments before Sinai, and answers that their observance "applied only in the Land of Israel, whereas Jacob married two sisters only when outside the Land." On Leviticus 18:25 he adds that Rachel died "on the way when they started coming into the Land," since Jacob "could not dwell in the Land with two sisters," and she was the one "by whose marriage the prohibition against two sisters took effect."
- *Foreseen.* Rashi, on 29:34, says "the Matriarchs were prophetesses and knew that twelve tribes would issue from Jacob and that he would have four wives."
- *A later Jewish ban.* Isaac Sassoon (TheTorah.com, 2016) reports that the Pharisees read Leviticus 18:18 narrowly, as forbidding two biological sisters, and "are known to have permitted and possibly practiced polygyny," while the Damascus Covenant from Qumran read the same verse as forbidding "taking two wives in their lifetimes." He writes that "Popular legend tells us that Rabbenu Gershom (d. ca 1028) was the first to prohibit polygyny," adds that the ascription "has been disputed," and says that "the custom took root in medieval Ashkenaz."

These readers differ on whether Jacob sinned, how far he could know it, and whether the marriages carry a further meaning. This lesson does not decide between them.

*A figural reading used against Jews.* In the same chapter, Justin reads the sisters as peoples: "Now Leah is your people and synagogue; but Rachel is our Church," and he turns Leah's weak eyes into a charge against the souls of his Jewish hearers. This curriculum's Genesis 25 lesson traced the similar Church-and-Synagogue reading of Jacob and Esau and its use against Jews. Genesis 29 gives no such meaning to either sister, and Ruth 4:11 honors both together "who together built up the house of Israel." The Second Vatican Council, in general terms that do not cite Genesis 29, taught that "the Jews should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God, as if this followed from the Holy Scriptures" (*Nostra Aetate* 4, 1965). Augustine also reads the sisters figurally, but as two kinds of Christian life, Leah active and Rachel contemplative (*Contra Faustum* 22.54), not as two peoples.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge. It models the shape; it is not an answer key.

**Passage element.** Genesis 29:25-26 (BSB). Jacob: "Wasn't it for Rachel that I served you? Why have you deceived me?" Laban: "It is not our custom here to give the younger daughter in marriage before the older."

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the rule Laban cites is stated only after seven years of work are done, and it changes what the work earned. Inference: a first audience would likely hear that a condition revealed after a bargain is kept by one side is a way of cheating, whatever the condition. The narrator states no moral; the commentators in Position 1 supply theirs.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** If you hold the power to set terms, state the conditions before the other person does the work, not after.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a bargain, work done in good faith, and a rule disclosed afterward by the stronger party. Not carried forward: the marriage, the sisters, the question of Leah's part, and any judgment about Laban's culture.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a narrative pattern applied by analogy. It is not a command. Promise scope: none. Nothing here promises that stating terms early will prevent disputes.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. You manage a small team and promised a staff member a raise after a year of extra duties. At the review, company policy turns out to require a certification first, and you knew the policy existed.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: honesty and justice in dealings. One faithful response: say plainly that you should have mentioned the requirement at the start, honor what you can of the promise now, and put the remaining conditions in writing with a date.

**Cautions.** This concerns an ordinary workplace promise. It does not compare any employee to Leah or any employer to Laban. A real dispute about pay may need a qualified advisor or the relevant labor agency, not a study note.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. Using the table in Literary Design, which echoes of Genesis 27 can you see in the BSB alone, and which appear only in the Hebrew? Does an echo the English hides still carry weight for you?
2. The narrator never says that Laban's deception was a punishment for Jacob's. Henry, Barnes and Keil and Delitzsch say it. What would the text need to contain for that reading to be stated rather than inferred?
3. Leah's first three speeches mention her husband and the fourth does not. Ellicott hears her giving up hope; Genesis Rabbah hears gratitude for more than her share. What in 29:32-35 could support each?
4. Calvin holds that the marriages were wrong even then; Augustine holds that they were no crime under the custom of their time. What in Genesis 2:24 and Leviticus 18:18 would each of them point to, and what do those texts leave unsettled?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 29:1-35 in about eight steps, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) Give Leah's four sons in order with the words that explain each name. (c) Say what Hebrew word stands behind the BSB's "older" in 29:26, and why it matters to the comparison with Genesis 27.
2. **Five-minute outline.** (0:00-1:00) the well and the bargain; (1:00-2:00) the substitution and the Genesis 27 table; (2:00-3:00) the names, and where Levi and Judah lead; (3:00-4:15) Positions 1-4, with the pastoral note as written; (4:15-5:00) the connections.
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: reading Laban's trick as God's retribution blames the victim of a fraud and draws attention away from Laban. Weigh it against Henry's "how unrighteous soever Laban was, the Lord was righteous." A second candidate, running the other way: refusing the retribution reading ignores how closely the scene mirrors Genesis 27. Weigh it against the table, and against Calvin, who comments on 29:25 without drawing the comparison.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) It does not say what Leah knew or wanted on the wedding night; every view in Position 2 is a reader's inference. (b) It does not state that Laban's deception was a punishment; that reading rests on the parallels with Genesis 27. (c) It does not approve or condemn the polygamy it records; the judgments in Position 4 come from later law and later readers. (d) Its naming speeches do not predict priesthood or kingship; those lines come from later texts and readers.

(a) **John 4.** John names "Jacob's well" (John 4:6) and "our father Jacob, who gave us this well" (4:12), and the Genesis 24 lesson's connection reports that Bashaw names Genesis 29 among the well-meeting scenes. (b) **Hebrews 7:5-14.** It names "the sons of Levi who become priests" and says "our Lord descended from Judah," the two lines traced above.
