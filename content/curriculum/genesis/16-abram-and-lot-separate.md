Genesis 13 is a story about too much rather than too little. The previous chapter ended with a famine and a departure from Egypt; this one opens with Abram "extremely wealthy in livestock and silver and gold" (13:2) and his nephew Lot holding "flocks and herds and tents" of his own (13:5), and the land "unable to support both of them" (13:6). Abram proposes a separation and gives Lot the first choice; Lot looks out over the Jordan plain, finds it "well watered like the garden of the LORD" (13:10), and moves toward Sodom, whose men the narrator immediately calls "wicked, sinning greatly against the LORD" (13:13). Then the LORD tells Abram to look in every direction and promises him all of it, "to you and your offspring forever" (13:15). This lesson keeps three things apart that are easily blurred: what this chapter actually says about Lot, which is less than many retellings say; what later readers -- 2 Peter, Rashi's Midrashic sources, and classic Christian commentators -- say about him, which disagrees sharply; and what the word "forever" in 13:15 has been read to promise, a question on which named traditions still divide. Every BSB quotation below, including in the three connection files, was printed from this app's own shipped corpus by script before drafting and checked again by a second script afterward; see Sources and Limits.

## Context

**What happens, in order (13:1-18).** Abram goes up out of Egypt into the Negev with his wife, "all his possessions," and Lot (13:1). He travels back by stages "toward Bethel, until he came to the place between Bethel and Ai where his tent had formerly been pitched, to the site where he had built the altar," and there "Abram called on the name of the LORD" (13:3-4). Lot's herds and Abram's are too large for the land to support together, and "there was discord between the herdsmen of Abram and the herdsmen of Lot" (13:6-7), with a narrator's aside that "the Canaanites and the Perizzites were also living in the land" (13:7). Abram speaks first: "Please let there be no contention between you and me... After all, we are brothers. Is not the whole land before you? Now separate yourself from me. If you go to the left, I will go to the right; if you go to the right, I will go to the left" (13:8-9). Lot looks over "the whole plain of the Jordan, all the way to Zoar" and sees it "well watered like the garden of the LORD, like the land of Egypt," with the narrator adding in parenthesis, "This was before the LORD destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah" (13:10). Lot chooses the plain and sets out "toward the east" (13:11). Abram stays in Canaan; Lot "settled in the cities of the plain and pitched his tent toward Sodom" (13:12). The narrator then says of Sodom's men, not of Lot, that they "were wicked, sinning greatly against the LORD" (13:13). "After Lot had departed," the LORD tells Abram to "lift up your eyes... and look to the north and south and east and west" (13:14), promises "all the land that you see, I will give to you and your offspring forever" (13:15) and offspring "like the dust of the earth" (13:16), and tells him to "walk around the land, through its length and breadth" (13:17). Abram moves his tent to "the Oaks of Mamre at Hebron, where he built an altar to the LORD" (13:18). That is the sequence; what it means is the rest of this lesson's work.

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens (`content/lens/eleven-stages.json`) places Genesis 12 through Malachi in stage 5, "Israel: The Twelve Tribes," with a machine-readable `chapters` field of `["1.12"]`; this chapter is the second unit in that stage. The companion unit on Genesis 12 has already treated the famine, the episode in Egypt, and the dispute over 12:6's "the Canaanites were in the land"; 13:7's similar aside about "the Canaanites and the Perizzites" raises the same question and is not reopened here. The destruction of Sodom and Lot's rescue (Genesis 19) are a later lesson's territory, and are mentioned below only where 2 Peter or a commentator ties them back to this chapter.

**Key words and observations, checked against the text.**

- **Lot is "nephew" in the narrator's voice and "brothers" in Abram's.** Genesis 11:27 names Lot as the son of Abram's brother Haran, and 12:5 calls him "his nephew Lot"; in 13:8 Abram says "we are brothers." The word in Abram's mouth is a kinship appeal, not a correction of the genealogy -- the two statements sit side by side in the text and can be checked against each other.
- **"like the garden of the LORD, like the land of Egypt" (13:10).** Two comparisons stand back to back. The Cambridge Bible for Schools and Colleges states, "“The garden of Jehovah” is the garden of Eden (chap. 2; cf. Isaiah 51:3), the ideal of beauty and fertility," and adds that Egypt "was well known for the richness of its soil and for the abundance of its irrigation." Not every reader agrees the first comparison names Eden. Matthew Poole gives two options -- "Like that famous garden of Eden which God himself planted" or "Like some excellent garden; for excellent things are thus expressed" by attaching God's name -- and the Pulpit Commentary names Calvin, Lange and Keil for the Eden reading, reports that "by some this is deemed unsatisfactory (Quarry)," and names Rosenmüller for reading the phrase as a superlative and Grotius and Junius for reading it as Mesopotamia. A scan of this app's whole BSB text finds the English phrase "the garden of the LORD" in exactly two verses: here and Isaiah 51:3 (see the first reviewed connection). The comparison to Egypt sits two verses after Abram has come "up out of Egypt" (13:1).
- **"all the way to Zoar" -- and a variant reading.** The BSB places Zoar as the far edge of the plain. Two commentators read for this lesson report a different text in the Syriac Peshitta: Ellicott states "The Syriac has preserved the right reading, namely, Zoan" (an Egyptian city), and the Cambridge Bible reports the same reading as one that "would connect the clause with the mention of Egypt." This is reported as two commentators' description of a variant; the Peshitta itself was not checked for this lesson.
- **"toward the east" (13:11).** Ellicott notes that the Hebrew word here "is the word translated “eastward” in Genesis 2:8, and “from the east” in Genesis 11:2." In the BSB those verses read "in the east" (the garden, 2:8) and "journeyed eastward" (the builders of Babel, 11:2). That the same Hebrew word recurs is Ellicott's report; what, if anything, the recurrence signals is not something Ellicott or this lesson claims. Ellicott himself adds, "Here it can only mean towards the east." Rashi, below, reads the same word in a very different way.
- **What 13:10-13 says about Lot, and what it does not.** The text says Lot looked, saw, chose, set out, settled, and "pitched his tent toward Sodom." The only moral evaluation in these four verses is of "the men of Sodom" (13:13). The text does not say whether Lot knew their reputation, does not state his motive beyond what he saw, and states no explicit verdict on him. Whether placing 13:13 directly after 13:12 is itself an implied judgment is read differently: Matthew Poole calls 13:13 "a secret reproof to Lot," and Rashi's Midrashic sources draw their verdict from this chapter's own words (13:10, 13:11, 13:13), while 2 Peter's verdict comes from outside the chapter.
- **The promise grows.** At 12:7 the LORD promised, "I will give this land to your offspring." At 13:15 the grant is "to you and your offspring forever," covers "all the land that you see" in four directions, and is joined to a numbering promise -- offspring "like the dust of the earth" (13:16). Barnes, commenting on that dust promise, writes that "the promise begins already to enlarge itself beyond the bounds of the natural seed of Abram." The Cambridge Bible notes the dust simile recurs at 28:14 and that elsewhere Abram's descendants are compared to the stars (15:5, 22:17, 26:4) and the sand (22:17, 32:12). It also observes that "The promise here includes, in the future possession of Israel, the land which Lot had chosen for himself" -- a checkable consequence of 13:14's "east" against 13:11.

## Literary Design

**The chapter opens and closes at altars.** Abram returns to "the site where he had built the altar" between Bethel and Ai (13:3-4; compare 12:8, "There he built an altar to the LORD, and he called on the name of the LORD"), and the chapter ends with a new altar at Hebron (13:18). The companion Genesis 12 lesson observed that no altar appears during the stay in Egypt (12:10-20); 13:3-4 resumes the pattern at the very spot where it stopped. That framing can be checked against both chapters; why the narrator frames it this way is not stated.

**Two looks.** In 13:10 Lot looks out over the land and chooses for himself; in 13:14 the LORD tells Abram to "lift up your eyes... and look," and then gives him what he sees. The BSB renders the two with different English ("Lot looked out" against "lift up your eyes"), so the echo is not visible in this translation. The Pulpit Commentary reports that the Hebrew uses the same expression in both verses -- of 13:10, "The same expression is afterwards used of Abram (ver. 14)," and of 13:14, "Perhaps a studied reference to the act of Lot, which Moses describes in similar language (ver. 10)." Pulpit's own "perhaps" is kept here: the repeated phrase is reported, the intent behind it is a suggestion.

**A seam, by one reading.** The Cambridge Bible reads 13:11-12 as two literary sources joined: "This is the account according to J. The next two clauses are from P," calling the result "a rare instance of unskilful combination of the two strata of tradition." This is one named critical reading of the verses' composition, reported as such; the commentators who read 13:11-12 as a single narrative are not required to answer it here, since the reading affects how the verses were assembled rather than what they report.

## Positions

Two questions, each reported through named positions in their own words. Neither is decided here. Positions report what traditions and commentators conclude; the Context section above reports what the text itself states.

**1. How should a reader assess Lot -- here, and in light of 2 Peter?** Genesis 13 records Lot's choice without an explicit verdict. Later readers supply very different ones, and the divide does not run neatly between Jewish and Christian readers.

- **Rabbinic Midrash on Genesis 13, as Rashi reports it: Lot's choice to his discredit.** Rashi (trans. Rosenbaum and Silbermann) gives the plain sense first and then the Midrash. On 13:10: "The Midrashic explanation (Horayot 10b; Genesis Rabbah 41:7) explains it to Lot's discredit — just because they (the people of Sodom and Gomorrah) were addicted to lewdness did Lot choose their locality." On 13:11 his plain sense is geographical ("He removed from Abram and went westward of Abram"), but the Midrash reads the same word as pointing to God, "the Originator": "He wandered away from the Originator (מקדמונו) of the Universe, saying, "I want neither Abram nor his God" (Genesis Rabbah 41:7)." On 13:13: "and yet Lot did not refrain from living with them." John Gill, commenting on 2 Peter 2:7, calls the rabbinic sources he cites "very injurious to this good man's character" -- his own polemical word -- but adds that Philo the Jew "speaks better of him, and says that he did not embrace and delight in the iniquities of the inhabitants, though he did not arrive to the perfection of wisdom." Gill's account is a Christian commentator's description of these Jewish sources, not their own words. Rabbinic reading of Lot is not uniformly negative either: on Genesis 19:29, Rashi records a Midrash (Genesis Rabbah 51:6) that God rescued Lot because, knowing Sarah was Abraham's wife, Lot "did not betray him because he had sympathy with him."
- **2 Peter and the Wisdom of Solomon: "righteous."** 2 Peter 2:7-8 calls Lot "a righteous man distressed by the depraved conduct of the lawless" and says "that righteous man, living among them day after day, was tormented in his righteous soul by the lawless deeds he saw and heard." Gill quotes the Wisdom of Solomon (10:6), a Hellenistic Jewish book, canonical for Catholic and Orthodox readers, using the same word: "When the ungodly perished, she delivered the righteous man, who fled from the fire which fell down upon the five cities." (Wisdom is not in this app's BSB corpus; the wording here is Gill's.) Barnes notes that the inner distress Peter describes "is not mentioned in Genesis."
- **Classic Christian commentators: a culpable choice and a righteous man.** Most commentators read for this lesson keep both. Matthew Henry: "Lot looked to the goodness of the land," "Lot little considered the badness of the inhabitants," and those who suffer among wicked neighbors suffer more "if, as Lot here, they have brought it upon themselves by a wrong choice." Jamieson-Fausset-Brown: "He seems, though a good man, to have been too much under the influence of a selfish and covetous spirit." Ellicott, on 2 Peter 2:8, names the tension directly -- the word "seems at first sight to be at variance with his willingness to remain, for the sake of worldly advantages, in the midst of such wickedness" -- and resolves it with a phrase his text opens with a quotation mark, so he may be quoting or paraphrasing another writer -- "righteous is a relative term" -- concluding that the word is "by no means without warrant." The Expositor's Greek Testament reads Peter's own grammar as already containing the criticism: "May it not be that in the use of the active a certain sense of personal culpability is implied? Lot was conscious that the situation was ultimately due to his own selfish choice (cf. von Soden)." Barnes, on the same verse, offers the most generous reading: "Perhaps it was one purpose of his remaining to endeavor to do them good."

These readings agree on what the text records and disagree about the man. A reader weighing them is weighing later readings that reach opposite verdicts -- rabbinic Midrash building on this chapter's own wording, 2 Peter speaking from outside it -- alongside commentators, Jewish and Christian, who soften or complicate both. None of them can be settled from Genesis 13 alone, because Genesis 13 states no explicit verdict on Lot; whether its arrangement of 13:12-13 implies one is itself part of what these readers dispute.

**2. What does "to you and your offspring forever" (13:15) promise?** This question carries real present-day weight, since the land in view is the same land over which political and religious disputes continue. The positions below are reported as each source states its view; this lesson does not rank them and takes no position on any modern political question.

- **A Jewish medieval reading: expulsion of the nations, then a lasting return after exile.** Radak (Rabbi David Kimhi; trans. Eliyahu Munk) reads the grant as legally made to Abram at once: "The fact that at the moment this land was under the sovereignty of the Canaanites was legally irrelevant." He reads "forever" first as the expulsion of the Canaanite nations -- "they would expel the nations living here from this land" -- and then as a final return: "Even though, eventually, the Israelites themselves would be exiled from their land, in the end they would return there on a permanent enduring basis."
- **A dispensational premillennial reading: unconditional and literal.** John F. Walvoord (*Bibliotheca Sacra*, 1952) cites 13:15 directly and argues that "the words “for ever” (Gen 13:15), “everlasting covenant” (Gen 17:7), and “everlasting possession” (Gen 17:8) carries with it the necessity of complete and unconditional fulfillment." He states his school's position: "Premillenarians consider the promises as given unconditionally as far as ultimate fulfillment is concerned and therefore hold that Israel has a bona fide ground for future possession of the land, particularly in the millennial kingdom period" -- a claim about a future kingdom, not about any present-day arrangement. He concludes that "Israel as such will possess the land," characterizes opposing views in his own terms ("amillenarians tend either to make these promises conditional, and therefore not requiring fulfillment, or to spiritualize them"), and allows that "the present enjoyment of it by an individual or a nation may have certain conditions."
- **A reading of the promise as fulfilled in a spiritual seed.** Keil and Delitzsch read "forever" as applying "not to the lineal posterity of Abram, to his seed according to the flesh, but to the true spiritual seed, which embraced the promise in faith," adding that the promise neither "precluded the expulsion of the unbelieving seed from the land of Canaan, nor guarantees to existing Jews a return to the earthly Palestine after their conversion to Christ," and frame their own reading positively: "Through Christ the promise has been exalted from its temporal form to its true essence; through Him the whole earth becomes Canaan." They cite Calvin here only in untranslated Latin; Calvin's own reading is reported in the next group.
- **Readings that make "forever" conditional or time-limited (until the Messiah).** Calvin, in his own commentary on 13:15: "In promising the land “for ever,” he does not simply denote perpetuity; but that period which was brought to a close by the advent of Christ." Matthew Poole answers in two steps: "This promise was made to them upon condition of their obedience," and the Hebrew word "doth not always signify eternity, but a long continuance," lasting "until the coming of the Messias; and so it may here be understood." Benson follows Poole closely: "the promise was made to them, and intended to be fulfilled, upon condition of their obedience," the phrase rendered "for ever" "often signifies only long continuance," lasting "till the coming of the Messiah. And thus it may be here understood." Barnes: "The term “forever,” for a perpetual possession, means as long as the order of things to which it belongs lasts."
- **A historical fulfillment until the Messiah, then a future one.** Gill shares the "until the Messiah" endpoint -- the grant was given to Abram's posterity "to be enjoyed by them until the Messiah came" -- and adds a future fulfillment "which Abram in person shall enjoy, with all his spiritual seed, after the resurrection." Gill's reading is millennial like Walvoord's, but its future heirs are Abram "with all his spiritual seed," where Walvoord's are Israel as a nation; it also differs from Keil and Delitzsch, who expect no future possession of the earthly land.

The text itself gives the grant, its four directions, the word "forever," and the command to walk the land. It does not, in this chapter, state any condition, define the "offspring," or say how a grant to Abram personally is to be squared with Stephen's later statement that God "gave him no inheritance here, not even a foot of ground" (Acts 7:5) -- a tension the Pulpit Commentary and Poole (followed by Benson) both raise and answer differently. Each position above is an answer to those silences. Readings of this promise have been used both to deny Jewish ties to the land and to dismiss the claims of its other inhabitants; reporting a position here is not endorsing any such use. A reader for whom this question touches family, faith or community -- and for many it does -- should take it to a pastor, rabbi, imam, or teacher in their own tradition; a study note can report the positions but cannot settle them.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from the passage in its own setting to a modern situation. It models the shape; it is not an answer key, and the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate step.

**Passage element.** Genesis 13:6-9. The land cannot support both households, the herdsmen quarrel, and Abram speaks first: he names the conflict ("Please let there be no contention"), appeals to the relationship ("we are brothers"), and offers the other party the first choice ("If you go to the left, I will go to the right").

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text records that the strain is material (13:6, "they had so many possessions"), that it shows up first among the workers (13:7), and that Abram is the one who proposes the solution and gives up the choice. Inference: for hearers who knew Abram as the head of the clan and Lot as the junior relative, an elder handing the younger man first choice of the land would likely have registered as unusual; one reading, the Cambridge Bible's, holds that Lot's selection "disregarded the rights of Abram his senior." The text itself does not comment on Abram's offer.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When a shared arrangement can no longer hold, the person with more standing can sometimes end the conflict by naming it plainly, putting the relationship ahead of the dispute, and accepting a less favorable share -- without that choice being forced on anyone else as a rule.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a real resource strain, a relationship worth protecting, and a choice about who chooses first. Not carried forward: the specific land, the divine promise that follows in 13:14-17, and any guarantee that giving up the better share will be repaid the way 13:15 promised Abram.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed; 13:14-17 is a promise made to Abram and is not treated here as a promise that generosity in a modern dispute will be rewarded.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family. Two adult siblings jointly inherit a parent's small rental property. One wants to sell; the other wants to keep it. The tension has started to spread to their spouses and children.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: peacemaking. One faithful response is for one sibling to say plainly that the relationship matters more than the property, propose a clean division, and offer the other the first choice of the options -- while being honest with themselves about what they are willing to give up.

**Cautions.** Inheritance and jointly owned property involve legal and tax questions; a sibling in this situation needs an attorney or a qualified financial adviser, not a study note. Yielding is not always right: where one party is being pressured, exploited or abused, giving way is not peacemaking, and a trusted outside person or professional help is the right next step. Not every reader treats Abram's offer as simply admirable: Radak, on 13:14, reads the LORD's words there as containing "an implied criticism of Avram having offered Lot a choice which would have made him owner of part of the land of Canaan." The bridge uses the offer as one model to test, not as the text's endorsement of it. The passage is not a property-law text; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing and may find does not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Genesis 13:10-13 records what Lot saw and where he went, and passes explicit judgment only on "the men of Sodom." Rashi's Midrashic sources on Genesis 13 judge Lot harshly; 2 Peter calls him "righteous" three times. If you had only Genesis 13 in front of you, what in its wording would you point to before reaching any verdict on Lot at all?
2. The same word for "east" that Ellicott traces to Genesis 2:8 and 11:2 is read by Rashi's Midrash as pointing to God, "the Originator". Both readings start from the same Hebrew word. What would you need to know to decide whether a recurring word is doing deliberate work in a narrative, or is simply the ordinary word for a direction?
3. In Genesis 13 a land "like the garden of the LORD" is about to be destroyed (13:10); in Isaiah 51:3 a ruined land is to be made "like the garden of the LORD." The reviewed connection grades the shared phrase `plausible`. Does the reversed direction of movement strengthen or weaken the case that the two passages are related, in your judgment?
4. Positions (2) reports five groups of readings of "forever" in 13:15. Which of them, if any, takes Acts 7:5 ("He gave him no inheritance here, not even a foot of ground") most seriously, and which least -- and how would a reader in Radak's tradition answer differently?

## Teach-Back Prompts

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate Genesis 13:1-18 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) State in the text's own words what Abram offers Lot (13:8-9) and what the LORD promises Abram (13:14-17), before saying what any tradition concludes from either. (c) Name one place elsewhere in Scripture where this chapter's people, places or phrases visibly reappear, and say what kind of connection it is.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the plain sequence and where the chapter sits, between the Egypt episode of Genesis 12 and the Sodom narrative of Genesis 19 (Context); (1:00-2:00) the two comparisons in 13:10 and the BSB-wide scan for "the garden of the LORD" (Key Words); (2:00-3:00) the two looks, the altar frame, and the reported seam at 13:11-12 (Literary Design); (3:00-4:00) the two named disputes, about Lot and about "forever," held open on purpose (Positions); (4:00-4:30) where the chapter travels later in Scripture, naming each connection's evidence label (prompt 5); (4:30-5:00) the boundary, stated last (prompt 4).
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then answer it. Form your own first. If you are stuck, here is one: by reporting Rashi's Midrash ("I want neither Abram nor his God") alongside 2 Peter, a lesson for Christian learners puts a non-canonical tradition on the same footing as an apostolic letter, and may leave a learner doubting 2 Peter's verdict. Before answering, weigh what Positions (1) does: it reports both as later readings of a chapter that itself states no explicit verdict, and notes that Christian commentators such as Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown and the Expositor's Greek Testament criticize Lot's choice too. A second candidate, running the other direction: reporting Walvoord, Radak and Keil and Delitzsch side by side on "forever" may look even-handed while ignoring the question a reader most wants answered -- what the promise means for the land today. Ask whether a study note that declines to answer that question is being honest about its limits or avoiding a hard question.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) Genesis 13 states no explicit verdict on Lot. Its one explicit moral judgment falls on "the men of Sodom" (13:13); whether Lot was "righteous" (2 Peter 2:7-8) or chose Sodom for its sin (Rashi's Midrash) is decided, if at all, by later readers -- the Midrash building on this chapter's own wording, 2 Peter speaking from outside it. (b) Genesis 13:15 does not, in its own wording, define "your offspring," state a condition, or say how "forever" relates to later exile. Every reading in Positions (2) supplies those from other passages or from a wider theological framework.

- **BSB text.** Every BSB quotation in this lesson and its three connection files was printed from this app's shipped corpus (`web/public/bible/BSB/1.json` Genesis, `5.json` Deuteronomy, `23.json` Isaiah, `44.json` Acts, `45.json` Romans, `58.json` Hebrews, `61.json` 2 Peter) by a Node script before drafting, and a second script confirmed every quoted fragment is a literal substring of the verse it is attributed to. The claim that "the garden of the LORD" appears exactly twice comes from a script scan of all 66 books of the same corpus, in English; the Hebrew phrase was not independently counted.
- **Classic commentaries.** Ellicott, Benson, Matthew Henry, Barnes, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, Matthew Poole, Gill, the Cambridge Bible, the Pulpit Commentary, Keil and Delitzsch, Meyer and the Expositor's Greek Testament are quoted from BibleHub's aggregator pages for Genesis 13:10, 13:11, 13:13, 13:14, 13:15, 13:16, 2 Peter 2:7, 2:8, Romans 4:13 and Isaiah 51:3, fetched by raw HTTP request and checked word for word against the fetched text. These pages reproduce public-domain commentaries; no printed edition was consulted. On the aggregated Genesis 13:15 page, a block of question-and-answer comment on "forever" and Acts 7:5 appears without a commentator's heading. Matthew Poole's own Genesis 13 page on BibleHub, fetched separately, carries the same text, so it is attributed to Poole here; Benson's comment follows Poole closely. Calvin is quoted from BibleHub's reproduction of his Genesis commentary, checked against an independent reviewer's fetch of that page, not against a critical edition.
- **Named scholars reported secondhand.** Calvin, Lange, Keil, Quarry, Rosenmüller, Grotius and Junius (the "garden of the LORD" dispute) are reported only as the Pulpit Commentary names them. Keil and Delitzsch cite Calvin on "forever" only in untranslated Latin; the English Calvin quotation in Positions (2) comes from his own commentary page, not from Keil and Delitzsch. Von Soden is reported as the Expositor's Greek Testament cites him. None of their own works was read.
- **Rashi** is quoted from the Rosenbaum and Silbermann translation on Sefaria. Sefaria blocks raw scripted requests, so the comments on 13:10, 13:11 and 13:13 were fetched with a web-fetch tool; 13:10 and 13:11 were re-fetched as raw JSON and matched word for word. The Midrashic sources Rashi cites (Genesis Rabbah 41:7, Horayot 10b, Yoma 38b) were not read directly. Gill's description of the wider rabbinic view of Lot is reported as Gill's.
- **Radak** on 13:15 is quoted from Eliyahu Munk's translation on Sefaria, fetched as raw JSON and checked word for word, including both clauses of his comment on "forever"; Radak on 13:14 and Rashi on 19:29 were checked against an independent reviewer's raw Sefaria fetches. The Midrash Rashi cites at 19:29 (Genesis Rabbah 51:6) was not read directly. No other Jewish commentator on 13:15 was read: Sforno's and Ramban's entries for the verse returned no English text, and Ibn Ezra's comment concerns only how land can be "given" by word. Modern Jewish readings of the land promise were not researched for this lesson. That is a gap in sourcing, not evidence that no such readings exist.
- **Walvoord** was read in full from the republication of his 1952 *Bibliotheca Sacra* article on walvoord.com. No covenant-theology or amillennial author was read directly in their own modern words; that side of Positions (2) rests on Keil and Delitzsch and on Calvin's own commentary. A modern author from that tradition writing on Genesis 13:15 is the obvious next source to add.
- **Wisdom 10:6** is quoted as Gill reproduces it; it is not in this app's BSB corpus, and no edition of Wisdom was checked.
- **The Zoan reading** in the Syriac Peshitta is reported through Ellicott and the Cambridge Bible; the Peshitta itself was not consulted.
