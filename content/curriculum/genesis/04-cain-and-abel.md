Genesis 4:1-16 is the first murder, the first recorded worship, and the first time the text states a divine verdict ("had no regard") without stating the reason for it. That silence is the hinge this lesson turns on: a careful reader has to notice what Genesis 4 does and does not explain, because almost every later tradition -- including the New Testament itself -- fills the silence with a reason of its own. Distinguishing the text's own bare statement from a later interpreter's added reason is not a trick for debunking those later readings; several of them are the New Testament's own words, cited here as such. It is a discipline for knowing, at every point, which kind of claim you are holding. This lesson also carries a second, heavier reason for that same discipline: a popular reading of the mark God puts on Cain in 4:15 was used for centuries to justify slavery and racial discrimination, by filling the text's silence about *what* the mark was with an answer the text never gives. Getting the "text says / tradition adds" distinction right is not only an academic exercise.

## Context

**What happens, in order (4:1-16).** Adam and Eve conceive Cain; Eve credits the LORD (4:1). Abel is born after him; Abel keeps sheep, Cain works the soil (4:2). In time, Cain brings an offering from his crops and Abel brings "the best portions of the firstborn of his flock" (4:3-4). The LORD regards Abel's offering but not Cain's; Cain is angry, and his face falls (4:4-5). The LORD questions Cain and warns him that sin is "crouching at your door," desiring him, and that he must master it (4:6-7). Cain tells Abel to go out to the field with him, and there kills him (4:8). The LORD asks Cain where Abel is; Cain denies knowing and asks, "Am I my brother's keeper?" (4:9). The LORD states that Abel's blood cries out from the ground and sentences Cain to a curse on his farming and a wandering life (4:10-12). Cain protests that the sentence is more than he can bear and that he will be killed by whoever finds him (4:13-14). The LORD answers "Not so," promises sevenfold vengeance on anyone who kills Cain, and places a mark on him so that no one who finds him will kill him (4:15). Cain leaves the LORD's presence and settles in the land of Nod, east of Eden (4:16). This is a plain retelling of what happens, in the text's own sequence; what each piece means is the rest of this lesson's work.

**Where Genesis 4 sits.** Genesis 4 continues directly from Genesis 3 within the "Primeval History" (see the Genesis 3 lesson's Context section on that unit and its disputed boundary). This app's own eleven-stage lens groups Genesis 3-5 together as one stage ("The Serpent, the Exile, and the First Deaths"), reading Cain's murder of Abel as the first of the "first deaths" the stage title names -- that stage grouping is this app's own reading of the canon's shape, not a claim the text asserts about itself, and this lesson does not depend on it. Literarily, 4:1-16 tracks Genesis 3's own shape at a smaller scale: a test involving two brothers instead of two spouses, a sin named explicitly in advance (4:7, unlike 3:1-6), an interrogation by the LORD ("Where is your brother Abel?" echoing "Where are you?" in 3:9), a curse on the ground tied to the perpetrator's own labor (4:11-12, echoing 3:17-19), and an expulsion to a place named "east of Eden" (4:16), which is the same direction Genesis 3:24 already used for the cherubim stationed "on the east side of the Garden of Eden." That repeated direction is a detail any reader can check in the text itself; nothing further is claimed about it here.

**Key words, read as observations.**

- **קָנָה** (*qanah*, "to get, acquire") underlies Eve's naming speech: "With the help of the LORD I have brought forth a man" (4:1, BSB). The verb *qanah* and the name Cain (*Qayin*) share consonants, and this wordplay is reported by name in the secondary literature (Nachmanides, via Wikipedia's survey article) -- it is not this lesson's own discovery, and the Hebrew pun does not survive into the BSB's English wording.
- **הֶבֶל** (*hevel*, "breath, vapor") is the name Abel, and the same word supplies Ecclesiastes' refrain "vanity of vanities" (Ecclesiastes 1:2). Whether this is a deliberate naming -- a life as brief as a breath -- or a coincidence of a common root is not settled by the lexicon itself; it is a pattern worth noticing, not a fact the text states about its own wordplay.
- **מִנְחָה** (*minchah*, "gift, offering, tribute") is the one word Genesis 4:3-5 uses for both Cain's and Abel's gifts -- the text does not use two different words to distinguish a "grain offering" from an "animal offering" the way Leviticus's later technical vocabulary does. Whatever distinguished the two offerings, the word choice itself does not announce it.
- **שָׁעָה** (*sha'ah*, "to gaze at, regard") is the same verb behind both "the LORD looked with favor on Abel" (4:4) and "He had no regard for Cain" (4:5) -- one verb, used positively of one brother and negatively of the other, in successive clauses. The text pairs the verb; it does not explain the pairing.
- **רָבַץ** (*rabats*, "to crouch, lie stretched out," about 30 occurrences) is the participle behind "sin is crouching at your door" (4:7). The same root elsewhere describes an animal lying in readiness (Genesis 49:9, 14), and a related Akkadian word, *rabisu*, names a type of demon said to crouch at doorways -- a real, checkable cognate, reported here as a cognate, not as proof of what the Hebrew author intended. The Hebrew grammar of the clause is itself disputed and is treated below (Positions).
- **תְּשׁוּקָה** (*teshuqah*, "desire") and **מָשַׁל** (*mashal*, "to rule, master") are the same two Hebrew words, in the same order, in 3:16 ("Your desire will be for your husband, and he will rule over you") and in 4:7 ("it desires you, but you must master it"). The BSB renders *mashal* as "rule" in 3:16 and "master" in 4:7 -- one Hebrew verb, two different English verbs, the same pattern the Genesis 3 lesson already flagged for *shuph* in 3:15. That the same two-word pair recurs across the two chapters is directly checkable; what the recurrence is meant to do is a genuine question, treated in Literary Design below.
- **אוֹת** (*'oth*, "sign, mark, token," about 79 occurrences) is the word behind "the LORD placed a mark on Cain" (4:15). The same word names the rainbow in Genesis 9:12-13 and circumcision in Genesis 17:11 -- both communicative, covenantal signs, not physical markings on skin. That the word's ordinary range in Genesis is "sign," not "skin pigmentation," is directly checkable against those other two uses and bears on the Positions discussion of the mark below.

**A textual fact worth flagging as a textual-critical observation, not a theological one.** The Hebrew of 4:8 reads, in a woodenly literal rendering, "And Cain said to Abel his brother. And it happened, when they were in the field..." -- a verb of speaking with no quoted words following it. The Samaritan Pentateuch, the Septuagint, the Peshitta, and the Vulgate all supply "let us go out to the field" (the clause the BSB and most English translations print as 4:8's opening line); the Dead Sea Scroll fragment 4QGen-b, otherwise very close to the Masoretic Text, lacks the clause. Peter J. Williams (Evangelical Textual Criticism) argues the Peshitta's own wording is better explained as translated from the Greek than from a Hebrew text that already had the clause, which would count as evidence the clause is not original to the Hebrew rather than an independent Hebrew witness for it. This lesson does not resolve whether the clause was original; it reports that English Bibles supply words ancient witnesses themselves disagree about, which is worth knowing before treating "let us go out to the field" as a settled part of what Cain said.

## Positions

**1. Why was Abel's offering regarded and Cain's was not?** Genesis 4:4-5 states the fact -- "the LORD looked with favor on Abel and his offering, but He had no regard for Cain and his offering" -- without stating the reason. One textual asymmetry is directly checkable: 4:4 qualifies Abel's gift as "the best portions of the firstborn of his flock," while 4:3 states only that Cain "brought some of the fruit of the soil," with no comparable qualifier. Named readings of what that asymmetry means, or whether it is the whole explanation, differ:

- **The offering itself was deficient.** David Guzik's commentary (Enduring Word) notes that grain offerings are elsewhere acceptable to God (citing Leviticus 2), so the *type* of offering was not automatically disqualifying, but holds that Abel's gift -- "of the firstborn of his flock and of their fat" -- was "extra special" in a way Cain's unqualified produce was not, and that "the offering of Cain was no doubt more aesthetically pleasing" while God was "more concerned with faith in the heart than with artistic beauty."
- **The deciding factor was faith, not the offering's content.** Hebrews 11:4 states plainly, "By faith Abel offered God a better sacrifice than Cain did" -- a reason the Genesis text itself does not state in those words (see the reviewed connection to Hebrews 11:4, below).
- **The deciding factor was Cain's heart or deeds, read backward from his anger and the murder that follows.** An anonymous aggregator commentary (BibleRef.com) argues "it seems more likely that God rejected Cain's offering because of Cain's heart and not merely because of the physical offering Cain brought," pointing to 1 John 3:12's "his own deeds were evil" as its basis (see the reviewed connection to 1 John 3:11-12, below).
- **A much later, non-biblical elaboration: the brothers disputed theology first.** Targum Pseudo-Jonathan expands 4:8 into an argument in which Cain denies that the world is justly governed, that there is a future judgment, and that the righteous are rewarded, while Abel affirms all three -- a full theological debate the Hebrew text of Genesis itself does not contain. This is a late interpretive tradition filling a narrative gap, reported here as exactly that, not as information Genesis 4 supplies.

These readings are not mutually exclusive, and this lesson does not rank them. What can be said without ranking them: Genesis 4:4-5 states the "regard" and "no regard" as facts without stating a reason, and every explanation offered above adds information from outside those two verses -- some of it from elsewhere in the New Testament's own stated terms, some of it from a scholar's inference, some of it from a much later paraphrase.

**2. What does "sin is crouching at your door... you must master it" mean (4:7)?** This clause is a recognized crux: the Hebrew subject "sin" (*chattat*) is grammatically feminine, while "crouching" (*rovets*) is a masculine participle, and the two do not agree in gender -- a real grammatical fact, not an interpretation of one. Readings differ on how to resolve it:

- **The majority reading: sin personified as a predator (or demon) at the door.** Most modern English translations, including the BSB, render the verse as warning Cain that sin itself is lying in wait for him, with the *rabats*/Akkadian-demon cognate noted above cited as background. On this reading, the grammatical mismatch is simply left as an unresolved roughness in the Hebrew.
- **A minority reading: *chattat* here means "sin offering," not "sin."** Peter Goeman (professor of Old Testament, Shepherds Theological Seminary) argues that the same Hebrew word denotes "sin offering" elsewhere (it is the ordinary term throughout Leviticus), that the masculine grammar fits a male sacrificial animal rather than the abstract feminine noun "sin," and that the verse should be read as God telling Cain a sin offering is available to him -- "there is a sin offering available for you to use to cover that transgression" -- a reading of mercy rather than warning. Goeman reports this reading as held earlier by Adam Clarke, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, and Adoniram Judson, and more recently by Michael Morales, and notes Young's Literal Translation as the one modern translation that renders it this way; none of those earlier citations were independently checked against their own texts for this lesson (see Sources and Limits).
- **Rashi reads "crouching" as a statement about the durability of sin's record, not a predator image**, glossing the clause "right up to the door of your grave your sin will be preserved," and reads "you must master it" as a real, achievable victory over "the evil inclination," citing the Talmud (Kiddushin 30b) for the claim that a person *can* overcome it.
- **The Septuagint renders the whole verse differently again**, in a cultic register: "If you offered rightly but did not divide rightly, have you not sinned?" -- treating the problem as a mistake in how Cain performed the offering, not a warning about an external threat at all.

This lesson does not decide among these; the grammatical mismatch behind the dispute is independently checkable by anyone reading the Hebrew interlinearly, which is the point of flagging it as a crux rather than a single settled meaning.

**3. Who was Cain's wife, and who are the people he fears in 4:14, and who built or populated the city of 4:17?** Genesis 4:14 has Cain say, "whoever finds me will kill me," and 4:17 (just past this lesson's own passage boundary, but part of the same episode) has Cain's wife bear Enoch and Cain build a city. The text itself does not say where the wife or the "whoever" came from; Genesis 5:4 states, outside chapter 4, that Adam "had other sons and daughters" over his remaining centuries, without naming them. Named positions on how to fill that gap:

- **The sister (or near relative) reading.** This is the oldest and most widely held answer in both Jewish and Christian tradition -- Wikipedia's survey reports it as "accepted for at least 1,800 years," with the Book of Jubilees naming Cain's wife Awan as his sister, and a statement attributed to Rabbi Joshua ben Karha in rabbinic tradition describing Cain and Abel each as born with a twin sister. None of these sources is Genesis 4 itself; they are later traditions supplying a name and a relationship the text does not give.
- **A young-earth population argument.** Answers in Genesis argues that, over what it reads as centuries before Cain's exile, Adam and Eve's unnamed sons and daughters (Genesis 5:4) would have multiplied into a population large enough to supply both Cain's wife and the "whoever" he fears, without requiring any humans outside Adam and Eve's line.
- **What the text itself does not do:** it does not say Cain feared a population descended from any source other than Adam's family, and -- as the Genesis 3 lesson's placeIds already establish for Eden and 3:20's naming of Eve "the mother of all the living" -- this app's content does not present a pre-Adamic human population as a reading Genesis itself offers; no source consulted for this lesson proposes that reading as a live scholarly position, only as a hypothetical some apologetics sources raise in order to reject it.

**4. What was the mark on Cain, and what was it for?** Genesis 4:15 states the mark's *purpose* plainly: "so that no one who found him would kill him" -- it is protective, not punitive; Cain's actual sentence is the exile and the cursed ground of 4:11-12, stated two verses earlier. The text never states what the mark physically was. Later tradition has filled that silence in several different, sometimes contradictory, ways:

- **A letter of God's own name on Cain's forehead** -- Rashi's reading, and also the reading in Targum Pseudo-Jonathan ("the Lord sealed upon the face of Cain the mark of the Name great and honourable"), citing Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer.
- **A horn growing from Cain's head**, or **a dog given to accompany him as protection** -- both attributed to named rabbis in classical Jewish tradition (Abba Jose ben Hanan and Abba Arika, respectively, per Wikipedia's survey of the tradition).
- **A bodily tremor or shaking** -- a reading drawn from the Septuagint's own rendering of 4:12, "groaning and trembling," which some later interpreters read back onto the mark itself rather than onto the wandering life described in that verse.

**A reading that must be named and rejected on textual grounds, not merely disapproved of.** At some point after the start of the trans-Atlantic slave trade, the mark was reinterpreted by some American and European teachers as dark skin -- a reading with no basis in any of the ancient traditions listed above, none of which identify the mark with skin color. Brigham Young is reported to have stated of Black people, "What is the mark? You will see it on the countenance of every African you ever did see," and the reading was used by some Southern Baptist ministers and others to defend slavery and later segregation. Daniel Heimbach (Southeastern Baptist Theological Seminary) refutes the reading on textual grounds reported by Wikipedia's survey: Cain's own line did not survive the flood (so no mark of his could have been inherited by any people group alive afterward), the Hebrew word for "mark" (*'oth*, noted above) never denotes skin color anywhere else in the Hebrew Bible, and the text never states that the mark was hereditary at all. The Southern Baptist Convention formally denounced racism in 1995, and the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints has issued a statement disavowing the theory that dark skin is a sign of divine curse. This lesson names this reading, and the real, serious harm attached to it, because the discipline this lesson teaches -- distinguishing what a text states from what a reader supplies -- is not only an academic exercise; this is the clearest case in this passage where getting that distinction wrong had real victims.

## Literary Design

**A plausible intra-canon echo, offered as a pattern to test, not a cited finding.** The recurrence of *teshuqah* ("desire") and *mashal* ("rule"/"master"), in the same order, in 3:16 and 4:7, is directly checkable (see Context, above). What the recurrence is *for* is read differently even among those who notice it: Marg Mowczko's comparison of the two verses argues the echo is thin -- in 3:16, "desire" and "rule" describe a relationship between the woman and her husband and the "rule" is not commanded; in 4:7, "desire" and "master" describe Cain's relationship to sin, and "master" is something Cain is directly commanded to do, making the two contexts different enough that, in her words, "the light that 4:7 sheds on 3:16 is minimal." A reader could instead propose that the two passages are meant to be read against each other -- the second occurrence modeling a kind of mastery the first occurrence's "rule" never achieves -- but that is this lesson's own hypothesis offered for testing, not a cited finding, and it is exactly the kind of chiasm-or-pattern claim the Genesis 3 lesson already cautioned against overreading without named criteria.

**A structural echo with Genesis 3, reported as a pattern a reader can check line by line.** Both units follow a similar shape: a wrong is done; the LORD interrogates the responsible party with a direct question ("Where are you?" in 3:9; "Where is your brother Abel?" in 4:9); the guilty party deflects responsibility (the man blames the woman and, implicitly, God who "gave" her, in 3:12; Cain denies knowledge outright and answers a question with a question, "Am I my brother's keeper?" in 4:9); the LORD states a sentence that curses the ground in relation to the perpetrator's own labor (3:17-19; 4:11-12); and the chapter ends with the person sent out to a place named in relation to Eden by direction ("east," in both 3:24 and 4:16). Each of these is independently checkable against the two chapters' own wording. What the pattern is *for* -- authorial design, a shared oral-narrative convention, or simply how any two "sin and its consequences" stories in the same book tend to be told -- is not settled by noticing the pattern itself, and this lesson does not claim more for it than that.

**The envy motif is a very old addition, not Genesis 4's own stated motive.** Genesis 4:5-6 states only that Cain was angry and that his countenance fell after his offering was not regarded; the text never uses a word for envy, jealousy, or resentment of Abel specifically. 1 Clement, writing to the Corinthian church around the end of the first century, reads the episode as a case study in envy: "You see, brethren, how envy and jealousy led to the murder of a brother" (1 Clement 4), and goes on to trace the same vice through Jacob, Joseph, Moses, and others. This is one of the earliest extant Christian readings of Genesis 4 outside the New Testament itself, and it is already an inference beyond what 4:5-6 states -- a textual fact about how early the "envy" reading appears, not a claim that the inference is wrong.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key: the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step, and a different passage element, situation, or response would be equally legitimate.

**Passage element.** Genesis 4:6-7. After Cain's anger, the LORD asks him directly, "Why are you angry... and why has your countenance fallen? If you do what is right, will you not be accepted? But if you refuse to do what is right, sin is crouching at your door; it desires you, but you must master it" (4:6-7, BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the LORD's question comes *before* the murder, while Cain still has a choice in front of him -- the warning is not a verdict on Cain but an intervention offered at the exact moment his anger could still go either way. Inference: for an audience reading this directly after Genesis 3's account of a temptation that was not interrupted by any such warning, 4:6-7 shows that anger at an unwelcome outcome is itself a moment the text treats as still open, not yet a sin, with a named alternative ("doing what is right") still available. The Context section above supplies what the text itself says; nothing in it lets this lesson say more about what first hearers took from the warning.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** A surge of anger at an outcome one did not want is itself a decision point, not yet the wrong done; naming what is "crouching" in that moment, before acting on it, is a real interval a person can use.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a person experiences a strong, unwelcome emotional reaction to an outcome, and there is a real interval between that reaction and whatever they do next. Not carried forward: the LORD's audible voice, the specific stakes of worship acceptance, and anything specific to Cain and Abel's relationship. The bridge holds only where these features match the modern case.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed; nothing in 4:6-7 is treated here as a promise that every such interval will be used well, or that mastering the impulse is automatic or easy.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. An employee's project proposal is turned down in favor of a colleague's, with no explanation given for the choice -- the manager's reasoning, like the LORD's "had no regard" in 4:5, is not stated to the person on the receiving end.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: self-governance in the interval between a reaction and an action (not a form of repentance, since no wrong has yet been done at this point). One faithful response is to notice the anger and the falling countenance as data, name them to a trusted person or in private reflection before any conversation with the colleague or manager, and choose what to do next deliberately rather than from the unexamined reaction itself.

**Cautions.** This example does not say the anger itself was wrong to feel, any more than 4:6's question treats Cain's anger as already sinful -- the warning comes before the act, not as a diagnosis of the feeling. It should not be used to tell someone their anger at a genuinely unjust outcome is illegitimate; 4:5 does not explain why Cain's offering had no regard, and this bridge does not assume the modern colleague's decision was fair, only that a real interval exists before a reaction becomes an act. The passage is not a workplace-management text; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing, and the learner may find it does not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Genesis 4:4-5 states that the LORD regarded Abel's offering and not Cain's, without stating why. What in the text itself -- apart from Hebrews 11:4, 1 John 3:12, or any other later text -- would count as evidence for one explanation over another?
2. The word *'oth* ("sign/mark") also names the rainbow (9:12-13) and circumcision (17:11), both of which are visible to other people and communicate something. If the mark on Cain works the same way, what would it need to communicate, and to whom, for 4:15's stated purpose ("so that no one who found him would kill him") to work?
3. 4:9's "Am I my brother's keeper?" is a question, not a claim; the text never records the LORD answering it directly. What in the rest of Genesis -- or the rest of Scripture -- bears on how that question is meant to be heard?
4. If the teshuqah/mashal recurrence between 3:16 and 4:7 is deliberate, what would be different about how a reader should take 4:7's command to "master" sin? If it is not deliberate, what else could explain two uses of the same rare word pair in consecutive chapters?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate what happens in Genesis 4:1-16 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) State what 4:4-5 says about the two offerings in the text's own words, before saying what any tradition concludes about why one was regarded and the other was not. (c) Name one place elsewhere in Scripture where this passage's language or imagery (the offering, the blood, the mark, "my brother's keeper") visibly reappears, and say what kind of connection it is.

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** Outline how you would teach this passage in five minutes, with at least one point in each segment you could cite to a source or a verse. A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the plain sequence of the narrative and where it sits after Genesis 3 (see Context); (1:00-2:30) the offering and the sin-crouching warning, read first for what the text states and then for the named positions on what it means (see Positions 1-2); (2:30-3:30) the mark of Cain, read first for its stated purpose and then for the racist misreading this lesson names and rejects on textual grounds (see Positions 4); (3:30-4:30) where this passage's language travels later in Scripture, naming the evidence label of each example, not just listing it (see the reviewed connections in prompt 5); (4:30-5:00) the boundary, stated on purpose as the last thing taught (prompt 4).

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first; the discipline of this prompt is that you do the forming, not that you pick from a list. If you are stuck, here is one worked candidate, stated at full strength: Hebrews 11:4's claim that Abel acted "by faith" is a New Testament author reading a later theological category back into a Genesis 4 narrative that never uses the word "faith" and states no reason at all for the LORD's regard -- so treating "faith" as Genesis 4's own explanation imports content the chapter does not contain. Before you answer it, state what Hebrews 11:4 and 1 John 3:12 actually claim (see the reviewed connections, below) and what Genesis 4:4-5 does and does not say on its own (see Positions 1). A second worked candidate, running the other direction: the textual asymmetry between Abel's qualified offering ("the best portions of the firstborn") and Cain's unqualified one is itself in the text, prior to any New Testament gloss, so "faith" and "deeds" readings may be filling in a mechanism for a distinction the text already marks, not inventing one from nothing.

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Genesis 4:15 does not state what the mark on Cain physically was. The verse states only its purpose -- "so that no one who found him would kill him" -- and every specific proposal (a letter of God's name, a horn, a tremor, a given animal companion, skin color) comes from later tradition, named as such in the Positions section above, not from this verse's own words. (b) Genesis 4 does not name where Cain's wife or the people he fears in 4:14 came from. Genesis 5:4 states that Adam had other sons and daughters, without naming them or saying when they were born relative to Cain's exile; every specific account of who Cain married and who else was alive is supplied by later tradition or by inference from that one unelaborated verse, not stated directly in chapter 4 itself.

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection from this passage and defend it: name its type (for example explicit reference, contrast and reversal, motif), give its evidence label, and point to the words in both texts that support that grade. The reviewed connections attached to this lesson are listed here with their own stated grades, which is the point of the exercise:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |

| 1 John 3:11-12 | explicit reference | explicit | John names Cain and states a specific reason ("his own deeds were evil") that Genesis 4 itself does not put in those words |
| Hebrews 11:4 | explicit reference | explicit | Hebrews names Abel and supplies "faith" as the reason for God's regard, a word Genesis 4:4-5 does not use |
