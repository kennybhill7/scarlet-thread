Genesis 30:25-43 is a negotiation about pay. After fourteen years of work for his two wives, Jacob asks to go home. Laban answers, "I have learned by divination that the LORD has blessed me because of you" (30:27), and asks him to name his wages. Jacob asks for no fixed wage, only for the speckled, spotted and dark-coloured animals, and adds, "So my honesty will testify for me when you come to check on my wages in the future" (30:33). Laban agrees, then removes every such animal that day and sends them three days' journey away with his sons (30:35-36). Jacob sets peeled branches in the watering troughs, where the flocks mate and bear "young that were streaked or speckled or spotted" (30:39), uses them only for the stronger animals (30:41-42), and becomes "exceedingly prosperous" (30:43). The narrator does not say whether the branches caused the result, or whether Jacob did right. In the next chapter Jacob tells his wives a different account, crediting God and a dream (31:9-12). Readers have explained the method, and judged the man, in very different ways. This lesson reports them in their own words and does not choose. Every BSB quotation was checked by script against this app's shipped corpus (see Sources and Limits).

## Context

**What happens, in order.** After Joseph's birth Jacob asks Laban, "Send me on my way so I can return to my homeland" (30:25-26). Laban asks him to stay, citing what he has "learned by divination" (30:27), and says, "Name your wages, and I will pay them" (30:28). Jacob reminds him, "you had very little before my arrival" (30:29-30), and proposes that his wages be "every speckled or spotted sheep, every dark-colored lamb, and every spotted or speckled goat" (30:31-33). Laban says, "Agreed" (30:34), and that same day removes those animals himself and gives them to his sons, putting "a three-day journey between himself and Jacob" (30:35-36). Jacob peels branches of "poplar, almond, and plane trees" and sets them "in the watering troughs in front of the flocks" (30:37-39). He sets the young apart and turns the flock to face the streaked and dark animals (30:40), uses the branches only for "the stronger females" (30:41-42), and grows rich in flocks, servants, camels and donkeys (30:43).

**Where this unit sits.** Stage 5 of this app's eleven-stage lens (Genesis 12 through Malachi; machine-readable `chapters: ["1.12"]`). This curriculum's Genesis 29 lesson covered Jacob's arrival, the seven-year bargain and Laban's substitution of Leah. Laban there asked a similar question, "Tell me what your wages should be" (29:15). The births of Genesis 30:1-24 and Jacob's departure in Genesis 31 have their own lessons. Two later verses bear on this one and are only cross-referenced here: Jacob says Laban "has cheated me and changed my wages ten times" (31:7), and to Laban's face, "you have changed my wages ten times!" (31:41). Genesis 30 does not narrate those changes. Ramban, on 31:7, says the charge "was true, even though Scripture did not previously relate it."

**Key words, read as observations.** Each count was made by this lesson's own script over the app's BSB text, case-insensitively and on word boundaries.

- **The colour words.** "Speckled" occurs in seven BSB verses of Genesis, all in 30:32-31:12 (30:32, 30:33, 30:35, 30:39, 31:8, 31:10, 31:12); the only other BSB verse is Jeremiah 12:9, "a speckled bird of prey." "Spotted" as a colour occurs only in Genesis 30:32-31:12 (six verses); the BSB's only other "spotted" is a verb at Matthew 22:11. "Streaked" occurs in six verses of Genesis 30-31 and once elsewhere, of hair "streaked with gray" (Hosea 7:9). "Dark-colored" occurs four times, all in 30:32-40. The vocabulary of this bargain is almost entirely confined to these two chapters.
- **"Divination" (30:27).** The BSB has "divination" in Genesis only at 30:27 and 44:5, and "divine" as a verb only at 44:15 ("a man like me can surely divine the truth"). Bible Hub's interlinear gives the Hebrew at 30:27 as *niḥashti* (Strong's 5172, *nachash*). Its concordance lists eleven occurrences of the verb, Genesis 30:27, 44:5 and 44:15 among them. Brown-Driver-Briggs, on the same page, sorts the uses into "practise divination" (44:5, 44:15 and the prohibitions) and "observe the signs or omens," under which it places 30:27. The King James Version renders 30:27 "I have learned by experience." The Cambridge Bible gives the Septuagint as οἰωνισάμην and the Vulgate as *experimento didici* (learned by trial).
- **"Because of you" (30:27) and "wherever I set foot" (30:30).** Laban's "because of you" renders *biglalekha* (Strong's 1558, *gelal*). Bible Hub's concordance lists the word ten times, three of them in Genesis: Abram's "on account of you my life will be spared" (12:13), Laban's words here, and "the LORD blessed the Egyptian's household on account of him" (39:5). Jacob's reply uses a different expression, *leragli*, literally "at my foot" (30:30). Ramban notes the change: Jacob, "in a humble manner, he did not want to say 'for my sake.'" Rashi reads *leragli* as "on account of my coming."
- **"My honesty" (30:33).** The Hebrew is *tsidqati*, "my righteousness" (Strong's 6666, *tsedaqah*). In Genesis the noun occurs only three times, by Bible Hub's list: "it was credited to him as righteousness" (15:6), "doing what is right and just" (18:19), and here. The BSB's "honesty" is a fair rendering of a commercial sense; it also hides the word's two earlier uses. In the whole BSB, "honesty" occurs in three verses (Genesis 30:33, Proverbs 17:26, Isaiah 59:14).
- **"Wages."** In Genesis, "wages" appears in seven BSB verses, all in Genesis 29-31 (29:15, 30:28, 30:32, 30:33, 31:7, 31:8, 31:41).

## Literary Design

**Laban and the white.** The Hebrew name Laban means "white." The Cambridge Bible, on 30:37: "Jacob's trick turns upon the whiteness of the rods; and this supplies a play upon the name 'Laban' (= 'white'), who is outwitted by Jacob." Genesis Rabbah 73:10 hears the same sound in the first tree named: "rods of fresh poplar [*livne*]... a white [*lavan*] rod." In 30:35 the animals Laban removes are "every one that had any white on it." Rashi glosses that phrase as "all that had a white spot." A reader can notice that the word for white sits at three points in the story: in Laban's name, in the animals he takes away, and in the bark Jacob strips to show "the white inner wood" (30:37).

**Two contracts, one pattern.** The bargain in 30:31-34 is the second pay negotiation between these men. In 29:15 Laban asks, "should you work for nothing? Tell me what your wages should be"; in 30:28, "Name your wages, and I will pay them." Both times Laban invites Jacob to name the terms. In Genesis 29 what followed was the substitution of Leah. Here what follows is Laban's own separation of the marked animals (30:35-36). Jacob had proposed to "remove from them every speckled or spotted sheep" (30:32), and readers differ on whether Laban's removal kept the deal or bent it. Rashi reads the removal as Jacob's own request, so that Laban could not later say the marked animals "were there originally." Keil and Delitzsch note that Laban "did not leave Jacob to make the selection. He undertook that himself, probably to make more sure." Calvin, on 30:35, draws a harder inference: "every provision was made for Laban's advantage," and "It also appears, from the distance of the places, in which Laban kept his flocks apart, that he was not less suspicious than covetous." Jacob's side of the story is his stratagem with the branches. The narrator reports both moves in sequence and gives no verdict on either.

**Two accounts of the result.** The narrator's account (30:37-42) names the branches, the troughs and the timing. Jacob's later account to his wives (31:8-12) names none of these. He says, "Thus God has taken away your father's livestock and given them to me" (31:9), and tells of a dream in which "the angel of God" said, "see that all the males that are mating with the flock are streaked, spotted, or speckled; for I have seen all that Laban has done to you" (31:12). How the two accounts relate is the subject of Position 2. The dream itself belongs to the Genesis 31 lesson.

**The blessing "because of you."** Laban's admission (30:27) and Jacob's answer (30:30) say that the LORD blessed one man's household because of another man who worked in it. Genesis says the same of Joseph in Potiphar's house, with the same Hebrew word (39:5). Genesis Rabbah 73:8, on 30:30, joins them with Isaac at Gerar: "everywhere the righteous go, blessing is sent," citing 26:12, 30:30 and 39:5. That is the one connection authored for this lesson (see Teach-Back 5). Readers who know the promise to Abram, "all the families of the earth will be blessed through you" (12:3), may hear it here. No source read for this lesson draws that link at 30:27, and the lesson leaves it as a question (Questions to Carry, 3).

## Positions

Three questions follow. Each is reported through named readers in their own words, and none is decided here. A note on one misuse of the passage follows Position 3.

**1. What does Laban mean by "I have learned by divination" (30:27)?** The verb is the one used later of Joseph's cup (44:5, 44:15). Readers divide on how literally to take it in Laban's mouth.

- *Literal divination.* Rashi: "He was a diviner." Ibn Ezra, in Strickman and Silver's translation: "Nichashti (I have observed the signs) means I have divined, for Laban was an enchanter and had teraphim." Gill reports that "the Targums of Jonathan and Jerusalem render it, 'I have used divinations'." Keil and Delitzsch quote Delitzsch: "a heathen expression, like augurando cognovi." The Cambridge Bible: "Perhaps there may be a reference to the custom of consulting the household gods or teraphim. Cf. Genesis 31:19." Barnes, on 31:19, lists 30:27 among the places where teraphim "were employed for the purposes of divination."
- *Observation or testing.* Genesis Rabbah 73:8 glosses the word, "I attempted and examined." Ramban: "The word nichashti means 'I have tested,' and every form of nichesh connotes testing," adding, "some say that Laban was a diviner with teraphim." Barnes, on 30:27: "I have been an attentive observer." The Pulpit Commentary: "not necessarily by means of serpents..., or even by consulting his gods..., but perhaps by close observation and minute inspection." The Cambridge Bible, alongside its teraphim suggestion: "Here Laban means he has 'discerned' by clear indications."
- *A faded idiom.* Ellicott: "Words of this sort lose, at a very early date, their special signification, and all that Laban means is—'I fancy,' 'I conjecture.'"
- *As if by augury.* Calvin sets out two readings, that Laban, "having been instructed in magic arts," found Jacob profitable, and that "he had proved it to be so by experiment," and takes a third: "To me the true interpretation seems to be, as if he had said, that the blessing of God was as perceptible to him, as if it had been attested by prophecy, or found out by augury."

The readers agree on one point the text states: Laban attributes his blessing to "the LORD" (30:27), using the divine name, and Jacob repeats it (30:30). Gill: "Laban had so much religion as to ascribe the blessings, the good things he had, to the Lord." What Laban's word says about his religion is the open question.

**2. The peeled branches (30:37-42): what produced the result?** The narrator records what Jacob did and what the flocks bore. It does not say that the branches caused the result, and it does not say they did not. Jacob's later account names God and a dream and does not mention the branches (31:9-12). Readers explain the relation in five ways. The lesson reports them and does not judge whether the method worked.

- *A natural effect, as these readers understood nature.* Several readers treat the method as working by a known natural law. Keil and Delitzsch: "This artifice was founded upon a fact frequently noticed, particularly in the case of sheep, that whatever fixes their attention in copulation is marked upon the young." Calvin: "as it respects physical causes, it is well known, that the sight of objects by the female has great effect on the form of the foetus." Ibn Ezra, on 30:39: "it is a natural law." Rashbam says Jacob acted "to reinforce the imagination of the sheep to be impregnated." Ellicott calls the result "physically certain to follow." Genesis Rabbah 73:10 describes the mechanism: the animal "would see the rods and recoil backward. The male would copulate with it and it would bear an offspring like that." Rabbeinu Bahya, as Zev Farber quotes and translates him, says, "The form of the sticks was before their eyes and carved itself into their minds."
- *A popular belief the narrator records.* The Cambridge Bible, on 30:38: "It was the popular belief that such objects, being presented to the eye at such a season, would be likely to affect the colouring of the progeny." On 30:37 it adds, "The device is said to be well known to shepherds." Zev Farber (TheTorah.com, 2013) calls the idea "a version of an outdated theory called maternal impression" and traces it through Pliny the Elder and rabbinic texts. Herbert Basser (TheTorah.com, 2015) describes Jacob's plan as "a form of sympathetic suggestion based on ancient science."
- *God's doing, through the branches as means.* Benson holds that God probably "directed him to take this method," "not as though it were sufficient of itself to produce such an effect," "but as a means which God would bless," comparing Naaman's washing in the Jordan. He quotes Dr. Shuckford: "it cannot be proved that the method which Jacob used is a natural and effectual way to produce variegated cattle," and, even granting a natural effect, "the effect must still be ascribed immediately to God himself." Calvin, on 30:31, says that in chapter 31 Moses "more fully declares that Jacob had been divinely instructed thus to act." Matthew Henry: "it was done by intimation from God, and as a token of his power."
- *God's doing, without the branches.* Ramban, on 31:8, says the changing colours were "not due to the power of the sticks, for he was telling them of the deed of the Great G-d who treated him wondrously each and every year," and, on 31:10, that the angel told Jacob "henceforth Jacob should not make the sticks." Genesis Rabbah 73:10 records other mechanisms besides the branches: Rabbi Hoshaya's view that "the water was transformed into semen in their innards," and Rabbi Huna's that "the ministering angels were carrying from Laban's flock and coming and placing in the midst of Jacob's flock." Basser reports Ibn Ezra's reading that Jacob "thought his plan was working, but later God showed him that it was really divine providence," and Benno Jacob's, that the branches were "a ritual of superstition that any shepherd would do 'just in case'" (both are reported only as Basser reports them).
- *Two accounts from different hands.* Basser reports that "Many scholars assume that the accounts in chapter 30 and chapter 31 derive from different sources": the branches in the source scholars call J, the dream in E, "where God elsewhere is revealed through dreams." He reports a second critical model: the dream was "added into the story by a later editor, who may have been uncomfortable with the image of Jacob as a cheat," citing Reinhard Kratz, who calls the section "the justification of Jacob."

**How the readings answer one another.** Shuckford's "it cannot be proved" stands against Keil and Delitzsch's "a fact frequently noticed," and Farber's "outdated theory" against Ibn Ezra's "natural law." The Cambridge Bible's "popular belief" describes the same mechanism Calvin calls "well known," and differs from him on whether it is true. The "God's doing" readings (Ramban, Benson, Calvin, Henry) read 30:37-42 and 31:9-12 as one account. Basser sets out the harmonizing readings and the critical models side by side, observing that "the literary solutions interpret the final form of the text and implicitly assume that the entire account should work together as a unified whole." Basser also reports a reading that rejects Jacob's later account: Arnold Ehrlich held that "Jacob was lying about the angel's revelation because he couldn't trust his wives with the truth" (reported only as Basser reports it). This lesson does not decide between these readings, or whether the method worked.

**3. Did Jacob act rightly?** Jacob says, "my honesty will testify for me" (30:33), and the same chapter reports a stratagem that moves "the stronger ones to Jacob" (30:42). Laban had already separated the marked animals and sent them three days away (30:35-36), and Jacob later says Laban "changed my wages ten times" (31:7). The narrator states no verdict. Five readings follow.

- **Wrong in itself, but commanded (Calvin).** Calvin sets out the defence that Jacob "sought nothing but the reparation of former losses," and rejects it: "although we may be unjustly treated, we must not enter the contest with equal injustice." He continues: "Jacob ought not to have resorted to this stratagem... but rather to have followed the rule which the Lord delivers by the mouth of Paul, that the faithful should study to overcome evil with good." He then adds the qualifier: "This simplicity, I confess, ought to have been cultivated by Jacob, unless the Lord from heaven had commanded otherwise." He reads the chapters as a "hysteron proteron," in which "Moses first relates the fact, and then subjoins that Jacob had attempted nothing but by the command of God." So, he warns, "it is not for those persons to claim him as their advocate, who oppose malignant and fraudulent men with fallacies like their own." To the objection that God would then have suggested deceit, he answers that "God is the author of no fraud, when he stretches out his hand to protect his servant," and compares a judge who "condemns a thief to restore twofold or fourfold." Poole and the Geneva Study Bible's note reach the same conclusion by the same route (Geneva: "Jacob used no deceit in this for it was God's commandment as he declares in Ge 31:9,11").
- **Within the terms of the deal (Ramban; Matthew Henry).** Ramban: "As soon as they agreed that his hire would be these colors it was permissible for Jacob to do whatever he could to cause them to give birth in this manner." He adds, "Perhaps Jacob had made a condition that he may do with them whatever he wants since Laban did not know of the ramifications of this measure." Henry: "Neither could Laban complain of Jacob, for he had nothing more than was freely agreed that he should have; nor was he injured, but greatly benefitted by Jacob's services."
- **Self-protection, prompted by the dream (Rabbeinu Bahya, as Basser reports him).** "Because of this dream Jacob came to perform the deed, acting in accord with knowledge of natural science by manipulating the sticks – not to deceive Laban but to protect himself from Laban's deceitful practices."
- **An overreaching the narrator does not approve (Keil and Delitzsch; the Pulpit Commentary).** Keil and Delitzsch: Laban "was overreached by Jacob, who adopted a double method of increasing the wages agreed upon." On 30:43: the account closes "without expressing approbation of Jacob's conduct, or describing his increasing wealth as a blessing from God. The verdict is contained in what follows." The Pulpit Commentary, on 30:38, calls the branches "Jacob's first artifice to overreach Laban."
- **A boast rebuked (Genesis Rabbah 73:9).** The midrash takes 30:33 as a claim about the future. Rabbi Yehuda bar Simon sets it against "Do not glory in a future day" (Proverbs 27:1), and says, "in the future, your daughter will go out and be violated," citing Dinah (34:1). The same section has Rabbi Hiyya bar Rabba say Laban would "renege upon it ten times," and the Rabbis, "One hundred times."

**Objections each reading faces.** Each reading has been answered by a named reader.

- *Against Calvin's "commanded" reading,* Keil and Delitzsch find no approval in the account itself ("without expressing approbation of Jacob's conduct"), and Ehrlich, as Basser reports him, denies the revelation Calvin relies on.
- *Against "within the terms of the deal,"* Keil and Delitzsch read the timing in 30:41-42 as concealment: Jacob acted "possibly, less to spare Laban, than to avoid exciting suspicion, and so leading to the discovery of his trick." Ibn Ezra says the same: "Jacob did this so that Laban would not know that he was employing the sticks."
- *Against the self-protection reading,* Calvin, quoted above: "although we may be unjustly treated, we must not enter the contest with equal injustice."
- *Against the "overreaching" reading,* Calvin: "from the context it will appear that this adroitness was not culpable." Henry says the course "has been considered an instance of his policy and management. But it was done by intimation from God."
- *Against reading 30:33 as a boast,* Calvin reads the verse as trust, not presumption: "Jacob does not expect success, except through his faith and integrity," and "he committed the success of his labors to the Lord, in order that his integrity might hence be made manifest." Sforno reads it the same way: "Hashem will reveal what Yaakov deserves for his faithful service by altering the coloration of the lambs."

These readers differ on whether Jacob deceived Laban, whether a wronged party may recover what he is owed by stealth, and whether Genesis 31 changes how Genesis 30 should be read. This lesson does not decide between them.

**A misuse: Jacob's flocks in *The Merchant of Venice*.** In Act 1, scene 3, Shakespeare's Jewish moneylender, Shylock, retells Jacob's branches ("The skilful shepherd peel'd me certain wands") and ends, "This was a way to thrive, and he was blest: / And thrift is blessing, if men steal it not." The Christian merchant, Antonio, answers that the result was "swayed and fashioned by the hand of heaven," and turns to his friend: "The devil can cite Scripture for his purpose." Herbert Basser, reading the scene on TheTorah.com, says Antonio holds that Jews are devils who misuse Scripture, and adds: "The charge of Jewish hypocrisy was and is often encountered in Christian teachings." He writes, "It would seem that Shakespeare means to paint Shylock—and probably Jews in general—as the type of people who muddy the lines between smart business and deceitful practices," though he also says that "Shakespeare himself allows their positions to speak for themselves." He reads the two characters as following the two accounts in Genesis 30 and 31, and holds that Shakespeare "leaves the scriptural conundrum without resolution." Several things can be checked against the text. The stratagem in 30:37-42 belongs to one man in one dispute with his father-in-law; Genesis gives it no meaning for any people. The readers who weigh it hardest include Christians (Keil and Delitzsch, "overreached"; the Pulpit Commentary, "artifice") and Jewish readers (Genesis Rabbah 73:9 rebukes his "my honesty" as a boast; Ehrlich, as Basser reports him, holds that Jacob lied to his wives). Calvin, a Christian reader, also says no one may cite Jacob here as warrant for meeting fraud with fraud. Jewish and Christian readers alike divide over Jacob's conduct, as Position 3 shows.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge. It models the shape; it is not an answer key.

**Passage element.** Genesis 30:33 (BSB): "So my honesty will testify for me when you come to check on my wages in the future. If I have any goats that are not speckled or spotted, or any lambs that are not dark-colored, they will be considered stolen."

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: Jacob proposes a test anyone can check, the colour of each animal, so that the record itself will settle disputes. Inference: a first audience would likely hear a man who has been cheated once asking for terms that cannot be reinterpreted. Whether what he did next kept faith with those terms is disputed (Position 3).

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When a working relationship has gone wrong before, terms that anyone can check protect both sides, and the one who proposes them is bound by them too.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a pay arrangement, a party with power to change it, and a test both sides can see. Not carried forward: the branches, the question of whether they worked, the dream, any verdict on Jacob or Laban, and any claim that God guarantees the outcome.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a narrative pattern applied by analogy. It is not a command. Promise scope: none. Nothing here promises that clear terms will prevent loss or that the more honest party will prosper.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. You are a contractor paid partly by a performance bonus. Your client has twice changed the formula after the work was done. You could quietly steer your work toward whatever the formula happens to reward this quarter, without telling the client.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: honesty and justice in dealings. One faithful response: ask that the formula be put in writing with a date, keep records the client can check, and raise any change openly rather than working the gap in silence.

**Cautions.** This concerns an ordinary contract. It does not compare any client to Laban or any worker to Jacob, and it does not take a side in Position 3. If you are not being paid what you are owed, that is a question for someone qualified, not a study note. In the United States, the Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division can be reached toll-free at 1-866-487-9243 (Monday to Friday, 8:00 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. local time; hours vary by region). A state labor agency or a licensed attorney can also help. Elsewhere, contact your local labor authority.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. The narrator reports what Jacob did and what the flocks bore (30:37-42); Jacob reports a dream and credits God (31:9-12). What would a reader need to assume to treat these as one account, and what would a reader need to assume to treat them as two?
2. Jacob says "my honesty will testify for me" (30:33), and the Hebrew word is the one used of Abram in 15:6. Calvin hears trust; Genesis Rabbah hears a boast. Which words in 30:31-34 support each?
3. Laban says the LORD blessed him "because of you" (30:27), and Genesis says the same of Potiphar's house (39:5). Does either verse need Genesis 12:3 to be understood? What in the text would show that the narrator meant the link?
4. Keil and Delitzsch say the verdict on Jacob is "contained in what follows." What in Genesis 31 might they mean, and could the same chapter be read as Calvin reads it?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 30:25-43 in about eight steps, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) Say what Jacob asked for as wages and what Laban did the same day. (c) Name the Hebrew words behind "divination" (30:27) and "honesty" (30:33), and one other place in Genesis where each occurs.
2. **Five-minute outline.** (0:00-1:00) the request to leave and the bargain; (1:00-2:00) the colour words, Laban's name and the two pay negotiations; (2:00-3:00) "divination" and "because of you," and Position 1; (3:00-4:15) the branches, Jacob's later account, and Positions 2 and 3 with their objections; (4:15-5:00) the misuse note and the connection.
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: reading the branches as God's command (Calvin) imports Genesis 31 into Genesis 30, where God is not mentioned after 30:30. Weigh it against Calvin's "hysteron proteron." A second candidate, running the other way: reading the branches as plain trickery ignores that Laban had already removed the animals and, by Jacob's later account, changed the wages ten times. Weigh it against Calvin's "we must not enter the contest with equal injustice."
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) It does not say that the branches caused the result; the narrator reports sequence, and Jacob later gives a different account. (b) It does not state God's approval or disapproval of Jacob's method; the LORD is named in 30:25-43 only in Laban's and Jacob's words (30:27, 30:30). (c) It does not say how Laban practised divination, or whether he did; the readings in Position 1 are inferences. (d) It does not describe any people as tricksters; the dispute is between two men in one family.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** The connection attached to this lesson:

Defend it, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` for a candidate. Four candidates were considered and not authored. (a) **Genesis 12:2-3** ("all the families of the earth will be blessed through you"). The theme of others blessed through Abraham's line is easy to hear in 30:27, but the verses share no distinctive wording, and no source read for this lesson draws the link; authoring it would have meant grading a reader's association as evidence. (b) **Genesis 44:5, 44:15.** The verb *nachash* recurs, and the Cambridge Bible cites both verses on 30:27, but only to contrast their sense ("used of obtaining an answer by means of magic") with Laban's ("he has 'discerned' by clear indications"). (c) **Genesis 31:7-12.** Jacob's own account of the flocks is the closest text of all, but it belongs to the Genesis 31 lesson, which can author it from that side. (d) **Hosea 12:12** ("for a wife he tended sheep") already has a row from Genesis 29 (conn-gen29-wife-hosea12). No New Testament verse quotes or names these events: a script scan finds no "Laban," "speckled" or "streaked" in the New Testament, and none of the New Testament's uses of "wages" was found to refer to this scene.
