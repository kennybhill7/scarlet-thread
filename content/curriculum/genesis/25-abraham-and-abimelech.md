Genesis 20 is the second time Abraham tells a foreign king that his wife is his sister. The first was in Egypt (12:10-20); a third version follows with Isaac and Rebekah (26:6-11). The chapter sits between the promise of a son "at this time next year" (18:10-14) and his birth (21:1-2), so in between, the mother of the promised son is taken into another man's house. This lesson does three things and settles none of them. It reads the chapter closely, including words that appear here first ("a prophet," "pray," "healed"). It sets the three wife-sister stories side by side and reports how two families of readers account for the repetition. And it reports four real disagreements among named Jewish and Christian readers: whether Abraham's defense in 20:12 was a lie, a half-truth or a technical truth; how innocent Abimelech was; what part Sarah played; and what the prophet's prayer (20:7) is taken to support. The debate over Abraham's conduct in Egypt belongs to the Genesis 12 lesson and is not repeated here. Every BSB quotation below, and in the four connection files, was printed from this app's shipped corpus before drafting and checked afterward by a second script; see Sources and Limits.

## Context

**What happens, in order (20:1-18).** In Gerar, Abraham says of Sarah, "She is my sister," and "Abimelech king of Gerar had Sarah brought to him" (20:1-2). God comes to Abimelech "in a dream": "You are as good as dead because of the woman you have taken, for she is a married woman" (20:3). "Abimelech had not gone near her," and he protests, "Lord, would You destroy a nation even though it is innocent?", claiming "the integrity of my heart and the innocence of my hands" (20:4-5). God answers, "Yes, I know that you did this with a clear conscience, and so I have kept you from sinning against Me. That is why I did not let you touch her" (20:6), and commands, "Now return the man's wife, for he is a prophet; he will pray for you and you will live" (20:7). Abimelech confronts Abraham: "You have done things to me that should not be done" (20:9), and "What prompted you to do such a thing?" (20:10). Abraham gives three answers: he assumed there was "no fear of God in this place" (20:11); Sarah "really is my sister, the daughter of my father—though not the daughter of my mother—and she became my wife" (20:12); and since leaving his father's house he had asked her to "say of me, 'He is my brother'" wherever they went (20:13). Abimelech gives gifts, restores Sarah, offers the land (20:14-15), and tells Sarah that the silver given her "brother" is "your vindication" (20:16). "Then Abraham prayed to God, and God healed Abimelech and his wife and his maidservants" (20:17), for "the LORD had completely closed all the wombs in Abimelech's household" (20:18).

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens (`content/lens/eleven-stages.json`) places Genesis 12 through Malachi in stage 5, "Israel: The Twelve Tribes" (machine-readable `chapters` field `["1.12"]`). The chapter follows the destruction of Sodom (Genesis 19) and comes just before Isaac's birth (21:1-3). The Cambridge Bible explains the placement as "intelligible on the supposition of its derivation from an independent source, not connected with chap. 18." Other readers find a purpose in it. Gill, on 20:6, says the sin was prevented "that it might not in any respect be a doubtful point whether Isaac, whom Sarah had now conceived, was a legitimate son of Abraham."

**Key words, read as observations.**

- **"he is a prophet" (20:7).** A script scan of this app's whole BSB finds "prophet" first in this verse, and nowhere else in Genesis. The Hebrew agrees: Bible Hub's concordance lists Genesis 20:7 as the first of 316 occurrences of *nabi* (Strong's 5030), followed by Exodus 7:1. The Cambridge Bible notes "the first occurrence of it in Scripture" and calls the title "an anachronism, indicating the atmosphere of the monarchical period," citing 1 Samuel 9:9 ("the nabi was in old times called roeh, or Seer"). The Pulpit Commentary denies that the term shows "that the Pentateuch ... cannot be of greater antiquity than the time of Samuel." On what the title means here, Barnes writes: "Abraham's first exercise in prophecy is not in speaking to men of God, but to God for men." (On a dispute this verse raises about intercession, see Positions 4.)
- **"he will pray for you" (20:7) and "Abraham prayed" (20:17).** In this app's BSB, 20:7 is also the first verse with any form of "pray," and 20:17 the second. In Hebrew, Bible Hub's concordance lists 20:7 and 20:17 as the first two occurrences of the verb *palal* (Strong's 6419). Abraham's long plea for Sodom in 18:23-32 is never called prayer in the BSB or by this verb.
- **"God healed" (20:17).** A script scan finds "heal" in Genesis only in this verse.
- **"in a dream" (20:3).** This is the first dream in the BSB. The Cambridge Bible notes that "E frequently describes Divine interposition by means of a dream."
- **"God" and "the LORD."** In the BSB of this chapter the narrator says "God" (20:3, 20:6, 20:17) and Abimelech says "Lord" (20:4), the convention for *Adonai*. "LORD," the convention for the divine name, appears only in the last verse, 20:18. The Cambridge Bible reads 20:18 as "An editorial addition, explanatory of Genesis 20:17. 'Jehovah' is here used for the only time in this narrative." Keil and Delitzsch read the change as deliberate: the wombs were closed by "Jehovah, the God of salvation," to protect "the birth of the promised son from Sarah."
- **"no fear of God in this place" (20:11).** This is the only occurrence of "fear of God" in Genesis in the BSB. Calvin judges the assumption unfair: "it was, nevertheless, unjust to form a judgment so unfavourable of a people whom he had not yet known."

## Literary Design

**Three tellings of one pattern.** Genesis tells a wife-sister story three times. The shared features and the differences can be read off the BSB.

| | 12:10-20 | 20:1-18 | 26:6-11 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Place and ruler | Egypt, Pharaoh | Gerar, "Abimelech king of Gerar" (20:2) | Gerar, "Abimelech king of the Philistines" (26:8) |
| The claim | "Please say you are my sister" (12:13) | "She is my sister" (20:2) | "She is my sister" (26:7) |
| Stated fear | "they will kill me" (12:12) | "They will kill me on account of my wife" (20:11) | "will kill me on account of Rebekah" (26:7) |
| Is the woman taken? | "taken into the palace of Pharaoh" (12:15) | "had Sarah brought to him" (20:2) | No |
| How the truth comes out | "severe plagues" (12:17) | God "in a dream" (20:3) | the king sees "Isaac caressing his wife Rebekah" (26:8) |
| The ruler's question | "What have you done to me?" (12:18) | "What have you done to us?" (20:9) | "What is this you have done to us?" (26:10) |
| Outcome | "Take her and go!" (12:19) | "my land is before you. Settle wherever you please" (20:15) | "Whoever harms this man or his wife will surely be put to death" (26:11) |

Only Genesis 20 gives the patriarch a defense of the claim itself (20:12-13), names him a prophet (20:7), and ends with his prayer and a healing (20:17). Two of this lesson's connections record the parallels.

**How readers account for the repetition.** Two families of readers answer this differently. Both are reported here at comparable length, and neither is adopted.

- *Separate sources or traditions.* The Cambridge Bible heads the chapter "Abraham and Sarah at the Court of Abimelech at Gerar (E)" and calls it "the first continuous narrative taken from E, the Elohist or Ephraimitic source." On 20:2 it writes: "It seems almost incredible that, after the event recorded in Genesis 12:13-20, Abraham should once again have displayed the same faults of cowardice and dissimulation. ... The narrative most probably is a duplicate of the tradition of Genesis 12:13-20." Gesila Uzukwu's 2021 article in *Old Testament Essays* surveys this family of readings. She reports Robert Culley's view that they are "versions of a single story that had existed in an oral form," that John Van Seters and Claus Westermann take 12:10-20 as the earliest, and that Martin Noth and E. A. Speiser take the Isaac version as the original. (These scholars are reported here as Uzukwu summarizes them; their own works were not read.)
- *Deliberate repetition within one story.* Keil and Delitzsch reject the view that the chapter is "merely an Elohistic side-piece appended to the Jehovistic account in Genesis 12:14." They argue that apart from "the one point of agreement," "the repetition of which in the space of 24 years is by no means startling, when we consider the customs of the age," "all the more minute details are entirely different," above all the king: "a heathen imbued with a moral consciousness of right, and open to receive divine revelation, of which there is not the slightest trace in the king of Egypt." Ramban, who also reads the chapters as two events, begins his comment on 20:2: "This was not like what happened in Egypt." Rabbi Dan Judson, writing for My Jewish Learning, applies Robert Alter's method of "type scenes" from *The Art of Biblical Narrative*. On this method a reader learns most from "how the scenes differ from each other." He concludes that the scenes show "the coherence and artistry of the biblical text" and the narrator's return "to the theme of deception." Uzukwu reports Alter calling the stories "purposefully deployed literary conventions" with fixed motifs. Alter's own book was not read for this lesson.

These two families ask different questions. The first asks how the text came to be; the second asks what the text as it stands is doing. Some readers combine them. Uzukwu, for instance, surveys the source debates, then reads "all three narratives ... as distinct literary units, though having a close connection with their sister narratives," and concludes that "The three sister-wife narratives are not duplications of one narrative." This lesson leaves the question open.

**Shared Hebrew across chapters.** Bible Hub's interlinear shows three words this chapter shares with nearby texts, each hidden or partly hidden by the English.

- *tsaddiq*, "righteous." Abimelech's "a nation even though it is innocent" (20:4) uses the adjective Abraham used in "sweep away the righteous with the wicked" (18:23). Readers disagree about whether the echo is meant (see the connection on 18:23).
- *naga*, "touch." God's "I did not let you touch her" (20:6) uses the verb of Abimelech's later decree, "Whoever harms this man or his wife" (26:11), and of Psalm 105:15, "Do not touch My anointed ones!"
- *nabi*, "prophet." The noun in 20:7 is also the noun in "Do no harm to My prophets!" (Psalm 105:15).

**The intercessor's role reversed.** In Genesis 18 Abraham pleads for a city he does not belong to. In Genesis 20 a foreign king pleads to God for his own "nation," and Abraham, whose conduct created the danger, is the one God names as intercessor. The Cambridge Bible notes the contrast in moral terms, on 20:9: "The moral standard of the heathen king here stands higher than that of Abraham the prophet." The reversal is checkable; what it means is part of what Positions 1 and 2 dispute.

## Positions

Four questions follow, each reported through named readers in their own words. None is decided here, and no position below is this lesson's conclusion. Positions report what commentators conclude; the Context and Literary Design sections report what the text says.

**1. "she really is my sister" (20:12): lie, deception or technical truth?** The text records Abraham's claim and no reply to it. Readers divide over two linked questions: who Sarah was to Abraham, and whether a true statement used to hide a marriage is a lie.

*Who was Sarah?* Genesis 11:29 names "Sarai" as Abram's wife without giving her parents.

- **A half-sister, Terah's daughter by another wife.** Ellicott: "Sarah was apparently Abraham's half-sister, being Terah's daughter by another wife." The Pulpit Commentary holds that "there seems no reason for departing from the statement of the text," listing Rosenmüller, Kalisch, Keil and Knobel. Haydock's Catholic commentary, quoting Calmet, agrees and asks: "Why should we not, therefore, believe Abraham, who certainly knew the real state of the question, and who would not tell a lie, rather than seek for improbable and far-fetched solutions?"
- **A niece or granddaughter, called "sister" and "daughter" in a wider sense.** Rashi identifies Sarah as Haran's daughter (so Abraham's niece) and explains "the daughter of my father" this way: "one's children's children are considered as one's own children. Thus, too, did he say to Lot, (13:8) 'For we are brethren.'" Poole and Gill give the same reading. Calvin, noting that "the name sister has a wider signification among the Hebrews," prefers "that she was his sister in the second degree." The Pulpit Commentary lists Josephus, Augustine, Jerome and "Jonathan" for the view that Sarah was Iscah, Haran's daughter (11:29). Ellicott answers that 20:12 "disproves the notion that Sarah was the same as Iscah," because "the distinction between the identity of the father and the diversity of the mother would in her case be unmeaning."

*The later law.* Leviticus 18:9 forbids sexual relations with "your sister, either your father's daughter or your mother's daughter," and Leviticus 20:17 and Deuteronomy 27:22 restate the ban (Leviticus 20:17 attaches a penalty). Commentators cite these laws only to ask whether Abraham's marriage was lawful in its own time. Rashi answers that a father's daughter "is permitted to be married to a descendant of Noah," since the law for non-Israelites did not count the father's line. Poole answers that "neither had God as yet given those prohibitions, Leviticus 18:1-30." Gill calls the marriage lawful "as in those times it was judged lawful." The Cambridge Bible notes that the laws forbid it, and that marriage with a half-sister "was evidently permitted in David's time (cf. 2 Samuel 13:13)."

*Was it a lie?*

- **A true statement, offered as a real defense.** Rashi: "He answered them in this way in order to bear out his former statement that she was his sister." Barnes says Abraham "pleads also in extenuation that she is in reality his sister," and that he "was not yet fully alive to the unlawfulness of his usual pretence."
- **Not a lie in words, but a lie in effect.** Calvin: "Abraham extenuates his offense, and draws a distinction between his silence and a direct falsehood; and certainly he professed with truth, that he was the brother of Sarah." He continues: "although he did not lie in words, yet with respect to the matter of fact, his dissimulation was a lie, by implication." Calvin also allows Abraham's stated purpose, which was "no other intention than to declare that he had not dealt fraudulently with Abimelech." Haydock: "By calling Sara his sister without any addition, Abraham intended that the people should conclude he was not married." Jamieson-Fausset-Brown say the statement "absolved him from the charge of direct and absolute falsehood, but he had told a moral untruth because there was an intention to deceive."
- **A half-truth that does not answer the charge.** The Cambridge Bible: "Abraham's excuse is based upon a half truth. Sarah may have been truly his sister; but this statement was no moral justification for his suppression of the fact that she was his wife." It adds that 20:13 "scarcely adds dignity to his line of defence." Keil and Delitzsch call 20:11-13 "two weak excuses."
- **Truth or falsehood is beside the point, though the speech has a reason.** Ramban: "I know not the sense of this apology. Even if it were true that she was his sister and his wife, nevertheless when they wanted to take her as a wife and he told them, She is my sister, in order to lead them astray, he already committed a sin towards them ... and it no longer mattered at all whether the thing was true or false!" He then proposes a reading that makes sense of it. Abraham was answering the king's question in 20:10, which Chavel's translation renders "What sawest thou?" He had not singled out Gerar: "I did not begin this practice upon entering your country for I did not see that you had committed any sin."

**2. How innocent was Abimelech (20:4-6)?** God tells Abimelech, "I know that you did this with a clear conscience" (20:6), and also threatens his life (20:3, 20:7). Readers weigh those two statements differently.

- **Upright, and acknowledged as such.** Ramban calls the king "perfect and upright, and his people were likewise good." Gill says "a very great testimony" is "borne to his integrity," and that "he appears to be a man of great honour and uprightness, especially in this affair, if not a good man."
- **Innocent in intention, not in deed.** Rashi divides Abimelech's two claims. God grants "the integrity of my heart" but not "the innocence of my hands": "It is true that at first you had no intention of sinning, but you cannot claim innocency of hands." He continues: "It was not of your own will that you did not touch her, but it was 'I' who withheld you." Calvin draws a related line: Abimelech "does not arrogate to himself a purity which is altogether spotless," and "We know how great is the difference between a crime and a fault." Calvin adds that while God "allows that his excuse is true, He nevertheless chastises him," and concludes that "even they who are pure, according to human judgment, are not entirely free from blame." Poole: "an extenuation of thy sin, though not a total excuse." Haydock, citing Menochius: "Sincere heart, abhorring adultery, but not altogether innocent."
- **Blameless by his own standards, but not acquitted.** The Pulpit Commentary reads God's words as "judged from thy moral standpoint," which "do not imply a Divine acquittal as to the essential guiltiness of the act." Barnes: Abimelech "was innocent in regard to the 'great sin' of seizing another man's wife," but "was wrong in appropriating a woman to himself by mere stretch of power, and in adding wife to wife," customs "for which his conscience did not upbraid him." Keil and Delitzsch hold that God treated him "as capable of deeper moral discernment and piety" by requiring Abraham's intercession.

*"I have kept you from sinning against Me" (20:6).* Readers agree that God restrained Abimelech. They differ on how, and the text does not say until 20:17-18. Rashi: "The Angel prevented him." Keil and Delitzsch: "God had hindered him by illness." Ramban: "Abimelech was stricken in his limbs." Gill allows "some impulse upon his mind" or "some disease upon him." Several draw a general lesson about providence that the verse does not itself state. Calvin: "it frequently happens, that the Spirit restrains, by his bridle, those who are gliding into error." Benson: "it is not from him that there is sin, but it is from him that there is not more sin." No source read here disputes that God restrains sin. The disagreement is about the means in this case, so the point is reported here as observation, not as a separate position.

**3. Sarah's part (20:2, 20:5, 20:13, 20:16).** Sarah never speaks in this chapter. Her words come only through others: Abimelech reports that "she herself said, 'He is my brother'" (20:5), and Abraham reports his old request to her (20:13). Readers reconstruct her part in opposite ways.

- **Not consulted this time.** Rashi, drawing on Genesis Rabbah, on 20:2: "This time he did not ask her consent but forcibly and against her inclination he stated that she was his sister, because she had already been taken once to Pharaoh's house on account of this." Haydock likewise says Abimelech "Took her, against her will, as Pharao had done."
- **A participant.** Uzukwu reads 20:5 as showing that "Sarah had also lied about the relationship with Abraham, making her an accomplice in a bid that will also endanger her life." She calls Sarah's silence "a complicit silence," but also writes that "it is not a submissive silence that leads to despair. Her silence was an act that looked beyond the present situation to offer a better tomorrow." She names the larger pattern plainly: "In objectifying the matriarchs, Abraham and Isaac made them vulnerable to use by others."
- **Rebuked at the end (20:16).** Calvin reads Abimelech's words as "a just censure; because Sarah, pretending that she was in the power of her husband, had deprived herself of the divine protection." He reads the closing clause as "Thus she was reproved," saying the Lord "suffered Sarah to be reproved by a heathen king, that he might the more deeply affect her with a sense of shame."
- **Vindicated, and still protesting (20:16).** Ramban reads the same clause the other way. Abimelech "mollified Abraham with money, and Sarah with words," but "Sarah did not accept his apology ... she yet continued arguing with him, saying that she would not forgive him. The verse thus speaks in her praise." Rashi reads the gift as protecting her name, so that no one could say "After he abused her he sent her back." The Cambridge Bible: "Sarah is righted, and her honour saved." The BSB's own rendering is "It is your vindication before all who are with you; you are completely cleared."

**4. "he will pray for you" (20:7): what the verse is taken to support.** Two traditions draw opposite lessons about intercession from the same words. Calvin: "Christ, even then, was the only Mediator; but this was no reason why some men should not pray for others," adding that "as the Lord does not here send the king of Gerar to Noah, or to any one of the dead fathers, but into the presence of the living Abraham," the verse gives no ground for "the patronage of the dead." Haydock, the Catholic commentary: "God will sometimes grant, at the request of his saints, what he would deny even such as Abimelech or the friends of Job. Is not this sufficient encouragement for us, to have recourse to the intercession of the saints?" Both are applying the verse to a later dispute; Genesis 20 itself speaks of a living prophet praying for one king.

All of these readers work from the same eighteen verses. They disagree about how to name what Abraham said, how much innocence God granted Abimelech, whether Sarah was acted on, acting, rebuked or vindicated, and what the prophet's prayer implies. This lesson reports them and does not rank them.

One thing the text does record is that Sarah is placed in danger twice by her husband's choices (12:15; 20:2), and that in this chapter she never speaks for herself. ## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from the passage in its own setting to a modern situation. It models the shape; it is not an answer key, and the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate step.

**Passage element.** Genesis 20:9-10. Abimelech has been misled into a serious wrong he did not intend. He names the harm plainly ("You have done things to me that should not be done"), and then asks a real question and waits for the answer: "What prompted you to do such a thing?"

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text records the king stating the harm, then asking for the reason, and Abraham answering at length (20:11-13). Calvin comments that it is "no common sign of a just and meek disposition in Abimelech, that he allows Abraham a free defense." Inference: a hearer might notice that the foreigner, not the patriarch, models a fair confrontation; whether the narrator intends that contrast is disputed (Positions 2).

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When someone's misleading statement has drawn you into a wrong, you can name the harm directly and still ask, and listen to, why they did it before deciding what comes next.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a misleading half-truth, real harm to the person misled, a direct confrontation, and a question that allows an answer. Not carried forward: the king, the dream, the threat of death, the marriage, the gifts, and the healing.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed. Nothing here promises that the other person's answer will be honest or that the relationship will be restored.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. A coworker gave you figures for a report that were technically accurate but left out a correction they knew about. You presented the report, and your manager has now questioned your accuracy in front of the team.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: honest confrontation. One faithful response is to meet the coworker privately, say exactly what happened and what it cost you, and ask what led them to leave out the correction, before you decide whether to raise it with the manager.

**Cautions.** This example concerns an ordinary workplace mistake between equals. It does not apply where one person controls, threatens or endangers another; there, the safety resources above or a qualified professional are the right next step. The passage is not a text about workplace ethics; the bridge is an analogy the learner may find does not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. Using the table in Literary Design, which differences between the three tellings seem to you to carry weight, and which look like variation in retelling? Does your answer favor either family of readers?
2. Abraham's three answers (20:11, 20:12, 20:13) give a fear, a fact and a habit. Which of the three does each reader in Positions 1 treat as the real defense, and which would you?
3. God tells Abimelech both "You are as good as dead" (20:3) and "I know that you did this with a clear conscience" (20:6). Using only the wording of 20:3-7, how would you describe Abimelech's position before God? Is anything in the text decisive for Rashi's division between heart and hands?
4. The first person in the Bible called a prophet is introduced at a moment of his own failure, and his first recorded act as one is to pray for the king his own claim had put in danger. Is that pairing enough to support a reading, or only to raise a question?
5. Sarah's words in this chapter come only through Abimelech (20:5) and Abraham (20:13). How would you test Rashi's "forcibly and against her inclination" against Uzukwu's "accomplice," using only the text?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 20:1-18 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) Quote God's description of Abraham in 20:7 as this app's BSB gives it, "he is a prophet; he will pray for you and you will live," and say which two words in it occur here first. (c) State what 20:12 says about Sarah and what it does not say.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) the scene between promise (18:10-14) and birth (21:1-3); (1:00-2:00) the first words and the shift to "LORD" in 20:18; (2:00-3:00) the three tellings and the two families of readers; (3:00-4:00) the four named disputes, held open; (4:00-4:30) the connections and their labels (prompt 5); (4:30-5:00) the boundary in prompt 4.
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: setting the source-critical and deliberate-repetition readings side by side as equals hides that they rest on different kinds of evidence. Before answering, check what each family, as quoted here, points to in the text: the Cambridge Bible to the dreams and the timing; Keil and Delitzsch and Judson to the differences between the tellings. A second candidate, running the other way: Positions 3 gives Calvin's rebuke of Sarah and Ramban's praise of her equal space, yet the text gives Sarah no words of her own in this chapter. Ask what each reader is building on, and whether either claims more about Sarah than the text reports.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) Genesis 20 does not settle who Sarah's mother was or whether "sister" means half-sister or niece. Readers from Rashi to Ellicott take 20:12 differently. (b) The passage does not state a general rule that God always restrains sin. God says, "I have kept you from sinning against Me" (20:6), about one man in one case. The general statements are Calvin's and Benson's, not the verse's. (c) The chapter does not report a verdict on Abraham from God. God calls him "a prophet" (20:7). The rebuke "You have done things to me that should not be done" (20:9) is Abimelech's.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** The reviewed connections attached to this lesson:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Psalm 105:12-15 | allusion | strong | shares *naga* (20:6) and *nabi* (20:7); commentators on both passages name Genesis 20; the psalm names the patriarchs but no king or episode |
| Genesis 12:10-20 | parallel | strong | the same sequence and much of the same wording (the sister claim, the taking, the intervention, "What have you done"); the Cambridge Bible reads the two as one tradition in two sources, Keil and Delitzsch as two events |
| Genesis 26:6-11 | parallel | strong | same city, same royal name, "She is my sister," the fear of being killed, the king's protest about guilt; no woman taken and no dream |
| Genesis 18:23-25 | motif | plausible | *tsaddiq* in both questions about killing the innocent; the Cambridge Bible cross-references 18:23, while Rashi hears the Flood instead and Calvin calls the Sodom comparison "perhaps too refined" |

Choose one row and defend it, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` for a candidate. This lesson looked for a New Testament use of Genesis 20 and found none: a script scan of this app's BSB finds neither "Abimelech" nor "Gerar" in the New Testament, and no New Testament verse quotes the chapter. Three candidates were not authored. (a) **1 Peter 3:6**, "just as Sarah obeyed Abraham and called him lord." Peter's wording points to Genesis 18:12, where the BSB has "my master." That link is already a reviewed row in this curriculum (conn-gen18-sarah-lord-1peter3). Gill, on 1 Peter 3:6, extends Sarah's obedience to husbands' "lawful commands, as Sarah did in Egypt, and in Gerar, though she exposed herself to great danger." That is Gill's application, not Peter's citation, so `no_warrant_yet` is the honest grade; the safety note in Positions applies to any reading of it. (b) **Hebrews 11:12**, "he as good as dead." The same English phrase appears in Genesis 20:3, "You are as good as dead," but the person (Abraham, not Abimelech) and the sense (old age, not a death sentence) differ, so this is a match only in the English. (c) **James 5:16**, "The prayer of a righteous man has great power to prevail." Calvin quotes the verse on 20:7 to support intercession among the living. James does not mention Abraham or Gerar, so this is a devotional cross-reference at most. The strongest later echo found is Psalm 105, an Old Testament text.
