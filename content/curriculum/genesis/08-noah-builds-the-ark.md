Genesis 6:9-7:24 is where the flood stops being announced and starts being built, boarded, and endured. The unit gives exact numbers -- an ark's dimensions, a count of days, a count of animals -- and a careful reader has to notice that some of those numbers do not obviously agree with each other, and that at least one phrase in it ("all the high mountains... were covered," 7:19) has been read in sharply different ways by scholars who are not careless readers. This lesson's discipline is to report those disagreements rather than pick a winner and present it as the text's own plain sense: the two sets of animal instructions (6:19-20 against 7:2-3), the meaning of "I will establish My covenant" at 6:18 (the first use of the word "covenant" in the Bible, by this app's own check against its shipped text), and the geographic extent of the flood's coverage each have real, named, published answers that disagree with each other, and this lesson keeps them separate rather than resolving them. The unit also sits beside a body of Mesopotamian flood literature -- the Gilgamesh and Atrahasis epics -- that this lesson compares with the same care the Genesis 2 lesson already gave the Atrahasis parallel to humanity's creation: real similarities, reported as real, and real differences, reported just as plainly.

## Context

**What happens, in order (6:9-7:24).** Noah is introduced as "a righteous man, blameless in his generation," who "walked with God" (6:9), with three sons: Shem, Ham, and Japheth (6:10). The earth is "corrupt... and full of violence" (6:11-12). God tells Noah the end of all living creatures has come and instructs him to build an ark of gopher wood, with its exact dimensions, rooms, and a door (6:13-16). God states He will bring floodwaters to destroy every breathing creature (6:17), but will establish His covenant with Noah, who will enter the ark with his family (6:18). Noah is told to bring two of every kind of creature, male and female, to keep them alive, and to store food for all of them (6:19-21). Noah does everything exactly as commanded (6:22). The LORD then tells Noah to enter the ark, this time specifying seven pairs of every clean animal and bird but only one pair of each unclean animal, and that rain will fall for forty days and forty nights starting in seven days (7:1-4). Noah again does all that he is commanded (7:5). At 600 years old, Noah, his family, and the animals board the ark; the LORD shuts him in (7:6-16). The flood rises for forty days until "all the high mountains under all the heavens were covered," fifteen cubits deep over the peaks (7:17-20). Every living thing outside the ark dies (7:21-23). The waters prevail over the earth for 150 days (7:24). This is a plain retelling of the sequence; what each piece means is the rest of this lesson's work.

**Where this unit sits.** Genesis 6:9 opens with its own *tôlədôt* heading ("This is the account of Noah"), the same recurring formula that marks major divisions throughout Genesis, so the text itself marks a new unit here even though the flood story it introduces continues directly out of 6:1-8's narration of spreading corruption. This app's own eleven-stage lens (`content/lens/eleven-stages.json`) names Genesis 6-9 as a single stage ("The World Judged and Destroyed," stage 3), and this app's own preceding lesson on 6:1-8 already resolved that the lens's machine-readable `chapters` field places all of chapter 6 in this stage rather than the previous one; since this unit continues directly into chapter 7 within that same named stage, it is filed here as stage 3 as well. That same preceding lesson identified an inclusio bracketing the whole flood narrative: 6:5's diagnosis of humanity ("every inclination of the thoughts of his heart was altogether evil all the time") is echoed almost verbatim at 8:21 ("every inclination of his heart is evil from his youth"), with the LORD's response reversed between the two -- destruction the first time, a promise never to destroy again the second. This unit's own boundary (6:9-7:24) sits entirely inside that inclusio's first half, ending at the flood's worst point (7:24's 150 days of prevailing water) and stopping just short of 8:1, where the narrative turns ("But God remembered Noah") toward the inclusio's eventual resolution. Readers who want the second half of that bracket, and the reversal it marks, will meet it in the unit that follows this one, not in this one.

**Key words, read as observations.**

- **תָּמִים** (*tamim*, "blameless, complete," 6:9) is the same word used of sacrificial animals required to be "unblemished" (Exodus 12:5; Leviticus 1:3) and of the LORD's own charge to Abraham, "Walk before Me and be blameless" (Genesis 17:1). That the same word spans ritual soundness and personal conduct is a lexical fact about the word's range, not by itself a claim about which sense is primary at 6:9.
- **הִתְהַלֵּךְ** (the Hithpael of *halak*, "walked with," 6:9) is the identical verb form used twice of Enoch two chapters earlier ("Enoch walked with God," 5:22, 24). The Hithpael stem carries a reciprocal, ongoing sense beyond plain physical walking. That the narrator reaches for the same rare form for both men, back to back in the text, is directly checkable; whether it is meant to mark Noah as a second Enoch is a reading this lesson does not adjudicate.
- **בְּרִית** (*berith*, "covenant," 6:18) is, by this lesson's own check of every chapter of Genesis before this verse against the real shipped BSB corpus, the first occurrence of the word "covenant" anywhere in this app's Bible text. The verb attached to it, however, is disputed: Genesis 6:18 uses the idiom *heqim berith* ("establish/raise up a covenant"), and whether that specific idiom means *making* a new covenant or *confirming* one already standing is a live, named grammatical dispute, taken up in Positions below.
- **תֵּבָה** (*tevah*, "ark," used throughout 6:14-9:18) occurs in the Hebrew Bible in only two narratives: Noah's ark, and the basket in which the infant Moses is set adrift (Exodus 2:3, 5). It is never the word used for the Ark of the Covenant, which is always *aron*. That the Bible's only other "ark" is a different Hebrew word attached to a different deliverance-through-water story is a checkable fact about the text's own vocabulary, reported here without claiming more for it than that.
- **יְקוּם** (*yequm*, "every living thing," 7:4, 23) occurs only three times in the entire Hebrew Bible -- these two verses and Deuteronomy 11:6 -- making it a genuinely rare word the narrator chooses to frame both the flood's announcement and its completed destruction.
- **גָּוַע** (*gava*, "to perish, expire," 6:17; 7:21) and **מָחָה** (*machah*, "to wipe out, blot out," 6:7; 7:4, 23) are the two verbs of ending used across this unit. *Machah*'s root recurs later for blotting out a name from God's own book (Exodus 32:32-33) and for blotting out sin itself (Psalm 51:1, 9; Isaiah 43:25, 44:22) -- the same root used of judgment here and of forgiveness there. That recurrence is checkable by concordance; what, if anything, it is meant to do across so many centuries of other texts is not something this lesson decides.
- **שִׁבְעָה שִׁבְעָה** ("seven seven," 7:2-3) is a repeated-cardinal idiom. Because Biblical Hebrew has no dedicated word for "pair," it sometimes doubles a number to express one of each: the same construction, "two two" (*shenayim shenayim*), is used for the animals boarding two by two at 7:9 and 7:15. Major English translations are split on how to render 7:2-3's "seven seven" as a result -- the KJV, NKJV, and NASB print "seven," while the ESV, NLT, HCSB, NRSV, and the 2011 NIV (reversing the 1984 NIV's own "seven") print "seven pairs." This app's own BSB follows the "pairs" rendering. That the translations disagree is independently checkable; which rendering is correct is a live dispute among translators, not a settled lexical fact.

**Two ancient Mesopotamian flood accounts, compared with the same care given to Atrahasis in this app's Genesis 2 lesson.** Genesis's flood narrative is routinely set beside two older Mesopotamian works: the Atrahasis epic, and Tablet XI of the Epic of Gilgamesh (in which the flood survivor is named Utnapishtim), which most specialists read as itself drawing on Atrahasis. The real similarities are well documented: a divine decision to destroy humanity, one person chosen to survive, the construction of a vessel, animals brought aboard, birds sent out to test whether the waters have receded, a sacrifice offered after landing, and a divine assurance against a repeat flood. One specific word is shared outright: the pitch used to caulk the ark (Hebrew *kofer*, 6:14) is cognate with the Akkadian word *kupru* used of the same material in the Mesopotamian accounts. The differences are just as specific and just as real. Atrahasis states the gods sent the flood because human overpopulation and "noise" disturbed their rest; Genesis states God responded to human violence and lawlessness (*hamas*, 6:11, 13). Atrahasis has the gods respond to the flood's aftermath with measures to limit future population growth (infertility, infant mortality, restrictions on marriage); Genesis has God command the opposite -- "be fruitful and multiply" -- alongside a new prohibition on killing. Gilgamesh's flood account involves multiple deities in conflict with each other, a storm described in vivid, almost sensory detail that Genesis's own account does not match, and a scene in which Utnapishtim deceives his neighbors about why he is building the boat -- none of which has a counterpart in Noah's story. Both the parallels and the differences are reported here from scholars who have read the primary texts directly; neither is resolved into a single verdict about literary dependence, borrowing, or polemical rewriting, which remains a live question this lesson does not need to settle to teach the comparison honestly.

**The flood's own vocabulary echoes Genesis 1's creation account, in reverse.** 7:11's "fountains of the great deep burst forth" uses *tehom* ("the deep"), the same word for the watery chaos of Genesis 1:2 ("darkness was over the surface of the deep"), and "the floodgates of the heavens were opened" undoes the very boundary Genesis 1:6-7 describes God setting between the waters above and the waters below. A widely reported reading (traced to Bernhard W. Anderson's 1978 *Journal of Biblical Literature* article, cited here from its bibliographic record and the secondary discussion of its thesis rather than an independent reading of the article itself) treats the flood as a deliberate undoing of creation's own ordering, pulling the world back toward its pre-creation state before the narrative, past this unit's own ending, reorders it again. The Hebrew vocabulary match between 7:11 and Genesis 1:2, 6-7 is independently checkable in translation; whether the narrator intended a full "de-creation" theology by it, as opposed to simply reusing the most natural words available for an immense flood, is a further claim this lesson reports rather than asserts.

## Positions

**1. Do 6:19-20's "two of every kind" and 7:2-3's seven pairs of clean animals agree, and if not, how is the difference accounted for?** The text itself states both numbers without reconciling them in so many words. Three kinds of answer are in print:

- **Supplementation.** Apologetics Press argues 7:2-3 is not a contradiction of 6:19-20 but an addition to it: God's first instruction set the general floor (two of every kind, enough to keep each kind alive), and the second instruction, given closer to boarding, specified extra clean animals and birds beyond that floor -- animals Noah would need just past this unit's own boundary, when he "built an altar to the LORD" and, "taking from every kind of clean animal and clean bird... offered burnt offerings on the altar" (8:20). On this reading, sacrificing from only the baseline two of a kind would have risked that kind's extinction; the extra five pairs solve a problem the first instruction did not yet need to address.
- **A translation and idiom question layered on top of the supplementation reading.** Answers in Genesis argues the Hebrew "seven seven" (*shivah shivah*) at 7:2-3 is itself the ordinary way Biblical Hebrew doubles a cardinal number to express pairs, paralleling "two two" (*shenayim shenayim*) at 7:9 and 7:15 -- so the clean animals numbered seven *pairs* (fourteen individuals), not seven animals total. The article states plainly that legitimate disagreement over this rendering remains among English translations and among Christian readers, which is independently checkable in the split among translations reported in Context above.
- **A source-critical reading: two originally separate accounts, not one instruction supplemented by a second.** Documentary-hypothesis scholarship (reported here via Jona Lendering's summary at Livius.org, which quotes the instructions in its own translation rather than this app's BSB) attributes the "two of every kind" instruction (6:19-20) and the flood's 150-day duration (7:24) to a Priestly source, and the seven-pairs instruction (7:2-3) and the 40-day rain figure (7:4, 12) to a second, older source (conventionally labeled Yahwist or J), later combined by an editor into the single narrative now in Genesis. On this reading, the two animal counts do not describe one instruction followed by a clarifying second instruction; they are vestiges of two different accounts that were never fully harmonized by whoever wove them together, and the same analysis credits the Priestly source specifically with 7:19-20's "all the high mountains under all the heavens were covered" language, which the next question below treats on its own terms.

This lesson reports all three without ranking them: the harmonizing readings treat the two counts as compatible once the idiom and the sacrificial need are accounted for, while the source-critical reading treats the two counts as evidence the final text has more than one hand behind it. Readers can weigh the Hebrew idiom argument and the two-source argument against each other directly; both are checkable against the same verses.

**2. What does "I will establish My covenant with you" mean at 6:18 -- is this the Bible's first covenant, or the confirmation of one already standing?** The Hebrew idiom is *heqim berith*, literally "raise up/establish a covenant." Peter Gentry and Stephen Wellum (reported here through Brian Collins's direct engagement with their argument, their own book not independently read for this lesson) argue the idiom *heqim berith* is used throughout the Hebrew Bible only to *confirm* a covenant already in force, never to inaugurate a new one, and conclude that Genesis 6:18 confirms an unstated prior covenant -- on their account, a "covenant of creation" implicit from Genesis 1-2 onward, not a brand-new arrangement with Noah. Brian Collins disputes the claim that the idiom is exclusively confirmatory, citing H.C. Leupold, Victor P. Hamilton, Douglas Stuart, and (via a further secondary reference) Tremper Longman III for the reading that *heqim berith* can denote making a new covenant, and argues no clearly identifiable prior covenant exists in the text for 6:18 to be confirming. This grammatical dispute runs alongside, but is a separate question from, how this app's own Genesis 3 lesson already reported the Reformed tradition reading Genesis 3:15 as the inauguration of a "covenant of grace," decades before the word "covenant" itself ever appears in the text -- a reminder that a tradition's own doctrine of when a covenant begins does not have to track the first appearance of the Hebrew word for it, and this lesson does not treat the word's first occurrence here as settling a larger covenant-theology question it was never positioned to settle.

**3. How far did the flood reach -- and this is a real, long-running, named disagreement, not a case where one side is simply not paying attention to the text.** 7:19-20 (BSB) states: "the waters completely inundated the earth, so that all the high mountains under all the heavens were covered. The waters rose and covered the mountaintops to a depth of fifteen cubits." Three positions, each with real defenders who have read the text closely:

- **A literal, planetary-global flood.** John C. Whitcomb Jr. and Henry M. Morris's 1961 *The Genesis Flood* is the foundational modern statement of this view, read here through later secondary reports rather than independently in this pass. Jason Lisle and Tim Chaffey (Answers in Genesis) argue the text supplies two separate universalizing phrases -- "all the high mountains" and "under all the heavens" -- and that for a local-flood reading to work, both phrases would have to be hyperbole at once, which they argue the context does not support; they also cite R. Laird Harris and the 1871 Jamieson-Fausset-Brown commentary as holding the same reading.
- **A flood "universal" to humanity and its associated animals, but not geographically global.** Hugh Ross (Reasons to Believe) states his own position plainly: "I believe the flood of Noah was universal but not global." By "universal" he means every human being and every animal living alongside humans outside the ark died; by "not global" he means the water did not cover the entire planet, only the region visible to Noah and sufficient to accomplish that universal human death. Ross points to 2 Peter's own qualifying language (2 Peter 2:5's description of the flood as coming on "its ungodly people," and 2 Peter 3:6's "the world of that time," using the Greek *kosmos*) and to the Hebrew word rendered "dry land" at 7:22 as evidence the text's own vocabulary does not require covering the whole planet's surface.
- **A local, regional flood described in deliberately hyperbolic, theologically loaded language.** Tremper Longman III and John H. Walton's 2018 *The Lost World of the Flood* (reported here through independently-read secondary summaries, not the book itself) argues the flood was a real, devastating regional event in Mesopotamia, described using the Bible's own ancient idiom of cosmic, universalizing language for rhetorical and theological effect -- "global in its significance" without being global "in its physical scope." On this reading, 7:19-20's wording is doing the same kind of exaggeration-for-effect work ancient Near Eastern royal and disaster accounts regularly did, not making a claim a modern reader should test against a map.

This lesson does not adjudicate among these three. Each has to answer a real question the others raise: the global reading has to explain how a flood that covers literally every mountain on the planet leaves the quantity-of-water and post-flood ecology questions some geologists have raised about it; the "universal but not global" reading has to explain why 7:19's own wording says "all the high mountains," not merely "the region Noah could see"; and the hyperbole reading has to explain why a reader should trust the same narrator's other numbers (the ark's dimensions, the day counts) as plain prose while treating this specific clause as rhetorical convention. None of those counter-questions is answered here; they are left, with the positions themselves, for the reader to weigh.

**4. Was Noah's "blameless" status in 6:9 an absolute moral standing, or only a standing relative to an especially corrupt generation?** Rashi reports two ancient readings of the same four words, "righteous... in his generation," without choosing between them: one line of tradition (via Genesis Rabbah) reads the phrase to Noah's credit -- he was righteous even surrounded by his generation's corruption, and would have been more righteous still in a better one. A second line (via the Talmud, Sanhedrin 108a) reads it to his discredit -- he counted as righteous only by comparison with an unusually wicked generation, and would have counted for little next to Abraham. Both readings take the same four Hebrew words as their evidence and reach opposite conclusions about what the qualifying phrase "in his generation" is doing there. This lesson reports both without resolving which reading the phrase was meant to carry.

## Literary Design

**A repeated obedience formula brackets the ark's construction and boarding.** 6:22 states, "So Noah did everything precisely as God had commanded him," and 7:5 restates it in nearly identical words: "And Noah did all that the LORD had commanded him." The first closes the building instructions (6:14-21); the second closes the boarding instructions (7:1-4). That the narrator uses the same formula twice, at the close of two different instruction sets, is directly checkable; it brackets the whole "preparation" half of the unit with the same single claim about Noah stated twice, before anything else happens.

**The destruction of 7:21-23 is reported with a deliberate, almost liturgical repetition, not narrated once and left there.** The same event -- every outside creature's death -- is restated across three consecutive verses in slightly different groupings: "every living thing that moved upon the earth... birds, livestock, animals, every creature that swarms... and all mankind" (7:21); "all that was on dry land, everything that had the breath of life in its nostrils" (7:22); "every living thing on the face of the earth... man and livestock, crawling creatures and birds of the air" (7:23), closing with "they were blotted out from the earth, and only Noah and those with him in the ark remained." Each verse re-lists overlapping categories (birds, livestock, wild animals, crawling things, mankind) rather than simply saying "everything died" once. This repeated-merism technique -- restating a totality several ways in a row -- is a recognizable feature of Hebrew narrative emphasis; whether this specific instance is best called "liturgical" (as this lesson's introduction described it) or simply emphatic is this lesson's own descriptive label, not a cited scholarly term, and a reader should test the label against the verses rather than accept it as settled terminology.

**This unit sits at the inclusio's low point, not its resolution.** As the Context section above states, this app's preceding Genesis lesson identified 6:5 and 8:21 as bracketing the flood narrative with nearly identical diagnoses of humanity's heart, under opposite divine responses. This unit (6:9-7:24) runs entirely within that bracket's dark half: it opens after 6:5's diagnosis has already been given and the decision to destroy already made, and it closes at 7:24's low point -- 150 days of water prevailing, with no narrated turn yet toward mercy. The turn itself ("But God remembered Noah," 8:1) falls on the other side of this unit's own boundary. A reader who stops at 7:24 is, by this unit's own design, left at the story's worst moment; that placement is a fact about where this lesson's passage boundary falls, not a claim about what the larger narrative is doing with the delay.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key: the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step, and a different passage element, situation, or response would be equally legitimate.

**Passage element.** Genesis 6:14-22. God gives Noah exact, costly building instructions for a disaster that has not yet produced a single drop of visible rain, and 6:22 states simply, "Noah did everything precisely as God had commanded him."

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the instructions are detailed and specific (dimensions, materials, rooms, a door), and the text gives no indication any rain had begun before Noah started building -- Hebrews 11:7's later description of Noah as "warned about things not yet seen" matches this unit's own sequence, in which the warning (6:13, 17) precedes any narrated rainfall by what the surrounding chapters imply is a long span of building time. Inference: for an audience hearing this account, the specificity of the instructions and the total absence of visible confirming evidence before Noah acts would have marked his obedience as costly and sustained, not a single decision made once a flood was already underway. The Context section above supplies what the text itself says about this sequence; nothing in it lets this lesson say more about what first hearers took from it than that.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Acting on a specific, costly instruction before any of the evidence that would vindicate it is visible to anyone else is a real kind of obedience, distinguishable from acting only once the risk has already become obvious to everyone.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a real warning is given, specific and costly preparation is required, and no confirming evidence is visible yet to the person acting or to onlookers. Not carried forward: the LORD's own audible instructions, the scale of a worldwide flood, and anything specific to Noah's own household. The bridge holds only where these features match the modern case.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed; nothing in 6:14-22 is treated here as a promise that every costly, evidence-ahead-of-its-time preparation will turn out to have been warranted.

**Modern domain and situation.** Civic life. A city engineer recommends reinforcing an aging levee based on a flood-risk model, even though the river has not flooded within living memory and the town council is skeptical the cost is justified by a risk nobody can currently see.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: sustained, specific preparation ahead of visible evidence (not blind action -- the engineer's recommendation rests on a stated model, the way Noah's rested on a stated instruction, not on no reason at all). One faithful response is to put the specific risk model and its assumptions in writing, propose the specific, costly remedy it implies, and keep advocating for it through the period when no flood arrives to prove the point, rather than letting the absence of visible danger quietly end the recommendation.

**Cautions.** This example does not say every costly recommendation nobody else can presently verify is therefore warranted; Noah's instructions came from a stated, named source (6:13-21's own recorded divine speech), and this bridge does not transfer that specific warrant to every risk model a modern reader might propose. It should not be used to shut down legitimate questions about cost, evidence, or competing priorities; the councilmembers' skepticism in this example is not treated here as equivalent to the corrupt "violence" the passage's own context names as its subject. The passage is not a civil-engineering text; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing, and the learner may find it does not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Genesis 6:19-20 and 7:2-3 give two different animal counts. What in the text itself -- not any later harmonizing or source-critical tradition -- would help a reader decide whether these are one instruction clarified by a second, or two accounts imperfectly joined?
2. 7:19-20's "all the high mountains under all the heavens were covered" has three real, named readings reported above. What would it take, from the text alone (not from geology, and not from a prior commitment to one reading), to judge between them?
3. The word *tevah* ("ark") is used only for Noah's vessel and for the infant Moses' basket. If that recurrence is doing deliberate narrative work rather than simply being the available word for "floating container," what would you expect to find elsewhere in Scripture that you have not yet checked?
4. 6:5 and 8:21 state nearly the same diagnosis of humanity's heart under opposite divine responses, and this unit ends entirely inside the first, unresolved half of that bracket. What would change about how you read 7:21-24's destruction if you read it without yet knowing that 8:21 restates the same diagnosis under a promise never to destroy again?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate what happens in Genesis 6:9-7:24 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) State, in the text's own words, what 6:19-20 instructs Noah to bring and what 7:2-3 instructs him to bring, before saying what any harmonizing or source-critical reading concludes about the difference. (c) Name one place elsewhere in Scripture where this passage's language or imagery (the ark, the covenant, the flood itself) visibly reappears, and say what kind of connection it is.

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** Outline how you would teach this passage in five minutes, with at least one point in each segment you could cite to a source or a verse. A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the plain sequence of 6:9-7:24 and where this unit sits inside the larger flood inclusio (see Context); (1:00-2:30) the two animal-count instructions and the "heqim berith" covenant question, each with its own named positions kept separate on purpose (see Positions 1-2); (2:30-3:30) the extent of the flood, with all three named readings stated fairly (see Positions 3); (3:30-4:30) where this passage's language travels later in Scripture, naming the evidence label of each example, not just listing it (see the reviewed connections in prompt 5); (4:30-5:00) the boundary, stated on purpose as the last thing taught (prompt 4).

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first; the discipline of this prompt is that you do the forming, not that you pick from a list. If you are stuck, here is one worked candidate, stated at full strength: a literal, planetary-global flood as deep as Mount Everest, drained within a year, is not geologically attested in the ice-core, sediment, and ocean-floor records mainstream earth science has examined, so a reader committed to a global reading is choosing a text's plain words over a large, independently cross-checked body of physical evidence. Before you answer it, state what each of the three positions in this lesson's own Positions 3 would say in response -- the global-flood sources cited here (Whitcomb and Morris, Lisle and Chaffey) make their own scientific case and should be represented in their own terms, not only characterized by their critics. A second worked candidate, running the other direction: if 7:19-20's "all the high mountains... were covered" is read as deliberate rhetorical hyperbole rather than a claim about physical extent, a reader needs some textual marker inside Genesis itself (not an appeal to ancient Near Eastern convention alone) that would tell them when the same narrator has switched from plain reporting (the ark's cubit measurements, the day counts) to this kind of exaggeration-for-effect language, and 7:19-20 itself does not announce that switch any more clearly than the numbers around it do.

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Genesis 6:19-20 and 7:2-3 do not, in their own wording, explain why two different animal counts are given; every account of how they relate -- supplementation, a Hebrew numeral idiom, or two source traditions -- is a reading brought to the text, named as such in Positions 1 above, not a mechanism the text states for itself.
