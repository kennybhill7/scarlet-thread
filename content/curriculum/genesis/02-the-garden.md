Genesis 2:4-25 is often taught as if it simply continues Genesis 1 in more detail. It is more useful to read it as its own account with its own vocabulary, its own pace, and its own two hardest words -- and to practice, on those two words, the same discipline this course uses throughout: telling apart a textual or lexical observation (what a word's actual range of use is, checkable in a concordance), a named position (what a tradition or scholar concludes the text establishes), and this lesson's own inference (labeled as such, never presented as either of the first two). Genesis 2 has two words where that discipline matters most -- *tsela* ("rib" or "side," 2:21-22) and *ezer* ("helper," 2:18, 20) -- and a block of real, live theological disagreement about what the chapter establishes about marriage. Both are handled below without this lesson adjudicating between the named positions it reports.

## Context

**What happens, in order (2:4-25).** The passage opens with a formula -- "this is the account of" -- before any plant has sprouted, because no rain has fallen and no man exists to work the ground (2:4-5). A mist waters the whole surface, and the LORD God forms the man from the dust of the ground and breathes life into him (2:6-7). God plants a garden in Eden and places the man there, with the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil at its center (2:8-9). A river flows out of Eden and divides into four named headwaters, two of which -- the Tigris and the Euphrates -- are still identifiable rivers today (2:10-14). The man is placed in the garden to cultivate and keep it, and is given one command with one named consequence: eat from any tree but one, or die (2:15-17). God then states that the man's aloneness is "not good," forms every animal from the same ground the man was formed from, and brings them to the man, who names each one -- but no suitable helper is found among them (2:18-20). God causes a deep sleep to fall on the man, takes something from his side, and builds it into a woman, whom the man recognizes at once in poetry: "bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh" (2:21-23). The chapter closes with a statement about marriage (2:24) and a simple fact about the couple's state before the next chapter changes it: "naked, and they were not ashamed" (2:25, BSB). This is a plain retelling in the text's own order; what the chapter's harder details signify is the rest of this lesson's work.

**Two accounts, read together.** Genesis 1:1-2:3 and Genesis 2:4-25 are two passages about the same creation, told in noticeably different styles. The first moves in six ordered days from cosmos to Adam, using the divine name "God" (Elohim) throughout and a refrain of seven repeated phrases; the second moves from a waterless ground outward, using the two-part name "the LORD God" (Yahweh Elohim) that appears nowhere in chapter 1, and narrates God forming, planting, and building rather than speaking creation into being. How to read this difference is a real, reported disagreement rather than a settled question this lesson resolves. One position, held across a wide range of modern commentary, treats Genesis 1-11 as a distinguishable literary unit and treats chapter 2 as a second, complementary account that narrows the lens to day six -- Bill T. Arnold's *Genesis* (New Cambridge Bible Commentary, 2008) is structured on exactly this unit boundary, and David L. Petersen, writing for the Society of Biblical Literature's Bible Odyssey project, describes Genesis 1-11 as a connected "Primeval History." A second, source-critical position, associated with the documentary hypothesis, reads the two accounts as originally separate compositions -- the first commonly assigned to a Priestly source (P), the second to an older, non-Priestly source conventionally labeled Jahwist (J) -- later combined into their present sequence; Wikipedia's survey of this scholarship reports the traditional dating of P to around the sixth century BC and J several centuries earlier, while a sidebar in the Biblical Archaeology Society's library states plainly that "recent scholarship has found serious flaws in details of the documentary hypothesis" even as the basic P/J division of these two chapters remains a standard reference point. Both positions have to explain the same checkable textual fact: chapter 1 never uses "the LORD," and chapter 2 uses "the LORD God" eleven times starting at its fourth verse. This lesson reports both readings and adjudicates neither; readers who want to track the question further will meet it again in the units that follow.

**Formed from dust, and a comparison worth handling carefully.** "The LORD God formed man from the dust of the ground and breathed the breath of life into his nostrils, and the man became a living being" (2:7, BSB). Scholars regularly set this alongside a specific Mesopotamian text, the Atrahasis epic, in which humanity is made from clay mixed with the flesh and blood of a slain god. A parallel-text aggregator (intertextual.bible) quotes the relevant Atrahasis line as "Nintu mixed clay / With his flesh and blood." The comparison is real and worth teaching, and so is its limit: Atrahasis has humans made from both clay and divine material combined, and no Mesopotamian comparandum anyone consulted for this lesson has a god breathing life into the formed figure the way Genesis 2:7 does. A second, more specific comparison is sometimes drawn to a Sumerian myth, *Enki and Ninhursag*: when the god Enki falls ill, the goddess Ninhursag creates a healing deity for each ailing body part, and the one who heals his rib is named Nin-ti, a name that puns in Sumerian on both "lady of the rib" and "lady who makes live." The historian Gerda Lerner (*The Creation of Patriarchy*, Oxford University Press, 1986, pp. 184-185) proposes that Eve's own name -- linked to life at 3:20 -- and her formation from a rib may echo this same pun. The proposal has a real caution attached to it: a cuneiformist, Jeremiah Peterson, reads the Ninti episode itself as "an example of folk etymology," arguing the ancient compilers chose her among the healing deities for the wordplay her name allowed, not for any prior connection to childbirth or life -- which, if right, makes the parallel to Eve looser than it first appears. Both the comparison and the caution are reported here; neither is resolved.

**The garden and its rivers.** The passage places the garden "in Eden, in the east" (2:8) and names four rivers branching from a single source: the Pishon, which "winds through the whole land of Havilah, where there is gold" (2:11-12); the Gihon, which "winds through the whole land of Cush" (2:13); the Hiddekel, "along the east side of Assyria" -- the same river elsewhere called the Tigris -- and the Euphrates (2:14). Two of the four are rivers with a known, present-day course; the other two, and Eden itself, are not confidently locatable. This app's own place data (OpenBible.info's geocoding dataset) marks Eden "unlocated": of several candidate identifications in that dataset, the highest-scoring one is "unknown location," so no marker is placed on a map for it here. That is a fact about the limits of present identification, not a claim that the text's geography is invented; the chapter names real river-types (gold-bearing lands, named regions) the way a writer names real places, whether or not a modern reader can still find them on a map.

**Two Hebrew words worth slowing down on.** The lexical facts below are independent of what any tradition concludes from them.

- **צֵלָע** (*tsela*, "rib"/"side," 2:21-22) occurs 41 times in the Hebrew Bible. Genesis 2:21-22 is the only place any major English translation, including the BSB, renders it "rib"; everywhere else -- a building's side-chamber, a hillside, a plank, a leaf of a door -- it is rendered "side." Brown-Driver-Briggs (via Bible Hub) glosses the word "rib" specifically at this passage while cataloguing "side" as its ordinary architectural sense elsewhere. That is the lexical fact; what it should be translated here, and what follows from the choice, is the first of two named disputes taken up in Positions below.
- **עֵזֶר** (*ezer*, "helper," 2:18, 20) is the word God uses for what the man lacks before the woman is formed: "I will make for him a suitable helper" (2:18, BSB). Brown-Driver-Briggs (via Bible Hub) defines the word's core sense as "effective strength supplied from outside oneself, meeting exigent circumstances" -- and the word's most frequent subject across the Hebrew Bible is not a human assistant but God: "He is our help and our shield" (Psalm 33:20); "my help comes from the LORD" (Psalm 121:1-2); "He is their help and their shield" (Psalm 115:9-11). That distribution is a checkable lexical fact, not yet an argument about Genesis 2:18; what traditions and scholars conclude from it is the second named dispute in Positions below.

**A verb worth noticing at 2:22.** Where 2:7 says God "formed" (*yatsar*) the man, 2:22 uses a different verb for the woman: "the LORD God fashioned the rib... into a woman" (BSB), from the Hebrew *banah*, "to build." Brown-Driver-Briggs (via Bible Hub) documents *banah* as the ordinary verb for building a physical structure -- a city, a wall, an altar -- and notes its extended, figurative use for building up a household or a family line, including the idiom used of a childless woman seeking to be "built up" through a servant's children (Genesis 16:2; 30:3; compare 2 Samuel 7:27, "I will build you a house"). That the text chooses a construction verb rather than repeating 2:7's "formed" is a checkable fact about word choice; whether it is doing deliberate theological work or is simply the natural verb for assembling a rib into a body is not something this lesson decides.

## Literary Design

**A reversed phrase at the seam, reported with its own caution.** Genesis 2:4 is the hinge between the two accounts: "This is the account of the heavens and the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made them" (BSB). In the underlying Hebrew, the verse's two halves are commonly reported to name the same pair in reverse order -- "heavens and earth" in the first half, "earth and heavens" in the second -- a small chiasm that some readers take as marking the transition from chapter 1's heavens-first, cosmic frame to chapter 2's earth-first, human-centered one. The clearest statement of this pattern available to this lesson comes from the Biblical Chiasm Exchange, an aggregator site, credited there only to an anonymous contributor ("SDS"); a commenter on the same page disputes the exact boundaries of the structure. BSB's own English does not preserve the reversal (both halves simply read "the heavens and the earth... them"), so a reader working from English alone cannot verify this one directly -- it is reported here as a widely noted feature, not as independently confirmed by this lesson's own check of the Hebrew.

**A delay, not a decoration.** The chapter opens with three stated absences -- no shrub, no plant, no rain, and explicitly "no man to cultivate the ground" (2:5) -- before anything is formed. That ordering holds the reader in an unfinished state for two verses before 2:7 resolves it, and the same pattern repeats at the chapter's center: God states that the man's aloneness is "not good" (2:18) and then holds that gap open through the entire naming-of-the-animals episode (2:19-20) before resolving it at 2:21-22. Read this way, the chapter is built around two stated lacks and two resolutions, not simply a list of acts in sequence. Whether this is the author's deliberate device or this lesson's own pattern-finding is worth testing the way the chiasm note above should be tested: by checking whether the structure is visible in the text's own wording, not only in a label applied to it afterward.

## Positions

Two disputes are reported here, each from a named source's own published or publicly stated position, in the text's own order. Neither is resolved by this lesson.

**Rib or side? -- the lexical dispute over *tsela*.** Virtually every major English translation, including the BSB ("He took one of his ribs," 2:21), renders *tsela* "rib" at this passage. Ancient Jewish interpreters already disagreed about this rendering, and the disagreement is verifiable at the primary texts rather than only reported secondhand. Targum Onkelos reads "rib" outright -- "וּנְסִיב חֲדָא מֵעִלְעוֹהִי," "he took one of his ribs," using the Aramaic cognate of the same anatomical word. Rashi (11th century), commenting on the same verse, instead glosses the word as "side" -- "מִצַּלְעֹתָיו... of his sides" -- citing the architectural use of the same word for "the side of the tabernacle" in Exodus 26:20, and reports a rabbinic tradition (Eruvin 18a; Sanhedrin 39a) that the first human was "created with two faces" before being divided -- a dual or two-sided being differentiated into man and woman at this point, not a male to whom a separate female part was later added. (A secondary survey located during this lesson's research additionally attributes "side" to Ibn Ezra, Rambam, and Ralbag, and "rib" to Targum Yonatan and an opinion in the Midrash; those four attributions were not independently checked against their own primary texts here and are reported only at this hedge, not asserted as confirmed.) In contemporary scholarship, Ziony Zevit, Distinguished Professor of Biblical Literature and Northwest Semitic Languages at American Jewish University, argues in *Biblical Archaeology Review* (Sept/Oct 2015) that the word's ordinary architectural sense ("something off-center, lateral to a main structure") should govern here too, and proposes -- more specifically than "side" alone -- that the word names "a non-specific... lateral limb," and that the limb in view may be the *os baculum*, the penis bone most male mammals have and humans lack; on his reading, the passage explains that absence. Zevit's proposal is his own; it is not the position of Rashi or Onkelos cited above, who read either "side" or "rib" without his anatomical argument. Brown-Driver-Briggs's own entry (reported in Context, above) notes that the word's sense is not fully settled even among lexicographers: "The basic meaning of the word in Hebrew is not clear... if *tsela* does mean 'rib,' it does so only in this one passage" -- a line this lesson found quoted via a Biblical Archaeology Society piece, not independently checked against Fabry's own publication.

**What does the chapter establish about marriage? -- a live, disputed question.** Genesis 2:18-24 is cited directly by two organized, published statements that read it in opposite directions, and by an influential academic essay that reads it a third way. The Danvers Statement (Council on Biblical Manhood and Womanhood, 1987) affirms that "distinctions in masculine and feminine roles are ordained by God as part of the created order (Gen. 2:18, 21-24...)" and that "Adam's headship in marriage was established by God before the Fall, and was not a result of sin (Gen. 2:16-18, 21-24, 3:1-13...)" -- reading the order in which the man and woman are formed as establishing an order of authority between them. Christians for Biblical Equality's statement "Men, Women, and Biblical Equality" cites the same verses for the opposite conclusion: "the word 'helper' (*ezer*) used to designate woman in Genesis 2:18 refers to God in most instances of Old Testament usage... [and] conveys no implication whatsoever of female subordination or inferiority," that "the word 'suitable' (*kenegdo*) denotes equality and adequacy," and that "nothing in Genesis teaches a 'creation order' giving man authority over woman" -- on this reading, Adam's rule over Eve is a consequence of the Fall announced in 3:16, not a pattern established in chapter 2. Phyllis Trible, late professor of Old Testament at Union Theological Seminary, argued a related but distinct position in "Depatriarchalizing in Biblical Interpretation" (*Journal of the American Academy of Religion* 41.1, 1973): that the Hebrew *'adham* in this chapter names an undifferentiated human, not yet specifically male, until the differentiation into *'ish* ("man") and *'ishshah* ("woman") at 2:23 -- a reading reported as her position, not independently checked here against her own full essay. That differentiation-at-2:21-23 observation is the same shape, from a different tradition entirely, as the rabbinic "two faces" reading Rashi reports above; the two are not the same argument and this lesson does not treat them as corroborating one another, only as worth noticing side by side. Where these positions agree: all three read 2:18-24 as making some kind of normative statement about marriage, not merely describing one ancient couple. Where they differ, sharply: whether the sequence of creation establishes an order of authority, and whether *ezer* and *kenegdo* carry any connotation of subordination at all. This lesson reports the dispute; it is not resolved here.

**A pastoral-safety note.** Genesis 2:18-24 has been invoked on both sides of real disputes about authority in marriage, and in some cases to justify one spouse controlling or isolating another. This lesson reports the lexical and positional dispute only. If you are in a relationship where either "headship" or "equality" language is being used against you -- to control you, isolate you, or excuse harm -- a qualified person, not a study note, is the right next step.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key: the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step, and a different passage element, situation, or response would be equally legitimate.

**Passage element.** Genesis 2:18-20. God states that the man's aloneness is "not good," then brings every animal to him to be named -- and only after that whole process does the text report the result: "for Adam no suitable helper was found" (2:20, BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text does not resolve the stated lack at 2:18 by simply presenting the solution; it first shows the man surveying every available animal, one by one, naming each. Inference: for a first hearer, the naming scene demonstrates rather than merely states that nothing already present in the garden answered the actual lack -- the search has to be shown, not asserted, before the reader can accept that something genuinely new was needed.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** A felt lack is not necessarily met by what is already on hand, however numerous the options; ruling out the available alternatives, rather than assuming none would work, is part of taking the lack seriously.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a real need, a reasonable survey of existing options, and a conclusion -- reached only after the survey, not before it -- that none of them answers the need. Not carried forward: the specific content of Eden's animals, the divine speech, and anything specific to marriage; this bridge is about the method of recognizing a genuine gap, not about who may fill one.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed; nothing in 2:18-20 is treated here as a promise that every lack will be resolved.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. A small medical practice's office manager notices patients waiting too long at the front desk and first tries adding a second general receptionist to the existing rotation.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: discernment (correctly identifying what a problem actually requires before acting). A faithful response follows the pattern of 2:19-20 rather than skipping it: survey what is already being tried (two receptionists handling the same mixed workload) before concluding, only after that survey, that the actual gap is a specific, different skill -- insurance verification -- that no amount of additional general staffing supplies.

**Cautions.** The example does not say that generalists are inferior or that every problem requires a specialist; many genuinely are solved by more of the same. It should not be used to justify skipping a real evaluation in favor of a predetermined answer, and it says nothing about people -- it is a method analogy about diagnosing a gap, not a claim about anyone's worth relative to a role they do or do not fill.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Genesis 2 uses "the LORD God" eleven times; Genesis 1 never uses "the LORD" at all. What in the text itself, apart from either position reported in Context, bears on why, and what kind of evidence would count toward either reading?
2. The chapter states two things are "not good" or incomplete (the ground with no one to work it, 2:5; the man alone, 2:18) and resolves each only after showing, not just asserting, that the available alternative does not work (rain is needed, not just a mist, by implication; no animal is a suitable helper). What would it look like to find a third such pattern in the chapter, if one exists?
3. *Tsela* occurs 41 times and means "side" in every other instance. If you set aside every tradition's conclusion about what that means for Genesis 2:21-22, what is the strongest argument you can construct for "rib" being right here anyway, using only the text and the lexical data in Context?
4. The two named statements in Positions (Danvers and CBE) cite the same verses for opposite conclusions. What specific textual detail would have to be true for one reading and false for the other -- that is, what would actually settle the disagreement, rather than restating it more forcefully?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate what happens in Genesis 2:4-25 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) State, in the text's own words, what *tsela* names elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible before saying what any tradition concludes it means in 2:21-22. (c) Name one place elsewhere in Scripture where this chapter's language (a word, an event, a formula) visibly reappears, and say what kind of connection it is: a quotation, a named reference, or something else.

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** Outline how you would teach this passage in five minutes, with at least one point in each segment you could cite to a source or a verse. A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) where the chapter sits relative to Genesis 1 and the plain sequence of the narrative (see Context); (1:00-2:00) the garden's geography and the ANE comparisons, stated with their limits (see Context); (2:00-3:30) the two contested words, *tsela* and *ezer*, read literally first and then through the named positions (see Context and Positions); (3:30-4:30) where the chapter's language travels later in Scripture, naming the evidence label of each example, not just listing it (see the table in prompt 5); (4:30-5:00) one open question, stated on purpose as the last thing taught (prompt 4).

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first; the point of this prompt is that you do the forming, not that you pick from a list. If you are stuck, here are two worked candidates running in opposite directions, so the exercise is not only "attack one side." First: the complementarian reading of 2:18-24 treats a narrative detail (who was formed first, or who named whom) as establishing a permanent authority structure that the text itself never states in those terms -- Genesis 2 never says "and therefore the man shall rule the woman"; that statement appears only at 3:16, inside the judgment scene, which both the Danvers Statement and CBE's own reasoning treat as a consequence of the Fall rather than the original design when it suits each side's larger argument. Before you answer it, note that the Danvers Statement itself locates Adam's headship at 2:16-18 and 21-24, deliberately before 3:1-13, so the objection has to engage that claim specifically, not a weaker version of it. Second, running the other direction: the egalitarian reading leans heavily on *ezer* being used of God elsewhere to argue the word carries no connotation of lesser status -- but every one of those divine occurrences describes God helping a weaker party from a position of unmatched strength, which does not by itself settle whether the word, applied to a human "helper," implies equal standing, lesser standing, or is simply unmarked for status and has to be read from context rather than from the word's distribution alone. Before you answer it, note that CBE's own argument also rests on *kenegdo* ("corresponding to him") denoting equality, which is a second, independent claim the objection above does not address.

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Genesis 2 does not state, in its own words, that the order in which the man and woman were formed determines which of them has authority over the other; that conclusion is drawn by later interpreters (reported in Positions above) from the order, not asserted by the chapter itself. (b) Genesis 2:24 ("a man will leave his father and mother and be united to his wife") does not, by itself, specify the number of parties it describes, define what "one flesh" requires beyond what the verse states, or address a situation the verse does not mention; later texts (reported in the connections below) draw further conclusions from it, which are those texts' own statements, not restatements of what 2:24 alone says.

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection from this passage and defend it: name its type (for example quotation, explicit reference, allusion), give its evidence label (explicit, strong, plausible, or devotional), and point to the words in both texts that support that grade. The reviewed connections attached to this lesson sit at different tiers, which is the point of the exercise:

Choose one row and defend it in your own words: name its type, its evidence label, and point to the words in both texts that support that grade. Or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` instead: name a candidate connection you were drawn to that is not in this table (Mark 10:6-9, which carries the same quotation as Matthew 19, is one legitimate candidate) and say what evidence would need to exist for it to earn its own row.
