Genesis 14 is the only chapter in Genesis where Abram goes to war. Four eastern kings defeat five kings of the Jordan plain, carry off the goods of Sodom and Gomorrah, and take Abram's nephew with them -- "They also carried off Abram's nephew Lot and his possessions, since Lot was living in Sodom" (14:12). Abram pursues, wins, and brings everyone back. Then, in three verses (14:18-20), a figure appears whom the text has not mentioned before and never mentions again: "Then Melchizedek king of Salem brought out bread and wine," blesses Abram, and receives "a tenth of everything." Those three verses travel further across Scripture than almost anything else in the chapter. Psalm 110:4 names Melchizedek once; Hebrews quotes that psalm three times and then, in Hebrews 7:1-10, retells the Genesis scene and builds an argument on it. This lesson follows that path verse by verse, checks which details each later text uses and which it leaves out, and reports -- without deciding -- three real disputes the scene has carried for two thousand years: who Melchizedek was, what his bread and wine signify, and where Salem was.

## Context

**What happens, in order (14:1-24).** The chapter opens with a list of eastern kings: "Amraphel king of Shinar, Arioch king of Ellasar, Chedorlaomer king of Elam, and Tidal king of Goiim" (14:1). Five kings of the plain, including the kings of Sodom and Gomorrah, had served Chedorlaomer: "For twelve years they had been subject to Chedorlaomer, but in the thirteenth year they rebelled" (14:4). The eastern coalition campaigns south through the Transjordan (14:5-7), then meets the five kings in the Valley of Siddim -- "four kings against five" (14:9). The valley "was full of tar pits" (14:10); the plain's kings flee, and the victors take Sodom's goods and Lot (14:11-12). A survivor reports to "Abram the Hebrew" (14:13), who "mobilized the 318 trained men born in his household" (14:14), attacks by night, and pursues "as far as Hobah, north of Damascus" (14:15). "He retrieved all the goods, as well as his relative Lot and his possessions, together with the women and the rest of the people" (14:16). On his return, the king of Sodom comes out to meet him "in the Valley of Shaveh (that is, the King's Valley)" (14:17). Before that king speaks, Melchizedek brings bread and wine, blesses Abram -- "Blessed be Abram by God Most High, Creator of heaven and earth" -- and blesses God, and "Then Abram gave Melchizedek a tenth of everything" (14:18-20). The king of Sodom then makes his offer: "Give me the people, but take the goods for yourself" (14:21). Abram refuses with an oath: he will "not accept even a thread, or a strap of a sandal, or anything that belongs to you, lest you should say, 'I have made Abram rich.'" (14:23), keeping back only "what my men have eaten" and his allies' share (14:24). That is the plain sequence; what any of it signifies is the rest of this lesson's work.

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens names stage 5 "Genesis 12 – Malachi — Israel: The Twelve Tribes"; this chapter falls inside that span, as the Genesis 12 lesson before it does. The chapter depends on Genesis 13, where Lot chose the plain of the Jordan and "pitched his tent toward Sodom" (13:12), with the narrator's note that "the men of Sodom were wicked, sinning greatly against the LORD" (13:13). By 14:12, Lot is "living in Sodom."

**The eastern kings: what can and cannot be said.** None of the four eastern kings has been securely matched to a known historical ruler.

- *Amraphel.* The Genesis volume of the Cambridge Bible for Schools and Colleges (early twentieth century) reported Amraphel as "very generally accepted as the Hebrew reproduction of the name Hammurabi." Ronald Hendel, writing for TheTorah.com, reports the later turn: "Older scholarship identified Amraphel with Hammurabi, but they are composed of different name-elements." Wikipedia's survey (a tertiary source) adds that the identification, current since Eberhard Schrader in 1888, "has been largely abandoned in recent decades," that John Van Seters "rejected the historical existence of Amraphel," and that Stephanie Dalley proposed either Hammurabi "with an unexplained suffix -el" or a king of Qatna. Van Seters and Dalley were not read in their own work for this lesson.
- *Chedorlaomer.* The same survey reports that the name was once read as an Elamite "Kudur-Lagamar," but "no mention of a king named Kudur-Lagamar has yet been found." Hendel says only that the name is "Elamite sounding."
- *The chapter as a whole.* Hendel reports that "Because of its unique style, vocabulary and content, which is not identifiable with any of the major Pentateuchal sources, most scholars view this story as an independent source." That is a claim about how the chapter was composed; it does not settle whether the events are historical, on which scholars also differ.
- The honest summary: the names have Mesopotamian, Elamite and Hittite shapes, and proposed identifications have come and gone. This lesson does not adopt one.

**Key words, checked against the text.**

- **"Abram the Hebrew" (14:13).** A scan of this app's BSB text of Genesis finds this the first use of "Hebrew" in the book; the next uses come in the Joseph story (39:14, 39:17, 41:12), always in the mouths of Egyptians or about a foreigner. The text does not explain the label here.
- **"God Most High" (14:18, 14:19, 14:20, 14:22).** In this app's BSB text of Genesis, the title occurs only in this chapter. Melchizedek uses it three times; Abram uses it once, in his oath to the king of Sodom, with an addition: "I have raised my hand to the LORD God Most High, Creator of heaven and earth" (14:22). The Cambridge Bible's Genesis volume notes that "The LXX and Syriac Peshitto omit 'Jehovah'" here and that the Samaritan text reads "ha-Elohim," and it offers its own inference that Abram swears by "the God of Melchizedek whom a later scribe probably identified with Jehovah." The Greek part of that claim was spot-checked for this lesson: Swete's edition of the Septuagint at 14:22 has no word for "LORD" before "God Most High" (though Brenton's older English translation of the Septuagint reads "the Lord the most high God"); the Peshitta and the Samaritan text were not checked. The scribal inference is the Cambridge commentator's, reported as such. The Aramaic Targum Onkelos, in the Metsudah translation, keeps the divine name: "I have lifted my hand [in prayer] to [before] Adonoy [before] the Most High Almighty."
- **The name.** Hebrews 7:2 gives the name's sense as "king of righteousness." Josephus, a first-century Jewish historian, gives the same sense: "That name signifies, The righteous King" (*Antiquities* 1.10.2, Whiston translation). A king of Jerusalem in Joshua 10:1 bears a name built the same way: "Adoni-zedek king of Jerusalem." Genesis Rabbah 43:6 connects the two names.
- **The tithe (14:20).** "Then Abram gave Melchizedek a tenth of everything." That is the whole of what Genesis says. It is the first tithe in Scripture; the only other tithe in Genesis is Jacob's vow at Bethel ("of all that You give me I will surely give You a tenth," 28:22). Hebrews 7:4-10 builds an argument on this verse ("Even the patriarch Abraham gave him a tenth of the plunder," 7:4). Whether or how 14:20 bears on modern giving is a live question among Christian traditions that this lesson does not take up. A reader weighing a real financial decision about giving needs a pastor and, where money is tight, a qualified financial counselor -- not a study note.
- **Abram's refusal (14:22-24).** Abram has already sworn before the king of Sodom speaks ("I have raised my hand," 14:22), and he gives his own reason: "lest you should say, 'I have made Abram rich.'" (14:23). He does not impose his refusal on his allies: "They may take their portion" (14:24).

## Literary Design

**One king interrupts another.** The king of Sodom "went out to meet him" in 14:17, but does not speak until 14:21. Melchizedek's three verses (14:18-20) sit between Sodom's coming out and Sodom's speech. Joshua Garroway, writing for TheTorah.com, reports one way scholars read this: "Modern interpreters generally regard the first episode as a later insertion into Abram's encounter with the king of Sodom," noting that "Verse 21 picks up right from v. 17." Others read the same placement as a deliberate literary frame. Either way, the bracket itself is a checkable feature of the text; what produced it, the text does not say.

**Two kings, two exchanges.** Read side by side, the two encounters run in opposite directions. Melchizedek brings bread and wine and a blessing, and Abram gives him a tenth. The king of Sodom offers Abram the goods, and Abram refuses all of it. The bracket is checkable; that it is meant as a contrast is an inference a reader can test.

**A phrase handed from one speaker to another.** Melchizedek blesses Abram "by God Most High, Creator of heaven and earth" (14:19). Four verses later Abram swears to the king of Sodom using the same title, "God Most High, Creator of heaven and earth" (14:22), with "the LORD" placed before it in the Hebrew text this app's translation follows (see the textual note in Key Words). The repeated phrase is checkable in the text; what it implies about how Abram regarded Melchizedek's God is part of what the commentators below disagree about.

## Positions

Three separate questions. Each is reported through named positions in their own sources' words. None is adjudicated; no position below should be read as this lesson's conclusion.

**1. Who was Melchizedek?** Genesis gives a name, a city, a title ("priest of God Most High"), three acts (bread and wine, a blessing, receiving a tithe), and nothing else -- no parents, no prior history, no later appearance. Interpreters have filled that silence in at least four different ways.

- **A historical priest-king of Salem, a non-Israelite.** Josephus describes him as a real king: "Melchisedec, king of the city Salem," who "supplied Abram's army in an hospitable manner" (*Antiquities* 1.10.2), and elsewhere calls him "a potent man among the Canaanites" (*Wars* 6.10.1). Barnes' Notes on Hebrews 7:1 calls this "The most simple and probable opinion," "that he was a pious Canaanitish prince." The Cambridge Bible's Hebrews volume (on 7:3) describes him as "In himself only the Priest-prince of a little Canaanite community." Readers in this group generally read Hebrews 7:3's "Without father or mother or genealogy" as describing what Genesis leaves unrecorded, not a man without parents.
- **Shem, son of Noah.** The Aramaic Targum Pseudo-Jonathan renders 14:18: "And Malka Zadika, who was Shem bar Noah, the king of Yerushalem, came forth to meet Abram" (Etheridge translation, 1862). The Babylonian Talmud (Nedarim 32b) builds on the same identification: "The Holy One, Blessed be He, wanted the priesthood to emerge from Shem," but because Melchizedek blessed Abraham before blessing God, "Immediately the Holy One, Blessed be He, gave the priesthood to Abraham," reading Psalm 110:4 in support. (In the edition consulted, the phrase "traditionally identified as Shem" is the translators' explanatory gloss, not the Talmud's own words.) Rashi reports the identification as "A Midrashic explanation." Ellicott notes the identification appears "in the later Targums on the Pentateuch, but not in the Targum of Onkelos" -- which this lesson's own check of Onkelos at 14:18 confirms. The view was not only Jewish: the Cambridge Bible's Hebrews volume (on 7:3) reports, at second hand, that it "satisfied Lyra, Cajetan, Luther, Melanchthon, Lightfoot." The NPNF editors summarize Jerome's Letter 73 as holding that Melchizedek "was a real man, possibly, as the Jews said, Shem" (the letter's own text was not available to this lesson). Against it, the same volume argues that Shem would have been "called by his own name," that "Canaan was in the territory of Ham's descendants," and that "Shem was in no sense... 'without pedigree.'"
- **A pre-incarnate appearance of Christ (Christophany).** Epiphanius (fourth century) reports this view among church members and rejects it: "Some suppose that he is the actual Son of God, and appeared to Abraham then in the form of a man... But they too have gone off the track; no one will ever become 'like' himself" (*Panarion* 55.7, Williams translation). Meyer's commentary on Hebrews 7:3 names later holders, at second hand: "P. Molinaeus... P. Cunaeus... J. C. Hottinger... d'Outrein, Starck, and others." Among living writers, Dan Merritt (personal blog, 2022) states that "after years of study this writer has pitched his tent in the camp with those interpreters who support the view that Melchizedek was an appearance of the preincarnate Christ." GotQuestions.org calls it "a possible theory" and concludes "A case can be made either way." The main textual objection, made by Epiphanius above and by Barnes, rests on Hebrews 7:3's "like the Son of God": as Barnes puts it, if he were the Son of God, "with what propriety could the apostle say that he 'was made like the Son of God'" Proponents answer that Hebrews 7:3's description is hard to apply literally to any ordinary man. One citation caution: the Pulpit Commentary lists Ambrose among those who saw Melchizedek as Christ, but Ambrose's own *On the Christian Faith* (3.11.88) calls him "a holy man and priest of God, who, prefiguring our Lord, is described as without father or mother." This lesson did not check Ambrose's other works.
- **An exalted heavenly figure (Qumran, 11Q13).** This is not a modern reading of Genesis but an ancient one. A Dead Sea Scrolls fragment from Cave 11, usually dated around 100 BC, portrays Melchizedek as a heavenly agent at the end of days. Carmen Joy Imes, writing for the Society of Biblical Literature's Bible Odyssey, summarizes: "Authors of the Dead Sea Scrolls expanded this idea by depicting Melchizedek as an angelic member of the divine council," with a role that "would include judging, redeeming, making atonement, freeing captives, and reigning." Wikipedia (tertiary) adds that the text applies to him the title "Elohim," usually reserved for God, and reads it as "an apocalyptic commentary on the Jubilee year of Leviticus 25." The scroll itself was not read for this lesson. Epiphanius reports a related later group, the Melchizedekians, who "regard him as a sort of great power."

These four readings share one fact and disagree about what to do with it: Genesis records no origin and no end for Melchizedek. One group reads that silence as narrative economy, one fills it from the genealogies of Genesis 10-11, one reads it as a sign of a divine visitor, and one (Qumran) reads him as a heavenly figure. This lesson does not choose among them.

**2. What do the bread and wine signify?** Genesis says only that Melchizedek "brought out bread and wine" (14:18). It does not say to whom they were offered or why.

- **A type of the Eucharist.** Clement of Alexandria (*Stromata* 4.25) writes that Melchizedek "gave bread and wine, furnishing consecrated food for a type of the Eucharist." Cyprian (Epistle 62, section 4) writes: "Also in the priest Melchizedek we see prefigured the sacrament of the sacrifice of the Lord," and asks "who is more a priest of the most high God than our Lord Jesus Christ, who offered a sacrifice to God the Father, and offered that very same thing which Melchizedek had offered, that is, bread and wine, to wit, His body and blood?" The Catechism of the Catholic Church states that "The Church sees in the gesture of the king-priest Melchizedek, who 'brought out bread and wine,' a prefiguring of her own offering" (para. 1333), and that the presentation of the offerings "takes up the gesture of Melchizedek" (para. 1350). The Council of Trent (Session 22, ch. 1) says Christ, "declaring Himself constituted a priest for ever, according to the order of Melchisedech... offered up to God the Father His own body and blood under the species of bread and wine"; note that this chapter ties the order of Melchizedek to the Supper but does not itself cite Genesis 14:18's bread and wine. Haydock's Catholic commentary answers the opposing reading directly: "Many Protestants confess, that this renowned prince of Chanaan, was also a priest; but they will not allow that his sacrifice consisted of bread and wine."
- **Royal hospitality to weary soldiers.** Josephus already reads it this way: Melchizedek "supplied Abram's army in an hospitable manner, and gave them provisions in abundance." Rashi's first comment is the same: "Thus is done for those wearied through battle." Calvin: "He refreshed a wearied and famishing army with royal liberality; but because he was a priest, he blessed." The Cambridge Bible's Genesis volume (on 14:18): "the bread and wine are not offered to God; they are given to Abram as a token of good-will and as a means of refreshment. There is nothing sacrificial in the gift." The Pulpit Commentary argues the sacrificial reading "is precluded by the statement that he brought forth the bread and wine before the people, and not before God."
- **Refreshment first, and also a foreshadowing.** Ellicott takes a middle course: "Though the primary object of this offering was the refreshing of the bodies of Abram's men... yet we cannot but recognise in it a foreshowing" of Christ's gift of "His most blessed Body and Blood." A Jewish midrash reads a different priestly significance into the same items: in Genesis Rabbah 43:6, Rabbi Shmuel says "'Bread' – this alludes to the showbread; 'and wine' – this alludes to the libations."

**A fact both sides use.** Hebrews 7 retells the meeting, the blessing and the tithe, but never mentions the bread and wine. Ellicott: "Of the facts recorded in Genesis none are passed over, except the gift of bread and wine." Calvin presses the point against the Eucharistic reading: the author of Hebrews "says not a word concerning the bread and wine," and the Cambridge Bible's Hebrews volume (on 7:3) calls that silence "decisive against such a view." The Catholic and patristic sources above do not rest their reading on Hebrews; Cyprian ties it to Psalm 110:4 and to the Supper itself. Whether Hebrews' silence counts as evidence against a reading that never claimed Hebrews' support is the real point at issue, and this lesson leaves it with the reader.

**3. Was Salem Jerusalem?** Genesis names "Salem" once (14:18) and does not locate it.

- **Yes.** Within Scripture, the one other use of the name in the Old Testament puts Salem in poetic parallel with Zion: "His tent is in Salem, His dwelling place in Zion" (Psalm 76:2). Josephus: "they afterward called Salem Jerusalem." Targum Pseudo-Jonathan renders "the king of Yerushalem," and the Metsudah translation of Onkelos renders "king of Shaleim [Yerushalayim]." Ellicott (on Hebrews 7:1) accepts the tradition: "certainly Salem is a poetic name of Jerusalem (Psalm 76:2)." The Cambridge Bible's Genesis volume calls Salem "In all probability to be identified with Jerusalem," adding that "In the Tel-el-Amarna tablets Jerusalem appears with the name Uru-salim."
- **Probably not.** The Cambridge Bible's Hebrews volume (on Hebrews 7:1) argues instead that "Salem is probably a town near Shechem," following Jerome, "who in his Onomasticon places it eight miles south of Bethshean," and a Samaritan tradition placing the meeting at Gerizim. It calls Psalm 76:2 "very dubious" as evidence and argues that "Jerusalem in the days of Abraham... was only known by the name Jebus" -- an argument the same series' Genesis volume calls "not conclusive." Jerome's own Latin (Letter 73.7) says Salem is not Jerusalem, "as Josephus and all our people think," but "a town near Scythopolis" ("Salem autem non, ut Josephus, et nostri omnes arbitrantur esse (f. est) Jerusalem... sed oppidum juxta Scythopolim"; the English is this lesson's own rendering). The Hebrews volume also cites Genesis 33:18, where some translations read a place "Shalem" -- though it concedes the words "may mean 'in safety,'" and this app's BSB renders that verse "he arrived safely at the city of Shechem," with no place called Shalem.

Psalm 76:2's parallel is a real, checkable link between Salem and Zion; it is not a statement that the Salem of Genesis 14 is Jerusalem, and named commentators read its weight differently. Hebrews 7:2, notably, uses the name for its meaning ("king of Salem" means "king of peace") rather than its location.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key; the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step. It works from 14:21-24 rather than from Melchizedek, because the Melchizedek verses carry the disputes above, and a worked "lesson" from them would quietly pick a side.

**Passage element.** Genesis 14:21-24. The king of Sodom offers: "Give me the people, but take the goods for yourself" (14:21). Abram refuses "even a thread, or a strap of a sandal," and gives his reason: "lest you should say, 'I have made Abram rich.'" (14:23). He keeps only "what my men have eaten" and leaves his allies free to take their portion (14:24).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text gives Abram's reason in his own words, and the reason concerns what the king of Sodom would be able to *say* afterward, not whether taking the goods was permitted. Inference: for hearers who knew Sodom from 13:13 ("wicked, sinning greatly against the LORD"), Abram's refusal would mark that his wealth was not to be traceable to that king's gift. The text supplies the reason; it does not supply a rule.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** A gift can create a claim -- a story the giver can later tell about you -- and declining a gift you are entitled to can be a way of keeping that claim from existing, without condemning others who accept their own share.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a legitimate benefit is offered by a party whose goodwill would later be useful to them; the person offered it has a clear reason for declining; others in the same position are not bound by that choice. Not carried forward: the war, the spoils, Sodom's specific reputation, and any claim that every gift is a trap.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed; nothing here promises that refusing a gift will be rewarded.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. A construction project manager is reviewing three bids for a subcontract. One bidder, whose bid is under review, offers him playoff tickets -- within company gift policy, and the other managers on the team have accepted similar tickets before.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: integrity (declining a permitted benefit to keep a decision clear of a claim). One faithful response is to decline the tickets plainly, give the reason in one sentence ("the bid's still open"), finish the review on the bids' merits, and not lecture colleagues whose own situation differs -- as Abram let Aner, Eshcol and Mamre take their portion.

**Cautions.** This is an analogy the learner is testing, not a rule the passage gives; company policy and law, not this lesson, govern what gifts are allowed. If a gift or a refusal is tied to pressure, threats, or retaliation at work, that is a matter for HR, a compliance officer, or legal counsel, not a study note.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Genesis records no origin and no end for Melchizedek. Positions (1) reports four ways of filling that silence. What would you need to see in the text of Genesis itself -- not in Hebrews, the Targums or Qumran -- to decide whether the silence is meant to signify anything at all?
2. Hebrews 7 retells the meeting, the blessing and the tithe but not the bread and wine. Is the silence of a later text evidence about what an earlier text meant, or only evidence about the later author's purpose? Try your answer on both sides of Positions (2).
3. Psalm 110:4 calls a king "a priest forever in the order of Melchizedek" -- a priesthood outside Aaron's line. Read the whole psalm (110:1-7). What does the psalm itself say about who this priest-king is, before any later interpreter is consulted?
4. Abram gives a tenth to Melchizedek and refuses everything from the king of Sodom (14:20-23). What, in the text, distinguishes the two kings in Abram's treatment of them?

(c) Genesis 14:20 records one tithe given once; it does not state a rule for anyone else.

   Or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` for a candidate this lesson did not author. Two real candidates, with the reason each was declined: (1) Genesis 14:18's bread and wine to the Last Supper (Matthew 26:26-28, where "Jesus took bread" and "Then He took the cup"). No New Testament text links the two scenes, and Hebrews, which retells Genesis 14 in detail, omits the bread and wine; named traditions do link them (Cyprian, the Catechism), so the link is reported in Positions (2) rather than authored as a reviewed connection, where its grade would quietly decide that dispute. (2) Abram's refusal of Sodom's wealth (14:23) and the LORD's word in the next chapter, "I am your shield, your very great reward" (15:1). The verbal and narrative proximity is real, but no source consulted for this lesson draws the link, so it stays a candidate until one is found.
