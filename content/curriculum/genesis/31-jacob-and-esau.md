Genesis 25:19-34 is short, and it reaches a long way. A barren wife conceives twins who struggle in the womb, and the LORD answers her with an oracle that ends, "the older will serve the younger" (25:23). The firstborn, Esau, later trades his birthright to his younger brother Jacob for a bowl of stew. The narrator closes with one verdict: "Thus Esau despised his birthright" (25:34). Centuries later, Malachi has the LORD say, "Jacob I have loved, but Esau I have hated" (Malachi 1:2-3). Paul quotes both lines together in Romans 9:12-13, and Hebrews 12:16 warns against being "godless like Esau." Romans 9 has long been disputed in Christian debate over election, and the lesson reports that debate in each tradition's own words without settling it. It also checks each link in the chain in the BSB, the Hebrew and the Greek, reports how readers judge Jacob's bargain and what "hated" can mean, and names two ways the twins became figures for later peoples, one of them used for centuries against Jews.

## Context

**What happens, in order (25:19-34).** A heading: "This is the account of Abraham's son Isaac" (25:19). Isaac marries Rebekah at forty (25:20). She is barren, Isaac prays, and she conceives (25:21). "But the children inside her struggled with each other," and she asks, "Why is this happening to me?" (25:22). She goes "to inquire of the LORD" and receives the oracle (25:22-23). The twins are born: Esau first, "red, covered with hair like a fur coat," then Jacob, "grasping Esau's heel" (25:24-26). They grow up different, and each parent has a favorite (25:27-28). Then comes the stew and the sale (25:29-34).

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens puts Genesis 12 through Malachi in stage 5 (machine-readable `chapters: ["1.12"]`). The unit follows Abraham's death (25:7-11) and Ishmael's line (25:12-18). Its sequel, Genesis 27, where Jacob takes Esau's blessing, belongs to a later lesson.

**Key words, read as observations.** Each count below was made by this lesson's own script over the app's BSB text.

- **"Barren" (25:21).** In Genesis, the BSB uses "barren" in three verses: Sarai (11:30), Rebekah (25:21) and Rachel (29:31).
- **"Inquire of the LORD" (25:22).** This is the first verse in the BSB with the phrase. It occurs in ten verses in all. Rashi, following Genesis Rabbah 63:6, says she went to enquire "at the school of Shem."
- **The oracle (25:23).** The Cambridge Bible calls it "a rhythmic oracle, in four stichoi, or lines." In the BSB, "two nations" occurs in three verses (here and Ezekiel 35:10 and 37:22), and "the older will serve the younger" in exactly two, Genesis 25:23 and Romans 9:12. The Hebrew has *rab* and *tsa'ir* (Bible Hub's interlinear); the Septuagint, "the greater" and "the lesser" (ὁ μείζων, τῷ ἐλάσσονι).
- **Names from the birth (25:25-26, 25:30).** Esau comes out "red" (Hebrew *admoni*), and later asks for "that red stew" (*ha-adom*, said twice in the Hebrew) at 25:30. The narrator adds: "(That is why he was also called Edom.)" In the BSB, 25:30 is the first verse to name Edom. Jacob is named from the heel he grasps (25:26), and Esau later plays on the name: "Is he not rightly named Jacob? For he has cheated me twice" (27:36). Malka Simkovich (TheTorah.com) reads the descriptions "red (adom)" and "covered in hair (se'ar)" as foreshadowing Esau's future "as the founder of the Edomites who live near Mount Seir."
- **"A quiet man" (25:27).** Jacob is *tam*. Bible Hub's interlinear glosses it "blameless," the BSB's word at Job 1:1 ("this man was blameless and upright"), and Wesley reads it "An honest man, that dealt fairly." Whether the word is an evaluation is itself a reader's question.
- **"Birthright" (25:31-34). In the BSB the English word first appears at 25:31 and occurs in eight verses: four here, Genesis 27:36, 1 Chronicles 5:1-2 and Hebrews 12:16. The Hebrew noun, *bekorah*, is older in the text. Bible Hub's concordance lists fourteen occurrences, the first at Genesis 4:4, where the BSB has "the firstborn of his flock." So "first at 25:31" is true of the English word only. What the birthright included is not defined in the chapter. The later law gives a firstborn son a "double portion" (Deuteronomy 21:17). The Cambridge Bible says, "What these were is not defined," and adds, "But it is clear from Genesis 43:33, Genesis 48:13-19, that the firstborn was regarded as entitled to a more honourable position and to a larger share of the inheritance than his brethren." Rashi, following Genesis Rabbah 63:13, holds that "the sacrificial service was then carried out by the first-born sons."
- **"Despised" (25:34).** The Septuagint's verb is ἐφαύλισεν, "made light of." It is the only explicit verdict the narrator states on either brother's conduct in the passage.

**The oracle beside Romans 9:10-13.** Set side by side in this app's BSB:

- Genesis 25:23: "Two nations are in your womb, and two peoples from within you will be separated; one people will be stronger than the other, and the older will serve the younger."
- Romans 9:10-12: "Not only that, but Rebecca's children were conceived by one man, our father Isaac. Yet before the twins were born or had done anything good or bad, in order that God's plan of election might stand, not by works but by Him who calls, she was told, 'The older will serve the younger.'"

Paul quotes only the last line. His Greek, ὁ μείζων δουλεύσει τῷ ἐλάσσονι, is word for word the Septuagint of 25:23 (Swete). Every Greek edition Bible Hub lists for Romans 9:12 has the same five words. The only difference among them is the spelling of the introducing verb ("she was told"): ἐρρέθη in Nestle 1904, Westcott-Hort, Tischendorf and the Greek Orthodox text; ἐρρήθη in the Byzantine and both Textus Receptus editions. Paul's "before the twins were born" matches Genesis, where the oracle comes while "the children inside her struggled" (25:22).

**Malachi 1:2-3 and Romans 9:13.** Paul then adds, "So it is written: 'Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated'" (Romans 9:13, BSB). The source is Malachi 1:2-3: "'Was not Esau Jacob's brother?' declares the LORD. 'Yet Jacob I have loved, but Esau I have hated, and I have made his mountains a wasteland and left his inheritance to the desert jackals.'" Three checks follow.

- **The Greek.** The second clause, τὸν δὲ Ἠσαῦ ἐμίσησα ("but Esau I hated"), is the Septuagint's exactly. The first clause has the same words in a new order. The Septuagint reads καὶ ἠγάπησα τὸν Ἰακώβ ("and I loved Jacob"), and Paul reads τὸν Ἰακὼβ ἠγάπησα, with Jacob placed first. The Cambridge Bible calls it "Nearly verbatim from LXX."
- **The formula.** Nestle 1904 and Westcott-Hort read καθάπερ γέγραπται; the Byzantine, Tischendorf, Greek Orthodox and Textus Receptus editions read καθὼς γέγραπται. Both mean "as it is written."
- **The English.** The BSB's "I have loved" (Malachi) against "I loved" (Romans) is a difference in the English only. Bible Hub shows the same Greek aorist, ἠγάπησα, in both.

Malachi's setting matters to every reading below. The prophet answers a people who ask, "How have You loved us?" (1:2). The verses go on to speak of land and nation: "Though Edom may say, 'We have been devastated, but we will rebuild the ruins,' this is what the LORD of Hosts says: 'They may build, but I will demolish'" (1:4). The Hebrew verb in 1:3, "I have hated," is from the same root (Strong's 8130) as the participle the BSB renders "unloved" of Leah at Genesis 29:31 (Bible Hub's text analysis).

**Hebrews 12:16-17.** "See to it that no one is sexually immoral, or is godless like Esau, who for a single meal sold his birthright" (12:16, BSB). The verse names this scene, and its Greek has the Septuagint's word for "birthright" (πρωτοτόκια; Swete spells it πρωτοτοκεῖα). Verse 17, on the blessing Esau "sought ... with tears," points ahead to Genesis 27. Commentators differ on whether "sexually immoral" attaches to Esau. Ellicott thinks it "not probable." The Cambridge Bible holds that "The word must be taken in a literal sense," notes that Genesis never charges Esau with it, and says the writer "is probably alluding to the Jewish Hagadah."

**Esau and Edom later.** Esau later "ran to him and embraced him" (33:4), and "Esau (that is, Edom) settled in the area of Mount Seir" (36:8). The Torah tells Israel, "Do not despise an Edomite, for he is your brother" (Deuteronomy 23:7). Edom's later subjection is recorded in 2 Samuel 8:14 ("all the Edomites were subject to David"). Benson says the oracle's last line "was fulfilled in the subjection of the Edomites, for many ages, to the house of David," and the Cambridge Bible, on the same words, points to 2 Samuel 8:14. Later, "Edom rebelled against the hand of Judah" (2 Kings 8:20).

## Literary Design

**An oracle in poetry inside a prose story.** Ellicott sets 25:23 as four lines, and the Cambridge Bible lists its four claims, ending "the younger shall be the lord of the elder." The story around it explains names as it goes: red becomes Edom, the heel becomes Jacob.

**A bare sale scene.** In 25:29-34 no one prays and God does not speak. Esau speaks twice, Jacob twice, and the narrator gives Esau four quick verbs, he "ate and drank and then got up and went away" (25:34), then a fifth, the verdict that he "despised his birthright." Calvin asks, "For what reason are these four things stated?" Genesis Rabbah 63:14 reads each verb in turn. The narrator says nothing about Jacob's part. The Cambridge Bible notes that "Jacob's action on this occasion is recorded without disapproval."

## Positions

Three questions follow. Each is reported through named readers in their own words, and none is adjudicated. The first is the most disputed of the three.

**1. What does the oracle say about God's choice, and what does Paul use it to show?** Every reading has to account for the oracle's "two nations" (25:23), Paul's "before the twins were born or had done anything good or bad" (Romans 9:11), and Malachi's move from "Esau" to "Edom" (Malachi 1:3-4). Four readings are reported here. The first three are Christian readings of Romans 9; the fourth is a Jewish reading of Genesis and Malachi. This curriculum's Matthew 11:20-30 lesson reported the same church traditions on a related question.

- **Individual election to salvation (Reformed: Calvin, the Canons of Dort, Schreiner).** John Calvin reads Romans 9:11 as Paul's statement of "the election of God," which is "gratuitous, and in no way depending on men; so that in the salvation of the godly nothing higher ... must be sought than the goodness of God, and nothing higher in the perdition of the reprobate than his just severity." He states the first proposition he finds in the verse: God "predestinates some to salvation, and others to eternal condemnation." On Genesis 25:23 Calvin grants that the oracle itself concerns peoples: "Moses here treats of the former kind of election," the common adoption of a whole people, while "Paul turns his words to the latter," the election of particular people to salvation. He holds that Paul "properly extends further" what "Moses commends in the person of Jacob alone." The Canons of Dort (1618-1619) quote Romans 9:11-13 in the article that says "the cause of this undeserved election is exclusively the good pleasure of God," which involves "adopting certain particular persons from among the common mass of sinners as God's own possession" (First Main Point, Article 10). Thomas Schreiner, professor of New Testament at The Southern Baptist Theological Seminary when he wrote, argues the same reading of Romans 9. He writes that "Isaac and Jacob were individually chosen by God." He grants that "election has corporate dimensions, but Paul's point in Romans 9 goes deeper. Mere physical descent from Abraham or Isaac does not prove that an individual is elect" (*JETS*, 2006).
- **The election of a people (Wesley, Clarke, Abasciano).** John Wesley's *Notes* (1755) read the oracle as fulfilled "Not in person, for he never did; but in his posterity." On Romans 9:13 he writes: "I have, comparatively, hated Esau - That is, the Edomites," and adds, "This does not relate to the person of Jacob or Esau Nor does it relate to the eternal state either of them or their posterity." Adam Clarke, a Methodist commentator, holds "That these words are used in a national and not in a personal sense." He argues that in the personal sense "they are not true, for Jacob never did exercise any power over Esau." Instead Jacob "acknowledged his brother to be his lord, and himself to be his servant" (citing Genesis 32:4 and 33:8, 33:13, where the BSB has "my master Esau" and "my lord"). Brian Abasciano, a pastor whose Aberdeen PhD thesis (2004) was on Paul's use of the Old Testament in Romans 9:1-9, answering Schreiner in the same journal issue (*JETS*, 2006), agrees that Romans 9 "surely concerns the salvation of Israel." But he argues that the election there is primarily corporate. Paul's quotations about Jacob "speak of his election primarily as the election of a people," and he holds that in the Old Testament concept of election "individuals are elect only as members of the elect people." He quotes C. E. B. Cranfield (secondhand through Abasciano): "There is no doubt that the concern of Mal I.2–5 is with the nations of Israel and Edom, and it is natural to suppose that by 'Jacob' and 'Esau' Paul also understands not only the twin sons of Isaac but also the peoples descended from them." He adds that corporate election "of necessity must include individuals in its purview to some extent."
- **Catholic teaching.** The Catechism of the Catholic Church, in the two paragraphs on predestination read for this lesson, does not cite Romans 9. It says that when God "establishes his eternal plan of 'predestination', he includes in it each person's free response to his grace" (§600), and that "God predestines no one to go to hell; for this, a willful turning away from God (a mortal sin) is necessary, and persistence in it until the end" (§1037). Older Catholic commentary on Romans 9 can be read in Haydock's *Catholic Bible Commentary* (1859), which quotes two eighteenth-century writers. Robert Witham reads the choice of Jacob as "not according to any works they had done, or that he foresaw they would do, but merely according to his mercy." He adds that "the preference which God gave to Jacob was literally true, as to temporal benefits," and reports that Augustine "observes in divers places, that Jacob was a figure of the elect or predestinate, and Esau of the reprobate." Bishop Richard Challoner compares God's mercy to a king who, "of two equally criminal," pardons one "out of pure mercy" while "he suffers justice to take place" for the other. The Catechism (1992) is the Church's current official statement; Haydock's sources are commentary from before it.
- **Jewish readings of the oracle and of Esau.** Genesis Rabbah 63 reads the oracle as being about peoples and history: "From you, Jews and Romans would arise" (63:7). On "the elder will serve the younger" it records a conditional reading from Rabbi Huna: "If he [Jacob] merits, he [Esau] will serve, but if not, he [Esau] will be served." Rashi reads "one people will be stronger" as alternation: "They will never be equally great at the same time: when one rises the other will fall." The midrash also traces a difference in the brothers' deeds back to the womb. When Rebekah passed "synagogues and study halls, Jacob would convulse to emerge," and at "houses of idol worship, Esau would run and convulse to emerge" (63:6). On Malachi's "I hated Esau," Rashi reads a matter of land: God chose "to push him off to a land because of Jacob, his brother." These are Jewish readings of Genesis and Malachi, addressed to Jewish readers, not commentary on Romans. Simkovich argues that some of them answer Christian readings indirectly (see the misuse named below).

**Objections each reading faces.** Each reading has been answered by a named reader.

- *Against the Reformed reading,* Clarke argues that God would not announce a personal reprobation "to a tender, weak, and delicate woman" before her children were born, and that there is "no reason, worthy the most wise and gracious God," for revealing such a thing. Abasciano argues that "The OT concept of election is clearly corporate." The Pulpit Commentary, on Malachi 1:3, says that "Malachi is not speaking of the predestination of the one brother and the reprobation of the other; he is contrasting the histories of the two peoples."
- *Against the corporate and national reading,* Calvin writes, "They who restrict it to earthly riches and wealth coldly trifle." The nineteenth-century editor of the Calvin Translation Society edition, in his own footnote, grants for argument's sake that "the election here is to temporal and outward privileges," but says it "is adduced by the Apostle as an illustration," and "most clearly of spiritual and eternal election." Schreiner holds that the corporate reading "fails to discern Paul's main point." Against election grounded in foresight, which the Arminian tradition holds, the Canons of Dort reject election "on the basis of foreseen faith" (Article 9), and Calvin writes, "False then is the dogma ... that God elects or rejects, as he foresees each to be worthy or unworthy of his favor."
- *Against a Catholic reader,* Meyer, on Romans 9:13, holds that the hatred of Esau is "completely independent of foreseen sins (in opposition to the Greek Fathers and Jerome on Malachi 1)." His named target is Jerome's reading of Malachi, not the Catechism.
- *Against the midrashic portrait of Esau,* Barry Dov Walfish (TheTorah.com) writes that Esau "in the Bible ... is not described as a bad person. In fact, he is generally painted as the wronged party in the rivalry between the two." Walfish's own summary of 25:27-34 is that Esau is "a bit of a brute, unrefined, uncouth ... But he is no villain." He traces the epithet "Esau the Wicked" to Israel's later history with Edom and to the equation of Edom with Rome.

These readings do not agree. They differ on whether the oracle concerns persons or peoples, salvation or historical role, and what part human response plays. This lesson does not decide between them. *A pastoral note:* if this question has stopped being a study question and become a fear about yourself or someone you love, please talk with a pastor who knows you. A study note cannot carry that weight. For some people the fear returns no matter how often it is answered. The International OCD Foundation describes scrupulosity as "A form of obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) involving religious or moral obsessions," and lists "Going to hell" among its common fears. Its packet for faith leaders notes that "providing reassurance serves to maintain the anxiety disorder cycle." If that sounds familiar, a licensed counselor can help alongside your pastor; the Foundation lists therapists and support groups at iocdf.org/find-help. If the fear ever comes with thoughts of harming yourself, in the United States you can call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. Elsewhere, call your local emergency number.

**2. What does "hated" mean in Malachi 1:3 and Romans 9:13?** This curriculum's Matthew 10:16-42 lesson met the same question with Luke 14:26 ("does not hate his father and mother") and reported three readings there. Here the readings divide in a similar way.

- *A comparative idiom ("loved less").* Barnes says the word means "not positive hatred, but merely a less love," and cites Genesis 29:30-31 and Luke 14:26. The Cambridge Bible, on Romans 9:13, points to Genesis 29: "One verse there tells us that Jacob 'hated' Leah, the other that he 'loved Rachel more.'" (The BSB has "unloved" at 29:31, and the Hebrew root matches Malachi's, as noted in Context.) Wesley writes "comparatively, hated." The Calvin Translation Society editor's footnote says the same, citing "Genesis 29:31; Luke 14:26; John 12:25"; that footnote is the editor's, not Calvin's.
- *More than comparison.* Meyer rejects "to love less" by name, along with a list of scholars who held it. He holds that the verb "expresses the opposite of the positive ἠγάπ., viz. positive hatred," though he roots it "solely on the free elective determination of God," and he places that choice in God's plan for "the theocracy and its privileges." Bengel says the verb "properly signifies to hate, nay, to hate greatly." He also says the reference "is not to the spiritual state of each of the two brothers: but the external condition of Jacob and Esau."
- *Hatred as allotment.* Rashi, as quoted above, reads it as Esau's being pushed "off to a land," and compares a father who "selects a beautiful portion for the firstborn." The Pulpit Commentary reports Jerome's reading of Malachi: "In Jacob vos dilexi, in Esau Idumaeos odio habui" (in this lesson's rough translation, "in Jacob I loved you; in Esau I hated the Idumeans").

**3. How should Jacob's part in the sale be judged?** Hebrews blames Esau, and the narrator's verdict falls on Esau too (25:34). Neither says anything about Jacob. Named readers fill that silence differently.

- *Jacob was wrong in the means.* Matthew Henry: "He was right, that he coveted earnestly the best gifts; he was wrong, that he took advantage of his brother's need." Poole asks, "Did Jacob well in this matter?" and answers, "No; because he tempted his brother to an act of profaneness and folly." Jacob "should have waited till God had executed his promise in his own way."
- *Jacob's act was hard, but it was not the point.* Calvin starts severely: "Jacob ought of his own accord to have satisfied the hunger of his brother... who would not condemn him for his inhumanity?" He then argues that God "brought to light what lay hid in both," and on 25:33 that "Jacob did not act cruelly towards his brother, for he took nothing from him, but only desired a confirmation of that right which had been divinely granted to him." The Cambridge Bible says that "The carelessness of Esau rather than the meanness of Jacob seems to meet with the contempt of the narrator."
- *Jacob had a reason.* Genesis Rabbah 63:13 asks "what did Jacob our patriarch see" that led him to seek the birthright. It answers that the firstborn performed sacrifice before the Tabernacle, and Jacob said, "Will this evil one stand and sacrifice?" Rashi follows: "This wicked man is unworthy to sacrifice to the Holy One, blessed be He." Walfish, as quoted above, reads the plain text the other way, with Esau as "the wronged party."

**Two figural readings, one of them a misuse.** Two uses of the twins as figures for later peoples are reported here. This lesson adopts neither.

*Church and Synagogue.* Tertullian, in *An Answer to the Jews* (chapter 1), reads the oracle as being about two peoples, "of the Jews, that is, of Israel; and of the Gentiles, that is ours." He concludes that "the prior and greater people — that is, the Jewish — must necessarily serve the less," and supports this with a list of Israel's idolatries. Augustine (*City of God* 16.35) reports that Christian writers almost unanimously read the elder as the Jews and the younger as the Christians, and he prefers that reading to the Idumean one. Simkovich calls Tertullian "The first Church Father to state explicitly that Jacob symbolizes the Church and Esau the Jews." She traces how the reading grew into a pattern of writing that assumed "the annulment of the covenant with Israel." The habit outlived the Fathers. In the same note on Romans 9:12-13, Adam Clarke, quoted above for the national reading, applies Malachi's words about Edom to the Jews who rejected the gospel, as a people under God's lasting indignation. Several things can be checked against the texts. The oracle names "two nations," and Genesis itself identifies them: "Esau was the father of the Edomites" (36:43). The Torah commands, "Do not despise an Edomite, for he is your brother" (Deuteronomy 23:7). Romans 9 opens with Paul's grief for "my brothers, my own flesh and blood, the people of Israel," and says, "Theirs is the adoption as sons; theirs the divine glory and the covenants" (9:3-4, BSB). The Catholic Church's Second Vatican Council rejected this kind of reading in general terms, without citing Genesis 25 or Romans 9: "Although the Church is the new people of God, the Jews should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God, as if this followed from the Holy Scriptures" (*Nostra Aetate* 4, 1965, addressed to Catholic teaching and preaching).

*Edom as Rome.* Rabbinic tradition read Esau as Rome. Genesis Rabbah 63:7 says "From you, Jews and Romans would arise" and names "Hadrian among the idolaters." Rashi on 25:23 finds in the oracle "an allusion to Antoninus and Rabbi," a Roman emperor and Rabbi Judah the Prince. Walfish and Simkovich trace the equation to the Idumean Herodian dynasty, which ruled for Rome, and to Rome's wars against Judea, and both report that after Rome became Christian, Edom became a cipher for Christianity too. Simkovich calls this "a particularly sharp and effective polemical counter-reading to that of Paul and the Church Fathers." She adds that the figure makes Christianity "a sibling to Judaism," and that "when we last see Esau in the Hebrew Bible, he has reconciled with his brother Jacob." This reading arose among Jews living under Roman and later Christian rule. It is reported as part of the passage's history; it is not named here as a misuse.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, as a model of the shape, not an answer key.

**Passage element.** Genesis 25:32-34: "'Look,' said Esau, 'I am about to die, so what good is a birthright to me?' ... So Esau swore to Jacob and sold him the birthright. ... Thus Esau despised his birthright" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: Esau is "famished" (25:29-30) and speaks as if the moment were final ("I am about to die"). Jacob asks for the trade at that moment, an oath seals it, and the narrator judges it as despising something lasting. Inference (this lesson's own): for a first audience, the scene shows a lasting good traded under pressure for immediate relief. Whether the first audience also saw a wrong in Jacob's pressing is disputed (Position 3).

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** A decision made in a moment of acute need can give away something lasting. (A further ethical step, the learner's own and beyond what the passage states: pressing someone to decide in such a moment can itself be a wrong.)

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the pressure of the moment, the lasting thing at stake, and the one who gives way. The role of the one who presses is carried only through the learner's own ethical step above. Not carried forward: the oracle, God's choice of Jacob, the birthright's religious meaning, and any verdict on who is "Esau" or "Jacob" in a modern family.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a narrative pattern applied by analogy. It is not a command. Promise scope: none. The passage promises no outcome to anyone who waits instead of trading.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family and property. After a parent's death, an adult son, exhausted and short of money, is pressed by a sibling to sign away his share of the family house that week in exchange for quick cash.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: wisdom and restraint, for both siblings. One faithful response is for him to decline to sign that week, ask for time, and have the papers reviewed by a licensed attorney (legal aid if he cannot afford one) before deciding. For the sibling, a faithful response is to stop pressing, make the offer in writing, and leave room for a decision made after rest.

**Cautions.** The bridge does not say that every sale under pressure is wrong, that the person who needs cash is "despising" anything, or that either sibling is a villain. Real need is not a moral failure. Legal questions about an estate need a qualified professional, not a study note. If grief or money pressure has become overwhelming, a pastor, doctor or counselor is the right next step.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. Genesis Rabbah has Jacob and Esau already turning toward study halls or idol temples in the womb. Paul stresses that the word came "before the twins were born or had done anything good or bad." What does each reading need the unborn twins to be, and which one stays closer to Genesis 25:22-23?
2. Clarke cites Jacob calling Esau "my lord" (33:8) against a personal reading of the oracle; Calvin cites the same flight and fear for the opposite purpose. Does the later story weaken or sharpen the oracle?
3. The narrator judges Esau ("despised") and says nothing about Jacob. Is the silence approval, neutrality, or a gap the reader is meant to notice? What in the chapter, or in 27:36, would help decide?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 25:19-34 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) Recite the oracle's last line in the BSB's words and name the one other verse where the BSB has it. (c) Name the narrator's single stated verdict in the passage.
2. **Five-minute outline.** (0:00-1:00) the birth and the names; (1:00-2:00) Genesis 25:23 and Malachi 1:2-3 in Romans 9:12-13, with the Greek; (2:00-3:30) the four readings on election, their objections, and the pastoral note as written; (3:30-4:15) "hated" and Jacob's bargain; (4:15-5:00) the two figural uses.
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: reading the oracle as about nations empties Romans 9 of its point, since Paul is answering why individual Israelites do not believe. Weigh it against Calvin's own statement that "Moses here treats of the former kind of election," and against Abasciano's claim that the corporate reading still concerns salvation. A second candidate, running the other way: reading Romans 9 as about individual salvation ignores that Malachi speaks of Edom's land. Weigh it against Meyer, who reads "the persons of the two brothers" but an election "to be the vehicle of the theocracy and its privileges."
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Genesis 25 does not define the birthright; every list in Context comes from later law or later readers. (b) Genesis 25 does not evaluate Jacob's conduct; only Esau's is judged (25:34). (c) Genesis 25:23 does not mention salvation, the Church or the Jewish people of any later age. Those applications come from Romans, from Christian and Jewish interpreters, or from both.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** The connections attached to this lesson:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Romans 9:10-12 | quotation | explicit | "she was told" introduces the oracle's last line, word for word the Septuagint of 25:23 in every edition listed |
| Romans 9:13 (from Malachi 1:2-3) | quotation | explicit | "So it is written" introduces Malachi; the second clause matches the Septuagint exactly, and the first changes only the word order |
| Malachi 1:2-4 | explicit_reference | strong | Names Esau as "Jacob's brother" and speaks of Edom's land, but does not quote or retell the oracle |

Choose one row and defend it, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` for a candidate. Three candidates were considered and not authored:

- **2 Samuel 8:14** ("all the Edomites were subject to David"). Benson and the Cambridge Bible tie it to the oracle, but 2 Samuel does not refer back to Genesis 25, and grading it as fulfillment would settle a question Positions leaves open.
- **Genesis 48:14-19.** Jacob crosses his hands to bless the younger Ephraim, saying "his younger brother shall be greater than he." A similar pattern, but with no shared wording and no source read here linking it to 25:23.
- **Hebrews 11:20** ("By faith Isaac blessed Jacob and Esau concerning the future"). This concerns Genesis 27, not this passage.
