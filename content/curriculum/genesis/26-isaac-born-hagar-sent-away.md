Genesis 21 opens with a promise kept, "the LORD did for Sarah what He had promised" (21:1), and ends with a treaty over a well and a new name for God. Between those, a household breaks apart. At Isaac's weaning feast Sarah sees Hagar's son doing something the Hebrew describes with one word, and she demands, "Expel the slave woman and her son" (21:10). God tells Abraham to listen to her and also promises a nation from the boy (21:12-13). In the wilderness, when the water runs out, Hagar sits down where she will not have to watch her son die; God hears, opens her eyes to a well, and is "with the boy" as he grows (21:15-20). Paul later quotes Sarah's demand and reads the boy's action as persecution (Galatians 4:29-30), and later Christians turned that reading against living peoples. This lesson keeps every person's cost in view, above all that of an enslaved woman and her son put out with bread and a skin of water (21:14), and it reports what named readers have concluded without choosing among them. Every BSB quotation here and in the four connection files was printed from this app's shipped corpus by a script and checked by a second script (see Sources and Limits).

## Context

**What happens, in order (21:1-34).** Sarah bears a son "at the very time God had promised" (21:2). Abraham, "a hundred years old" (21:5), names him Isaac and circumcises him at eight days (21:3-4). Sarah says, "God has made me laugh" (21:6). At the weaning feast (21:8) Sarah sees that Hagar's son "was mocking her son" (21:9) and demands his expulsion (21:10). The matter "distressed Abraham greatly" (21:11). God tells him, "Listen to everything that Sarah tells you, for through Isaac your offspring will be reckoned" (21:12), and "I will also make a nation of the slave woman's son, because he is your offspring" (21:13). Abraham sends Hagar away with "bread and a skin of water" (21:14). When it is gone she says, "I cannot bear to watch the boy die!" and weeps (21:16). "Then God heard the voice of the boy" (21:17); the angel promises, "I will make him into a great nation" (21:18); God shows her a well (21:19) and "was with the boy" (21:20), who settles in Paran and marries a wife from Egypt (21:21). Then Abimelech and Phicol make a covenant with Abraham, a disputed well is secured with seven ewe lambs, the place is called Beersheba, and Abraham calls on "the Eternal God" (21:22-33).

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens files Genesis 12 through Malachi under stage 5 (`chapters: ["1.12"]`). Isaac's birth fulfills 17:19 and 18:10. The laughter behind his name was treated in earlier units: the Genesis 17 lesson on Abraham's laugh (17:17), and the Genesis 18 lesson on Sarah's laugh (18:12) and the named dispute over which laugh the name recalls. This lesson does not repeat that material; it adds only that the same root returns in 21:9 (Key Words). Hagar's earlier story, the misuse of 16:12, and whether Paul's reading is allegory, type or illustration are in the Genesis 16 lesson; this lesson takes up Galatians 4's quotation of chapter 21.

**Key words, read as observations.**

- **"Mocking" (21:9) and the root of Isaac's name.** In this app's BSB, 21:9 is the only verse in Genesis with "mocking." The Hebrew is the participle *metsacheq*. Ellicott says the verb "is the same as that rendered to laugh in Genesis 21:6, but in an intensive conjugation," and Calvin that the epithet and "the name Isaac, are both derived from the same root." Ellicott adds, "What exactly Ishmael was doing is not said." In the Hebrew the participle has no object; it is the last word of the verse. The Septuagint adds one (*paizonta meta Isaak*, "playing with Isaac," in this lesson's rendering), and the BSB's "mocking her son" also supplies one. The Pulpit Commentary and Genesis Rabbah cite other verses with the same root, which the BSB renders "joking" (19:14), "caressing" (26:8), "make sport" (39:14, 39:17) and "indulge in revelry" (Exodus 32:6). How much of that range belongs in 21:9 is Positions (1).
- **Ishmael is not named in this chapter's Hebrew.** On a check of the Hebrew text (Sefaria), the name Ishmael does not occur in Genesis 21. He is "the son of Hagar the Egyptian," "the slave woman's son," and "the boy." Where the BSB prints "his son Ishmael" (21:11), the Hebrew says "his son." Gill reads the indirect description in 21:9 as "observed by way of slight, both to Hagar and her son."
- **Who says Hagar's name.** In the dialogue Sarah calls her "the slave woman" (21:10) and God, speaking to Abraham, "your maidservant" (21:12). The one speaker who says "Hagar" is the angel: "What is wrong, Hagar?" (21:17). The Genesis 16 lesson found the same pattern at 16:8.
- **"Heard," twice.** In 16:11 the angel explains the name Ishmael: "for the LORD has heard your cry of affliction." In 21:17 the verb comes twice: "God heard the voice of the boy," and "God has heard the voice of the boy where he lies." The text records Hagar's weeping (21:16), but what God is said to hear is the boy's voice; readers explain this differently (Positions 2). The link to 16:11 is this lesson's observation, not a claim any source read here makes.
- **"Your offspring," for both sons, and "a great nation."** God's two sentences sit side by side: "through Isaac your offspring will be reckoned" (21:12) and "because he is your offspring" (21:13). The Hebrew word is the same in both (*zera*, checked against the Hebrew text). In this app's BSB, "great nation" occurs four times in Genesis: twice to Abraham or Jacob (12:2, 46:3) and twice of Ishmael (17:20, 21:18).
- **"God was with the boy" (21:20) and "God is with you" (21:22).** In this app's BSB, "God was with" and "God is with" occur in Genesis only in these two adjacent verses: one about Ishmael in the wilderness, one spoken by a foreign king about Abraham.
- **"The angel of God" (21:17).** In chapter 16 it was "the angel of the LORD"; in this app's BSB, 21:17 is the first verse with "the angel of God." Ellicott reads the change: "Hagar was then still a member of Abraham's family; here she is so no longer." The change is checkable; the reason is his reading.
- **"Beersheba" appears before it is named.** By script, its first BSB occurrence is 21:14, "the Wilderness of Beersheba," seventeen verses before the place "was called Beersheba" (21:31). Calvin says Moses used the name there "proleptically." Benson gives both senses: "the well of the oath, or the well of the seven, (for the word equally signifies either,)." The Cambridge Bible calls the text's own explanation "clearly not a complete explanation of the word," with "the well of seven" "probably hinted at in Abraham's pledge of the seven lambs." Isaac names Beersheba again in 26:33 (see the connections). "From Dan to Beersheba" (or the reverse) later marks the length of the land in nine BSB verses.
- **"The Eternal God" (21:33).** By script, 21:33 is the first of only three BSB verses with "eternal God," in any capitalization (with Deuteronomy 33:27 and Romans 16:26). Ellicott gives the Hebrew as *El 'olam*, beside "El 'elyon" (14:22) and "El shaddai" (17:1). Onkelos renders it "Almighty of the universe." Ramban reads "He Who in His might directs the time," or "the G-d of the world." Rashi, following Sotah 10a, says that through Abraham's hospitality God came to be called "God of the entire Universe," because Abraham told his guests, "Bless Him of whose possessions you have eaten!"

**One translation choice in 21:14.** Ishmael was thirteen at his circumcision (17:25), so he was in his teens at the weaning; Ellicott estimates "sixteen or seventeen." The BSB reads, "put them on Hagar's shoulders, and sent her away with the boy." Rashi and Genesis Rabbah 53:13 read the compressed Hebrew as placing the boy himself on her shoulder, because he was ill. Ellicott answers: "The narrative, therefore, does not represent Ishmael as a small child."

**Ishmael and Hagar in Islamic tradition, briefly, as a different tradition's account.** A hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari (no. 3364, read directly) tells its own story of a mother and child left in a dry place. Abraham brings "Ishmael's mother" and her nursing son to a valley near the Kaaba with dates and "a small water-skin." She asks, "Has Allah ordered you to do so?" and, when he says yes, answers, "Then He will not neglect us." When the water runs out she runs seven times between Safa and Marwa, and the Prophet is reported as saying, "This is the source of the tradition of the walking of people between them"; an angel then brings out the water of Zamzam. This is a different tradition's account, reported so readers know Hagar and Ishmael are honored in a large living tradition; this lesson does not compare the accounts.

## Literary Design

**Two wells, and two sayings that God is "with" someone.** The chapter's two halves, joined by "At that time" (21:22), each turn on a well: one God shows Hagar (21:19), one secured by oath (21:25-31). In the first God is "with the boy" (21:20); in the second a king tells Abraham, "God is with you" (21:22). The echoes are checkable; whether the narrator built the pairing deliberately is not settled here.


## Positions

Three questions follow, then the history of how this chapter has been turned against living peoples. Each position is reported in named readers' own words; none is adjudicated here.

**1. What was Ishmael doing (21:9)?** The text gives one participle and Sarah's response. The readings fall into four groups.

- **Ordinary play, and a jealous mother.** Ibn Ezra: "Ishmael was acting as a boy is wont to act. Sarah was jealous because he was older than her son." The Cambridge Bible renders the word "playing" and argues that "there is no need to introduce the meaning of 'mockery,' which would require an object." It reads the Septuagint's wording ("playing with Isaac," in this lesson's rendering) as describing a scene in which Sarah "had been seized with a sudden fit of passionate jealousy."
- **Derision of Isaac or of the occasion.** Ramban, after weighing the other views, gives his own: "this event took place on the day that Isaac was weaned," and "Sarah saw Ishmael mocking Isaac or the great feast." Radak: "Ishmael was belittling Yitzchok for having a father who was so much older than he." Calvin: "a malignant expression of scorn, by which the forward youth manifested his contempt for his infant brother," and "Both (so to speak) are the sons of laughter: but in a very different sense." Ellicott dismisses "all those interpretations which charge him with abominable wickedness," since such guilt would not have left Abraham so grieved (21:11), but thinks Ishmael, after "fourteen years the heir," may have "by word and gesture derided and ridiculed" Isaac. The Pulpit Commentary lists Kimchi, Calvin, Keil and others for "mockery, scoffing."
- **Grave wrongdoing, and the rabbinic answer to it.** Genesis Rabbah 53:11 records several readings. Rabbi Akiva connects the word with sexual misconduct (citing Genesis 39:17), Rabbi Yishmael with idolatry (citing Exodus 32:6), and Rabbi Elazar son of Rabbi Yosei HaGelili with bloodshed (citing 2 Samuel 2:14). Rabbi Azarya, in the name of Rabbi Levi, has Ishmael shooting arrows toward Isaac and "conducting himself as though he was [just] playing." Rabbi Shimon ben Yohai frames the passage with his own answer: "Rabbi Akiva used to say something derogatory regarding this, but I say something complimentary regarding it." On his reading, the word concerns inheritance: Ishmael said, "I am the firstborn and I will inherit twice as much," which he infers from Sarah's own words in 21:10. Ramban quotes a parallel teaching (Tosefta Sotah 6:6) in which Rabbi Shimon son of Eleazar says, "Heaven forbid that such be in the house of the righteous one!" Rashi lists the three grave readings and then the inheritance quarrel, citing Genesis Rabbah. Gill reports the grave readings and says "there does not seem to be any foundation for them."
- **Paul's reading: persecution.** Galatians 4:29 reads, "At that time, however, the son born by the flesh persecuted the son born by the Spirit. It is the same now." The BSB of Genesis 21:9 says "mocking," so "persecuted" is Paul's word for it. Commentators explain it in different ways. Meyer says that "in Genesis 21:9 Ishmael is designated only as a mocker," and that "Paul follows the tradition, which, starting from the basis of that statement, went further." The Cambridge Bible says Paul "refers to one of" the rabbinic interpretations, "which understood the word to denote 'teasing' and 'persecution.'" Calvin keeps it to words: "Was it with sword or violence? Nay, but with the scorn of the virulent tongue." Ellicott reasons from Paul back to Genesis: "we may feel sure that Sarah was not without good reason for her conduct; for St. Paul bears witness that Ishmael persecuted Isaac." Ellicott's commentary on Galatians 4:29, a different volume, says the reverse about the Hebrew: "It seems doubtful whether the Hebrew can really mean more than 'playing.'"

What can be checked: Genesis 21:9 gives one word and no object in the Hebrew, Sarah's demand in 21:10 is about inheritance, and the chapter states no verdict on the boy.

**2. How should a reader weigh Sarah's demand, Abraham's provision, and God's part (21:10-21)?** The text records each act and judges none of the three.

- **The expulsion as God's will carried out through Sarah, with the exiles at fault.** Calvin holds that Sarah, "being moved by womanly feelings, exceeded the bounds of moderation," yet "her tongue and mind were governed by a secret impulse of the Spirit." On the provision he asks, "with what a slender provision does he endow his wife and her son?" He offers two possible explanations, one being that Abraham "limited her provision, in order that she might not go far from his house," for "doubtless he would prefer to have them near himself, for the purpose of rendering them such assistance as they would need." He then reads the hardship as deserved: "Such was the reward of the pride, by which they had been vainly inflated." On 21:17 he declines to credit Hagar and the boy with repentance, "of which I can see no sign," and says God helped them "because he was the son of Abraham." Matthew Henry: "If Hagar and Ishmael had behaved well in Abraham's family, they might have continued there; but they were justly punished." Rashi, citing Exodus Rabbah, says Abraham gave bread and water "not silver and gold, because he hated him for taking to degenerate ways," and, citing Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer 30, that in the wilderness Hagar "reverted to the idol worship of her father's house." Ellicott places the strain on both women: Hagar's "bitterness would grow more intense," and "These jealousies are the inevitable results of polygamy; and wherever it exists, the father's life is made wretched by the intrigues of the women for their children."
- **Abraham obedient, and less harsh than he looks.** Ibn Ezra answers those who ask "Where was his kindness?": "I am amazed at those who are amazed at Abraham, for Abraham acted according to God's dictates." He adds that Abraham gave enough to reach Beersheba, that "it is possible that Abraham gave Hagar some gold and silver, although this is not mentioned in Scripture," and that after Sarah's death "he gave gifts to Ishmael's children" (citing 25:6). Radak likewise supposes Abraham gave "silver and gold" though "the Torah did not make a specific mention of this." Barnes says "Hagar obtained her freedom." Genesis Rabbah 53:13 calls Abraham's household "generous."
- **The cost named, without excusing it.** Ramban says Abraham was grieved "on account of his son," and places the meagre provision on Sarah's command, which Abraham was bound to obey: "it was at her command that he did not give them silver and gold, servants, and camels to bear them." On Genesis 16, Ramban had already said, "Our mother did transgress by this affliction, and Abraham also by his permitting her to do so." He continues that God "heard her [Hagar's] affliction and gave her a son who would be a wild-ass of a man," to "afflict the seed of Abraham and Sarah with all kinds of affliction." Freidenreich reads this as Ramban explaining the suffering of Jews in his own day.
- **God's care for Hagar and the boy, as readers describe it.** Genesis Rabbah 53:14 reads Psalm 56's "Put my tears in Your flask" (in its own translation) as David's plea that God treat his tears "like that woman with the flask," and has David say, "To the tears of Hagar You were not silent." Genesis Rabbah 53:13 reports Rabbi Berekhya's reading of 21:16 as a protest: Hagar reminds God that "Yesterday you said to me" that her descendants would be multiplied (16:10), and "now he is dying of thirst." Genesis Rabbah 53:14 also says that in filling the skin "she was lacking in faith." Rashi reads "where he lies" (literally "where he is") as God judging the boy by "his present deeds." The angels object on account of what his descendants would later do to Israel, and God answers, "According to his present deeds will I judge him." Ramban prefers a plainer sense: God heard the boy "in the place in which he was." Ellicott reads "take him by the hand" (21:18) as a charge for the future: she was to be "his brave and faithful protector, such as we learn that she really became." Calvin also writes of 21:17 that "when father and mother forsake us, the Lord will take us up."

What can be checked: God's instruction to Abraham comes paired with a promise for the boy (21:13). In the wilderness God hears (21:17), speaks to Hagar by name (21:17), repeats the "great nation" promise (21:18), shows her water (21:19), and is "with the boy" (21:20). Later Genesis keeps both sons in view: "His sons Isaac and Ishmael buried him" (25:9), Ishmael's twelve sons are listed as "twelve princes of their tribes" (25:16), and Esau marries "Mahalath... daughter of Abraham's son Ishmael" (28:9).

**3. In Galatians 4:21-31, whom do Hagar and the "slave woman's son" stand for?** Paul writes to Gentile believers in Galatia who, he says, are being pressed to be circumcised (5:2; 6:12-13). He calls the two women "two covenants" (4:24), says "Hagar stands for Mount Sinai in Arabia and corresponds to the present-day Jerusalem" (4:25), says "It is the same now" (4:29), and quotes 21:10 (4:30). Named readers identify his targets in three ways.

- **The Jewish people and Judaism.** The Cambridge Bible on 4:25: "Jerusalem stands for the whole Jewish people, nationally considered." Meyer: "the present Jerusalem and its children... represent the Israelitic commonwealth and its members." Byrne's abstract notes that the two covenants have been "traditionally identified with Judaism and Christianity respectively" (the abstract does not state Byrne's own identification). Jerome, as Freidenreich quotes him, reported that "Nearly all the commentators on this passage [of Galatians] interpret it to mean that the slave woman Hagar represents the Law and the Jewish people, but that the free woman Sarah symbolizes the church," a reading Freidenreich says Jerome "himself endorsed." Some draw harder conclusions. Matthew Poole: "even so it is now, the carnal seed of Abraham, the Jews, persecute the Christians." Meyer reads 4:30 as teaching "the exclusion of the non-free Jews" from the people of God. Ellicott on 4:30 calls it "a bold declaration of the incompatibility of Judaism with Christianity," though on 4:29 the same commentary says "It is the same now" has "especial reference to the behaviour of the Judaising party in Galatia, but would also apply to the relations between Jews and Christians generally."
- **Rival teachers within the Jesus movement who urged circumcision on Gentiles.** Meyer reports, and rejects, Hofmann's reading that the readers "are to dismiss from among them... those who desired to force upon them their own legalism." Brendan Byrne's abstract summarizes J. Louis Martyn's view that the two covenants refer to "a Christian Jewish Law-observant Gentile mission," whose "Teachers" are "disturbing Paul's Galatian converts," set against "the Law-free Gentile mission promulgated by Paul." David Freidenreich, a rabbi and professor of Jewish Studies at Colby College, reads Paul as allegorizing the expulsion "to argue that his rivals should be expelled from the church." He describes those rivals as "the Jerusalem-based Christ-believers" who insisted that Gentile believers adopt "Jewish practices such as circumcision." He notes that Paul "and most other members of the community he joined proudly self-identified as Jews." Nicholas J. Schaser (Israel Bible Center) argues from Paul's other uses of "Jerusalem" that "'Hagar' represents any follower of Jesus (regardless of ethnicity) who would demand circumcision for Gentiles."
- **Two kinds of people within the church in every age.** Calvin, in his commentary on Genesis 21:12, reads the figure as "the perpetual condition of the Church": "two kinds of persons born in the Church," the faithful and "hypocritical disciples." He applies it in his own day: "while at this day, the Papists proudly exult," and "our own age also has its Ishmaelites," itself a polemical use of the figure across church lines. He also says the apostles "were oppressed by the Jewish hypocrites of their age," so his reading does not leave Paul's Jewish opponents out.

A Catholic statement sits partly across these lines. The Pontifical Biblical Commission (2001) says the passage's mention of "Sarah and Hagar serves to demonstrate that Gentiles who believe in Christ are 'children of the promise,'" and (section 81, citing Galatians 4:28-29) distinguishes the "children of the promise like Isaac" from children "according to the flesh." It also says that in Galatians 4:24 "it is a question of typology." The document read here does not say whom Paul's "present-day Jerusalem" means. Freidenreich, Martyn and Schaser each tie the passage to a dispute among believers in Jesus over Gentile circumcision rather than to Jews or Judaism as such.

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the first reading: Schaser argues that "Paul's use of 'Jerusalem' always refers to the Jesus assembly," and Freidenreich that the Jewish-people reading arose after "Christianity became a mostly gentile movement." Against the second: Meyer calls Hofmann's account of "persecuted" "very inappropriate" and his reading of 4:30 a "weakening of the idea" that is "impossible," and Byrne argues that "in the light, particularly, of Paul's overall usage of 'covenant', Martyn's interpretation is not sustainable." Byrne adds at once, "though this need not imply a return to an anti-Jewish interpretation of the text." Against the third, Meyer's footnote rejects Calvin's view that Paul meant only mocking: it "is not in harmony with the comprehensive sense of the word."

**How this chapter and Paul's use of it have been turned against Jews, Muslims and Arabs.** Freidenreich's article, read in full, documents this history.

- **Against Jews: from reading to contempt and force.** In Freidenreich's account, the Jewish-people reading "remained dominant among Christians into the late 20th century." Holding reading 1 is not the same as the uses documented here. Byrne's abstract treats the two as separable.
- **Against dissenters, Muslims and Jews by force.** Freidenreich writes that Augustine "justifies violence against those who challenge Christian orthodoxy," quoting him (*Homilies on the Gospel of John* 11.13): "this is God stirring up Sarah to give Hagar a beating." He quotes Oldradus de Ponte, a fourteenth-century lawyer at the papal court, who identified Hagar with Islam and argued that the Church may treat Muslims as Sarah treated Hagar, "by driving her out and depriving her children of inheritance and possession." Freidenreich reports that Oldradus "offers identical guidance regarding Christian–Jewish relations," and that "premodern Christians frequently portray Jews and Muslims alike as the heirs of Hagar." He sums up the effect: such rhetoric "justifies the use of force even against subservient neighbors." Augustine's and Oldradus's own texts were not read for this lesson; their words are given as Freidenreich quotes them.
- **Against Arab peoples.** Some classic commentaries carry the old equation of Ishmael's line with the Arabs into their notes on this passage. Ellicott's note on Galatians 4:29, for example, describes the Arab tribes as long-standing enemies of Israel. The Genesis 16 lesson documents the same equation at 16:12 and Croteau and Yates's answer to it.

**What answered it.** The Second Vatican Council's *Nostra Aetate* (1965) states, "Although the Church is the new people of God, the Jews should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God, as if this followed from the Holy Scriptures" (section 4). Of Muslims it says that "the Church regards with esteem also the Moslems," and, since "not a few quarrels and hostilities have arisen between Christians and Moslems," it "urges all to forget the past and to work sincerely for mutual understanding" (section 3). Byrne's abstract, from a Catholic New Testament scholar, rejects Martyn's specific reading while saying that this "need not imply a return to an anti-Jewish interpretation." How Paul relates to Judaism is not adjudicated here.

**What the texts themselves do and do not say, checked across Genesis and Galatians.** Genesis 21 applies "Expel" to one woman and one boy in one household, and in the same speech God promises the boy a nation "because he is your offspring" (21:13). Genesis has Isaac and Ishmael bury their father together (25:9) and lists Ishmael's tribes (25:12-16) without applying Sarah's words to them. Paul applies 21:10 to his own day ("present-day Jerusalem," 4:25; "It is the same now," 4:29), and whom he means is Position 3. No verse applies the words to any people after Paul's time; readers who do so bring that to the texts.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, a model of the shape and not an answer key; the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step. It deliberately does not build a bridge from the expulsion (21:9-14), since a worked "application" of it could be heard as a judgment on someone sent away. It works from the treaty at Beersheba instead.

**Passage element.** Genesis 21:22-30. Abimelech asks for an oath of good faith (21:23). Before swearing, Abraham "complained to Abimelech about a well that Abimelech's servants had seized" (21:25). Abimelech answers, "You did not tell me, so I have not heard about it until today" (21:26). Abraham gives seven ewe lambs "as my witness that I dug this well" (21:30).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the grievance is raised during the treaty, the other side says it did not know, and a public token seals the agreement. Inference: a first audience used to disputes over wells would likely have heard a model of settling a claim openly with a witness. The text states no such moral.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** A standing grievance is better raised directly, before a new agreement is made, and the agreement is better recorded in a way both sides can point to later.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: neighbors, a disputed resource, a grievance one side never heard, and a visible record. Not carried forward: the oath "before God," the covenant setting, and who was right about the well.

**Application class and promise scope.** Principle carried by analogy, not a command. Promise scope: none claimed.

**Modern domain and situation.** Neighbors. Two households share a driveway, and one family's renters have been parking across the other's spot. The owners are now drafting a shared-maintenance agreement.

**Response type and faithful response.** Raising a grievance plainly and recording the settlement. Before signing, one owner names the problem, hears the other out, and both add a written line about parking.

**Cautions.** If a dispute involves threats, harassment or a legal claim to property, a qualified person, such as a mediator or attorney, not a study note, is the right next step. The bridge is an analogy the learner is testing.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. Positions (1) reports readers who hear "play," "mockery," grave wrongdoing, and (with Paul) "persecution" in one participle. What in 21:9-10 itself, and not in a later reading, would you point to first? Does Sarah's reason in 21:10 count as evidence about 21:9?
2. God tells Abraham to listen to Sarah (21:12) and in the same breath promises a nation from the boy (21:13). Does the text let a reader say that God approved Sarah's motive, or only her outcome? What would you need to decide that?

3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: by reporting Calvin's "Such was the reward of the pride" beside Genesis Rabbah's "To the tears of Hagar You were not silent," without choosing, the lesson gives a reading that blames an expelled slave woman the same standing as one that does not. Before answering, check what the lesson claims: each reading in its own words, with the text's record of God's care set out separately.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) Genesis 21:9 does not say what Ishmael did; every account of it, including Paul's "persecuted," is a reading of one participle. (b) Genesis 21 does not apply "Expel the slave woman and her son" to any later people, and neither does Galatians; Paul applies it within an argument to Gentile believers about circumcision (Galatians 5:2-6). (c) The name "Ishmael" at 21:11 in the BSB is the translators' clarification of "his son." (d) Genesis 21 does not say Hagar and Ishmael were outside God's care. It says the opposite in 21:17-20.

One popular use this lesson declines to author: reading Galatians 4:29's "It is the same now" as a statement about Jews, Muslims or Arabs of later centuries.
