Jacob leaves home with his brother planning to kill him (27:41-45). He stops for the night at "a certain place," puts a stone under his head, and dreams: "a ladder that rested on the earth with its top reaching up to heaven, and God's angels were going up and down the ladder" (28:12). The LORD stands at the top and repeats to Jacob the promises made to Abraham and Isaac, adding a personal one: "Look, I am with you, and I will watch over you wherever you go" (28:15). He sets up the stone as a pillar, pours oil on it, names the place Bethel, "house of God," and makes a vow that begins with "if" (28:20). In John's Gospel, Jesus tells Nathanael, "you will all see heaven open and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man" (John 1:51). This lesson reads the dream closely, tests that New Testament line against the Greek it shares with the Septuagint, and reports, without a verdict, what Jewish and Christian readers have made of the ladder, the "if," and the tenth Jacob promised.

## Context

**What happens, in order (28:10-22).** Jacob leaves Beersheba for Haran (28:10). At sunset he stops, takes "one of the stones from that place," and lies down (28:11). He dreams of the ladder and the angels (28:12). The LORD, standing "there at the top," names Himself, promises the land, numberless descendants and a blessing for "All the families of the earth" (28:13-14), then promises to be with Jacob, to bring him back, and "not leave you until I have done what I have promised you" (28:15). Jacob wakes, is afraid, and calls the place "the house of God" and "the gate of heaven" (28:16-17). In the morning he sets up the stone as a pillar, pours oil on it, and names the place Bethel, "though previously the city had been named Luz" (28:18-19). He vows: if God is with him, keeps him, feeds and clothes him and brings him home safely, "then the LORD will be my God," the stone "will be God's house," and "of all that You give me I will surely give You a tenth" (28:20-22).

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens puts Genesis 12 through Malachi in stage 5 (machine-readable `chapters: ["1.12"]`). The previous unit ended with the stolen blessing; Esau's threat (27:41) and Isaac's charge to find a wife in Paddan-aram (28:1-5) send Jacob away. Esau's marriage (28:6-9) interrupts the story. Jacob's arrival in Haran (29:1) belongs to the next unit.

**Key words, read as observations.** Each count below was made by this lesson's own script over the app's BSB text, unless a lexicon is named.

- **"Dream" (28:12).** In this app's BSB, the first verses in the Bible with "dream" are 20:3 and 20:6, where God speaks to the Philistine king Abimelech "in a dream." Genesis 28:12 is the next, and the first in which a patriarch dreams. (Abram receives "a vision" at 15:1; Isaac is told only that "that night the LORD appeared to him," 26:24.) The Hebrew matches: Bible Hub's concordance lists the noun *ḥălôm* first at 20:3, and the verb *ḥālam*, "to dream," which 28:12 uses, first at 28:12. The Cambridge Bible compares 20:3. Rabbi Abbahu in Genesis Rabbah 68:12 warns that "The content of dreams has no effect." Calvin calls this "no common dream."
- **"Ladder" (28:12).** "Ladder" occurs in this app's BSB only here, and Bible Hub's concordance lists the Hebrew *sullām* only here (Strong's 5551, with the gloss "a stair-case"). The Cambridge Bible says that "we must not understand a house ladder, with uprights and rung of wood; but, rather, a stairway, or ascent by successive terraces." It adds, "Possibly, the 'ladder' here mentioned resembled the ascent to Babylonian and Assyrian temples, in which the shrine or sanctuary, on the summit, was reached by steps leading through seven terraces," and refers to its note on the tower of Babel (11:4). It also reports Stanley's suggestion that the ledges of rock on the Bethel hill took "the shape, in his dream, of a flight of steps." Jamieson-Fausset-Brown reports "Some writers" for whom the image was "a gigantic mountain pile, whose sides, indented in the rock, gave it the appearance of a scaling ladder." The BSB's "reaching up to heaven" (28:12) is close to its tower of Babel, "a tower that reaches to the heavens" (11:4); the comparison is the reader's, not the text's.
- **"Going up and down" and "on it."** The angels go up first. Rashi asks why ("It states first ascending and afterwards descending!"). The Cambridge Bible answers that "the process of Divine ministration to the sons of men has been going on before it is finally revealed." The last Hebrew word of the verse is *bô*, which Bible Hub's interlinear renders "on it" and tags as a third-person masculine suffix. *Sullām* is masculine, so the same word can mean "on it" (the ladder) or "on him" (Jacob). The Septuagint, in Swete's text on Bible Hub, chooses: "οἱ ἄγγελοι τοῦ θεοῦ ἀνέβαινον καὶ κατέβαινον ἐπ᾽ αὐτῆς," with a feminine pronoun. It cannot refer to Jacob, and it most naturally refers back to the feminine κλίμαξ, "ladder." The question returns in Positions (2).
- **"Behold" and "place."** Bible Hub's interlinear shows the Hebrew *hinnēh*, "behold," four times in 28:12-15 (twice in 28:12, once each in 28:13 and 28:15, where the BSB has "Look"). Ellicott counts three in the dream itself: "each of the three stages in the dream has emphasis given to it by the word behold." The same interlinear shows *māqôm*, "place," six times in 28:11-19: three times in 28:11, then once each in 28:16, 28:17 and 28:19. Genesis Rabbah 68:9 asks why God is called *ha-makom*, "the place": God "is the place of the world, and His world is not His place." Ibn Ezra rejects that line of reading here: "we never find in the entire Bible the word makom (place) meaning God."
- **"Above it" (28:13).** The BSB has "there at the top the LORD was standing." Bible Hub's interlinear renders the Hebrew *ʿālāw* "above it," again with a masculine suffix. The Cambridge Bible says it is "Better, probably, as R.V. marg., beside him. Both renderings are possible." Onkelos (in Sefaria's English) has "stood over him," and Rashi "STOOD ABOVE HIM." Calvin says that those who render the preposition "near" "entirely destroy the sense of the passage."
- **"Angels of God."** "God's angels" (28:12) and "the angels of God" (32:1, when Jacob comes back toward Canaan) are the only Genesis verses in this app's BSB with that phrase. The Cambridge Bible calls it "most rare" and cross-refers 32:1-2.
- **"House of God," "gate of heaven" (28:17).** In this app's BSB, "the gate of heaven" occurs only at 28:17. "House of God" occurs in 76 verses. In Genesis, 28:17 is the only one, and it is also the first in the Bible. The Cambridge Bible calls the clause "the popular etymology of the name Bethel."
- **Pillar, oil and vow (28:18-22).** Bible Hub's concordance lists the Hebrew *maṣṣēbāh*, "pillar," first at 28:18. (The "pillar of salt" of 19:26 is a different word.) "Oil" occurs in Genesis only at 28:18 and 35:14. "Vow" occurs in Genesis only at 28:20 and 31:13. Benson and the Cambridge Bible call this the first religious vow in Scripture. The word "tenth," used of giving, occurs in Genesis only at 14:20 (Abram) and 28:22; the other "tenth" in Genesis (8:5) is a month.

**Bethel after Genesis 28.** "Bethel" occurs in 65 verses of this app's BSB. The narrator already uses the name at 12:8, before Jacob gives it. Genesis returns to the place twice (31:13; 35:1-15), and Jacob, near death, remembers it as "Luz" (48:3). Later, Jeroboam set a golden calf "in Bethel" (1 Kings 12:29). Amos mocks the shrine ("Go to Bethel and transgress," Amos 4:4), and a priest there calls it "the sanctuary of the king" (Amos 7:13). Hosea calls it "Beth-aven" (Hosea 4:15; 10:5), and also remembers the patriarch: "he found Him at Bethel and spoke with Him there" (Hosea 12:4). The connections below grade those links.

## Literary Design

**The promise and the vow match.** God's personal promise has four parts: "I am with you," "I will watch over you wherever you go," "I will bring you back to this land," and "I will not leave you" (28:15). Jacob's vow repeats them as conditions: "If God will be with me and watch over me on this journey, and if He will provide me with food to eat and clothes to wear, so that I may return safely to my father's house" (28:20-21). Rashi takes each clause of the vow from 28:15 ("even as He said to me"). The Cambridge Bible lists "three conditions," presence, preservation and return. Genesis Rabbah 69:6 notices the gap: "He answered him regarding everything, but regarding sustenance, he did not answer him." Rabbi Isi replies that "I will not leave you" covers sustenance too. Whether the vow's echo of the promise is trust or bargaining is Positions (3).

**The stone frames the scene.** A stone goes under Jacob's head (28:11), becomes a pillar with oil on it (28:18), and in the vow "will be God's house" (28:22). The Cambridge Bible notes that in 28:22 "the title 'God's house' is applied to the stone itself."

## Positions

Four questions follow. Each is reported through named readers in their own words. None is adjudicated, and nothing in a position's statement should be read as this lesson's own conclusion.

**1. What does the ladder stand for?** The text says that the ladder rested on the earth, reached heaven, carried angels going up and down, and had the LORD at the top (28:12-13). It gives no explanation, except what the LORD then says. Four kinds of reading follow.

- **God's providence and the work of angels.** Ibn Ezra: "The way to interpret Jacob's dream is to view it as a parable. It teaches that nothing is hidden from God and that what happens below is contingent on the decree from above." Angels go up "to inform God what they have seen on earth" and others "come down to fulfill God's commands." Ramban: God "showed Jacob that whatever is done on earth is effected by means of the angels," but promised that Jacob "will not be under the power of the angels, but he will be G-d's portion." Keil: "The ladder was a visible symbol of the real and uninterrupted fellowship between God in heaven and His people upon earth." Benson and Matthew Henry give it as a first meaning.
- **Israel's worship and history (midrash).** Genesis Rabbah 68:12 gives several readings side by side. Bar Kappara reads the ladder as the altar ramp: "'Behold, a ladder' – this is the ramp," and the angels are "the High Priests." "The Rabbis interpret it regarding Sinai": "'He dreamed, and behold, a ladder' – this is Sinai," with Moses and Aaron as the angels going up and down the mountain. The midrash adds, "The letters of this equals the letters of that," a play on letters that Ibn Ezra describes as making the ladder "numerically equivalent" to Sinai. Another reading (68:14) takes the angels as the angels of "the four kingdoms," each going up and then down. Rashi on 28:12 keeps one reading from the same section: the angels "who accompanied him in the land of Israel were not permitted to leave the Land: they ascended to Heaven and angels which were to minister outside the Land descended to accompany him."
- **Christ the mediator (Protestant commentators).** Calvin: "It is Christ alone, therefore, who connects heaven and earth: he is the only Mediator who reaches from heaven down to earth." He cites John 1:51 and concludes, "If, then, we say that the ladder is a figure of Christ, the exposition will not be forced." The Geneva Bible's note: "Christ is the ladder by which God and man are joined together." Matthew Henry's second meaning is "The mediation of Christ. He is this ladder; the foot on earth in his human nature, the top in heaven in his Divine nature." Gill, Benson and Poole say the same. Poole calls this reading "warranted by our Saviour himself, John 1:51."
- **The angels and the stone (early Christian readers), and the Mother of God (Orthodox hymnody).** Augustine (*Tractates on John* 7.23) reads the angels as preachers: "the angels of God are good preachers, preaching Christ; this is the meaning of, they ascend and descend upon the Son of man." For him the anointed stone points to Christ: "Why anointed? Because Christus comes from chrisma." Justin Martyr (*Dialogue* 86) writes that "the stone symbolically proclaimed Christ" and that the anointing "had reference to Him." In the Akathist Hymn, as published by the Orthodox Church in America, the angel greets the Virgin: "Rejoice! Heavenly Ladder by which God came down: Rejoice! Bridge leading men from earth to heaven."

**Objections each reading faces.** Calvin rejects the providence reading: "The interpretation of some of the Hebrews, that the ladder is a figure of the Divine Providence, cannot be admitted: for the Lord has given another sign more suitable." Ibn Ezra rejects the midrashic readings: "Those who say that the ladder seen by Jacob refers to a statue, or that it refers to Sinai because it is numerically equivalent to it, are indulging in Midrash." Against reading the ladder itself as Christ, the Pulpit Commentary on 28:12 holds that it is "more probable" that Jacob, not the ladder, is the type of the Son of Man (Position 2). Against the patristic stone reading, Calvin writes of those who make the stone "a symbol of Christ": "I do not know that any such thing entered the mind of Moses or of Jacob." No source read for this lesson argues against the Marian reading. That is a gap in this lesson's sourcing, not evidence either way.

**2. On the ladder, or on Jacob? And what does John 1:51 do with the dream?** John 1:51 comes at the end of the scene with Nathanael. Jesus greets him as "a true Israelite, in whom there is no deceit" (John 1:47), Nathanael calls Him "the King of Israel" (1:49), and Jesus answers with the angels "ascending and descending on the Son of Man." Ellicott on John 1:47 hears Jacob there too: "The word for 'guile' is the same word as the LXX. word for 'subtlety' in Genesis 27:35," and the thought is one "in whom there is nothing of the Jacob." (The Septuagint of 27:35 has μετὰ δόλου, and John 1:47 has δόλος, in the Greek texts read on Bible Hub. The BSB renders them "deceitfully" and "deceit.") The readings below differ on whom the angels are "on."

- **On the ladder.** The Septuagint's feminine pronoun rules out Jacob and points to the ladder (Context). In Genesis Rabbah 68:12 (and again at 69:3), "Rabbi Ḥiyya and Rabbi Yanai, one said: 'Ascending and descending' on the ladder, and one said: 'Ascending and descending' regarding Jacob." The midrash adds of the first view, "it works out well." Onkelos (in Sefaria's English) has the angels "ascending and descending on it." Among Christian readers, the Pulpit Commentary lists Calvin, Luther, Ainsworth, the *Speaker's Commentary* and Murphy as reading John 1:51 so that the ladder in Jacob's vision is "an emblem of himself, the one Mediator between God and man." Jamieson-Fausset-Brown writes that in John 1:51 Jesus shows "the Son of man the real Ladder of this intercourse."
- **On Jacob.** The other rabbi in Genesis Rabbah 68:12 reads the angels as going up to see Jacob's "likeness" and down to find him: "'Ascending' on High and seeing his likeness, descending below, and finding him asleep." Targum Pseudo-Jonathan (Etheridge's translation) has the angels say, "Come, see Jakob the pious, whose likeness is inlaid in the throne of glory," after which "the rest of the angels of the holy Lord descended to look upon him." Gill, on John 1:51, says that "some of the Jewish writers ... understand the ascent, and descent of the angels ... to be, not upon the ladder, but upon Jacob; which makes the phrase there still more agreeable to this." He cites "Bereshit Rabba, sect. 68" and "sect. 69." The Pulpit Commentary on Genesis 28:12 names Hengstenberg, Baumgarten, Lange and Bush for the reading that Jesus takes "Jacob himself as type of him, the Son of man."
- **Neither, chiefly: the open heaven of Jesus' ministry.** Some commentators on John read the line less as a key to the ladder than as a promise about the ministry that follows. Meyer calls the angels "a symbolical representation of the uninterrupted and living intercourse subsisting between the Messiah and God," which the disciples "would see as a matter of experience throughout the ministry of Jesus." Barnes takes it as evidence that God's aid would show "more fully that he was the Messiah." The Expositor's Greek Testament: "What Jacob had dreamt was in Christ realised," so that the Son of Man "makes earth a Bethel, and the gate of heaven."

The Pulpit Commentary reports the first two Christian readings side by side and calls the second "what is more probable." The other readers above state their own reading without arguing against the alternatives. One textual note bears on all three readings. The Byzantine text and the Textus Receptus begin Jesus' saying with ἀπ' ἄρτι, "from now on" (the KJV's "Hereafter"). Nestle 1904, Westcott-Hort and Tischendorf lack it, and the Cambridge Bible on John says it "is wanting in the best MSS." Ellicott notes "early evidence for the insertion" and suggests that the omission arose because it "removes a difficulty in the interpretation."

**3. Faith or bargain: what is Jacob's "if"?** The text gives the facts every reading must explain: God has just promised presence, protection and return (28:15); Jacob "was afraid" (28:17); and his vow begins "If God will be with me" (28:20). Readers also divide on where the "then" begins. The BSB, Keil (who says "והיה in Genesis 28:21 commences the apodosis") and the Cambridge Bible start it at "then the LORD will be my God." Rashi, Ellicott and the Pulpit Commentary keep "the LORD will be my God" among the conditions, so the promise proper begins with the stone (28:22). Three readings follow.

- **Not doubt but gratitude: "if" as "since."** Calvin: "by interposing this condition, Jacob did not by any means act from distrust, as if he doubted of God's continual protection; but that in this manner made provision against his own infirmity." He grants that Jacob "seems indeed, at first sight, like a mercenary," but answers that "he depends entirely upon the promises given unto him." Jamieson-Fausset-Brown: "Let 'if' be changed into 'since,' and the language will appear a proper expression of Jacob's faith." Matthew Poole: he speaks not "like a mercenary person, make a bargain with God, but rather supposeth that God will do this for him." Ellicott: the vow "implies no doubt on his part, but is his acceptance of the terms of the covenant." Ramban allows a similar sense on the plain meaning: "ve'im does not mean 'if it occurs,' but rather 'when it occurs.'"
- **A real condition, resting on God's own promise.** Rashi reads "If God will be with me" as "if He will keep for me these promises which He has made me." Ramban explains the condition: "The reason for the condition is lest the sin cause the abrogation of the promises." He quotes Genesis Rabbah: "from here you infer that there is no assurance to the righteous in this world." In Genesis Rabbah 70:4, one of two rabbis holds that "The portion is out of order," since God "had already promised him"; the other has Jacob say, "If the conditions that He said, to be with me and to keep me, will be fulfilled, I will fulfill my vow." Keil: "if Jehovah, who had appeared to him, proved Himself to be God by fulfilling His promise, then he would acknowledge and worship Him as his God."
- **Calculation or bargaining.** The Cambridge Bible on 28:21: "Jacob's vow, with the conditions attached to it, reflects his calculating character. But it acknowledges that Jehovah is the God who has revealed Himself to his fathers, and is distinct from mere nature-gods." Andreas Köstenberger and David Croteau (*Bulletin for Biblical Research*, 2006) argue that "it appears that Jacob's vow shows his lack of trust in God's word," since "God had already promised to fulfill three of these four conditions." They quote Walter Brueggemann, who reads the scene more positively but still writes, "Jacob will be Jacob. Even in this solemn moment, he still sounds like a bargain-hunter," and Thomas Whartenby, who they say "generally views Jacob positively in this passage": "The man who has always lived by his wits now seeks to strike a bargain."

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the "since" reading, Köstenberger and Croteau point to the structure of the vow itself: "Jacob's vow is very revealing in that it is a conditional vow. 'If' God does what he asks, 'then' he will do the following." Against reading the "if" as a real condition, the Pulpit Commentary calls it "not the language of uncertainty." Against Rashi's way of holding "the LORD will be my God" inside the condition, Ramban objects: "This is not a condition, as Rashi would have it. It is rather a vow." The Cambridge Bible prefers the BSB-style division, calling it "on the whole preferable." Against the bargaining reading, and against dividing the verse in a way that implies it, the Pulpit Commentary holds that "to have bargained and bartered with God in the way which this suggests before assenting to accept him as an object of trust and worship would have been little less than criminal," which is why it keeps "the LORD will be my God" among the conditions. Köstenberger and Croteau themselves report that Gordon Wenham, "along with most scholars, views Jacob as being portrayed positively." They also quote Allen Ross: "Vows were not made to induce God to do something He was not willing to do."

**4. The tenth, and giving today.** Genesis records two tenths before the law of Moses: Abram's gift to Melchizedek (14:20) and Jacob's promise here (28:22). Genesis never records Jacob paying it. The Cambridge Bible calls the promise "Very strange," and connects it with the later practice at Bethel ("cf. Amos 4:4"). Ibn Ezra reads it as giving "out of respect for God to one who is fit to receive it." Christians who disagree about whether believers today should give a tithe cite these two verses on both sides. This lesson reports that disagreement and does not take a side.

- **A practice older than the law.** Travis Arnold (Portland Bible College, 2014) argues that "tithing existed before the Law of Moses. Abraham tithed to Melchizedek (Gen 14:20; Heb 7:6). Jacob vowed to tithe to the Lord at Bethel (Gen 28:22)," so that it "is a biblical practice that precedes the Law and exists on its own quite apart from the Law." He adds: "We do not have to tithe to be righteous."
- **An occasional gift, not a pattern.** Köstenberger and Croteau argue that "The texts that discuss tithing prior to the Mosaic Law do not portray tithing as a systematic, continual practice but as an occasional, even exceptional, form of giving," and that "Nowhere in Genesis is Jacob ever recorded as giving this tithe to God."

Each side anticipates the other. Arnold grants that "the law requiring us to tithe has been nailed to the cross (Col 2:14)" before arguing that the principle remains. Köstenberger and Croteau grant that "since much of the argument is based upon silence, there remains the possibility that tithing did exist," and answer that a pre-law custom need not bind Christians, as with circumcision. Older commentators who praise the tenth also qualify it. Matthew Henry: "The tenth is a fit proportion to be devoted to God ... though it may be more or less, as God prospers us."

Where this question touches your own money, these are reports of how others read the text, not advice. Decisions about giving belong to you, in conversation with your own pastor or faith community. If money is tight or debt is pressing, a qualified financial counselor can help.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, as a model of the shape, not an answer key. It deliberately does not build a bridge from the vow or the tenth, because Positions (3) and (4) show how sharply readers disagree about both.

**Passage element.** Genesis 28:16-17. Jacob wakes in a place he chose only "because the sun had set" (28:11) and says, "Surely the LORD is in this place, and I was unaware of it."

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Jacob is far from home, in a place he did not choose. The text records surprise ("I was unaware of it") and fear ("How awesome is this place!"). Keil reads the surprise as finding that God should be near "even here, far away from his father's house and from the places consecrated to His worship." Ellicott reads it the same way. The Pulpit Commentary also reports Calvin's alternative: Jacob found "that whereas he fancied himself alone, he was in reality in the company of God."

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** A place passed through as ordinary, or a season entered unwillingly, may turn out to matter in ways a person did not see at the time. Noticing that, and marking it, is a different act from moving on without looking back.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a person away from home in a stretch they did not choose, and the act of stopping to notice. Not carried forward: the dream, the angels, the promise of land and descendants, the vow, the pillar and the tenth.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed. The promise "I am with you" (28:15) was spoken to Jacob. Whether and how it applies to anyone else is a question for the reader's own tradition, not for this bridge.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work and relocation. Someone has moved to a new city for a job they took because it was the only offer. The first months feel like a waiting room.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: attentiveness and remembrance. One faithful response is to keep a short weekly record of what happened in that season, such as people met and help received, and to read it back after a year.

**Cautions.** The bridge does not say that every hard season hides a blessing, or that a lonely person should simply look harder. Loneliness and distress after a move are real. If they persist, or if you feel unsafe or hopeless, talk to a doctor, counselor or pastor; in the US you can call or text 988 at any hour. The bridge is an analogy the learner is testing and may find does not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. The angels go up before they come down. Does the order matter to the dream, and does it matter to John 1:51, which keeps it?
2. Read 28:15 and 28:20-21 side by side. Which words in Jacob's vow come from God's promise, and which are new? What would you need to know about Hebrew "if" before deciding whether the vow doubts the promise?
3. Jacob says "I was unaware of it" (28:16). What in 28:10-11 shows that he had not chosen the place?
4. Bethel is "the house of God" in 28:17 and the site of a golden calf in 1 Kings 12:29. How might a later reader of Genesis have heard 28:17?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 28:10-22 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) Give the four parts of God's promise in 28:15 and the conditions of Jacob's vow in 28:20-21. (c) Say what the Hebrew *bô* in 28:12 could mean, and which way the Septuagint takes it.
2. **Five-minute outline.** Sequence and key words (Context); the promise and the vow (Literary Design); the four readings of the ladder (Positions 1); John 1:51 and the "on him" question (Positions 2); the "if" (Positions 3); the tenth (Positions 4); the four connections (prompt 5).
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: the Christological reading of the ladder brings John 1:51 back into Genesis, when Genesis says only that angels went up and down. Check first what Calvin actually claims ("the exposition will not be forced"). A second candidate, running the other way: the providence and midrashic readings must account for John 1:51's use of the dream's own Greek wording. Does any reading in Positions (1) explain why John chose this scene?
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) The passage does not say what the ladder is or stands for; the LORD's words (28:13-15) explain the promise, not the image. (b) It does not settle whether the angels were "on it" or "on him"; only the Septuagint's pronoun chooses. (c) It does not record Jacob paying the tenth, or command anyone else to give one.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** The reviewed connections attached to this lesson:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| John 1:51 | allusion | strong | The Greek shares "the angels of God," "ascending and descending" in that order, and "on" with the Septuagint of 28:12; there is no formula and no name, and the Son of Man stands where the ladder was |
| Genesis 31:13 | explicit_reference | explicit | "I am the God of Bethel, where you anointed the pillar and made a solemn vow to Me" names the place, the act and the vow |

Choose one row and defend it, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` for a candidate. Four candidates this lesson chose not to author:

- **John 1:51 as `type_antitype`.** No "as ... so" statement, and readers differ on whether the Son of Man answers to the ladder or to Jacob (Positions 2). `no_warrant_yet`.
- **Hebrews 13:5, "Never will I leave you, never will I forsake you."** Bengel lists Genesis 28:15, but Ellicott says the words are "clearly taken from Deuteronomy 31:6" and Meyer calls Genesis 28:15 "Less corresponding in point of expression."
