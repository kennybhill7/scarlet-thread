Genesis 22 opens with a sentence that tells the reader something Abraham does not know: "Some time later God tested Abraham" (22:1). Then comes the command: "Take your son ... your only son Isaac, whom you love ... Offer him there as a burnt offering" (22:2). Jews call the chapter the *Akedah*, "the binding," and read it on Rosh Hashanah. Christians have read it alongside the death of Jesus. Readers of every kind have asked how a good God could give such a command. This lesson reads the text closely, reports what named Jewish and Christian readers have concluded, and does not settle their disagreements. One thing is not left open. **None of the traditions reported in this lesson treats this chapter as permission for anyone to harm a child, or as a reason to obey a voice that demands harm.** What each says on that point, and where to get help, is set out at the end of Positions. Every BSB quotation below, and in the five connection files, was checked by script against this app's own corpus (see Sources and Limits).

## Context

**What happens, in order (22:1-19).** God calls, and Abraham answers, "Here I am" (22:1). The command names "the land of Moriah" (22:2). Abraham sets out early with two servants and Isaac (22:3), and on the third day sees the place (22:4). He tells the servants, "The boy and I will go over there to worship, and then we will return to you" (22:5). Isaac carries the wood; Abraham carries "the fire and the sacrificial knife" (22:6). Isaac asks, "where is the lamb for the burnt offering?" (22:7), and Abraham answers, "God Himself will provide the lamb for the burnt offering, my son" (22:8). Abraham builds the altar, binds Isaac (22:9), and "took the knife to slaughter his son" (22:10). The angel of the LORD calls from heaven (22:11): "Do not lay a hand on the boy" (22:12). A ram caught in a thicket is offered "in place of his son" (22:13), and Abraham names the place "The LORD Will Provide" (22:14). The angel calls "a second time" with an oath and renews the promise (22:15-18), and Abraham returns to Beersheba (22:19).

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens puts Genesis 12 through Malachi in stage 5 (machine-readable `chapters: ["1.12"]`). The chapter before has already said, "through Isaac your offspring will be reckoned" (21:12), the promise that makes the command so hard. Nahor's genealogy (22:20-24) and Sarah's death (23:1-2) follow; this lesson notes them only where readers connect them to the binding.

**Key words, read as observations.** Each count below was made by this lesson's own script over the app's BSB text.

- **"Tested" (22:1).** The narrator tells the reader at the start what kind of event this is. In this app's BSB, 22:1 is the first verse in Genesis with "tested" (the others are 42:15 and 42:16, Joseph testing his brothers). Readers explain the word differently. Ramban says that from the side of the one tried it is a trial, but from God's side "it is a command that the one being tested should bring forth the matter from the potential into actuality so that he may be rewarded for a good deed, not for a good thought alone." Calvin says God "made trial of Abraham's faith, not in the usual manner, but by drawing him into a contest with his own word."
- **"Here I am" (22:1, 22:7, 22:11).** Abraham gives the same two-word answer three times: to God, to his son ("Here I am, my son"), and to the angel. In this app's BSB, 22:1 is the first verse in the Bible with "Here I am." Genesis has it in nine verses (22:1, 22:7, 22:11, 24:13, 24:43, 27:1, 27:18, 31:11, 46:2). The Cambridge Bible calls 22:1 "The first instance of this response." Rashi calls it "the answer of the pious: it is an expression of meekness and readiness."
- **"Take your son ... your only son Isaac, whom you love" (22:2).** The command names Isaac in four steps. MacLaren calls it "a beautiful and instructive climax," in which "Each word heightens the greatness of the sacrifice demanded." Rashi, following Genesis Rabbah 55:7, turns the steps into a dialogue in which Abraham answers, "I have two sons," and "I love both of them," until God says, "even Isaac." The word "love" first appears in this app's BSB at 22:2: no earlier verse contains any form of it. Bible Hub's concordance likewise lists 22:2 as the first occurrence of the Hebrew verb *ʾahab*. The phrase "only son" (Hebrew *yachid*, which Brown-Driver-Briggs glosses "only one, especially of an only son") appears at 22:2, 22:12 and 22:16. Ishmael is alive (21:20), so readers explain the word. The Cambridge Bible: "Ishmael is here disregarded ... He is no longer considered one of the true family." The Septuagint renders the word as "the beloved" (τὸν ἀγαπητόν), not "only."
- **Moriah (22:2).** In this app's BSB, "Moriah" occurs in only two verses: here, and 2 Chronicles 3:1, where "Solomon began to build the house of the LORD in Jerusalem on Mount Moriah." Rashi ("This is Jerusalem"), Ibn Ezra ("The temple was later built on this mountain") and Calvin ("it is by no means doubtful," he says, that the temple was later built there) identify the two. The Cambridge Bible doubts it. It holds that "the land of Moriah" is a region, and that the Chronicler recorded "the popular tradition of his own time," so that the name was "popularly, although inaccurately, assigned to the Temple hill"; "Probably the name is irrecoverable by conjecture." The Septuagint has "the high land" (τὴν γῆν τὴν ὑψηλήν).
- **"God Himself will provide" (22:8, 22:14).** Bible Hub's interlinear gives the verb behind "provide" in 22:8 and 22:14 as *raʾah* (Strong's 7200), the ordinary verb "to see," and the Septuagint keeps that sense: "God will see (ὄψεται) for himself a sheep," and the place is called "The Lord saw" (Κύριος ἴδεν). Both English renderings are the lesson's own; Brenton has "God will provide himself a sheep" and "The Lord hath seen." Rashi hears a second meaning in 22:8: God will choose a lamb, "and if there will be no lamb for a burnt offering, then, בני MY SON will be the offering." Note that the Septuagint's word in 22:8 is πρόβατον ("sheep"). That matters below, when John 1:29 is tested.
- **"Together" (22:6, 22:8, 22:19).** "the two of them walked on together" comes twice, once before and once after Isaac's question. Bible Hub's interlinear shows the same Hebrew word, *yaḥdāw*, in all three verses. In 22:19 "they ... set out together," but Isaac is not named. In this app's BSB, Isaac's name appears in 22:2, 22:3, 22:6, 22:7 and 22:9, and not again in the passage. Genesis Rabbah 56:11 asks, "and where was Isaac?" Ibn Ezra answers that "Isaac is not mentioned because he was under Abraham's care," and calls the claim that Isaac was killed and revived a contradiction of Scripture.
- **"in place of his son" (22:13).** Genesis Rabbah 56:9 and Rashi both read the phrase as Abraham's prayer that each act done to the ram be counted "as though my son is being slain" (Rashi). The ram is where the chapter's later use in Jewish worship begins (below).

**How old was Isaac, and did he agree?** The text gives no age. It says only that he could carry the wood (22:6) and that he was bound (22:9). Readers fill the gap differently.

- Genesis Rabbah 56:8 has Isaac ask to be bound: "Father, I am a young man ... Therefore, bind me very well." It then asks, "Is a person capable of binding a thirty-seven- year-old [variant reading: a twenty-six-year-old] without his consent?"
- Ibn Ezra accepts the age of thirty-seven only "If this be a tradition." He adds that "from a strictly logical point of view it is unacceptable," because Scripture would then have praised Isaac's self-offering. He concludes "that Isaac was close to thirteen years old and that Abraham overpowered him and bound him against his will."
- Calvin thinks Isaac "was then of middle age" and "voluntarily surrendered himself."
- Gill reports that Josephus gives "twenty five years" (Josephus not read here).

**The Akedah in Jewish worship.** In Rosh Hashanah 16a, Rabbi Abbahu explains the ram's-horn shofar of the New Year: God says, "Sound a blast before Me with a shofar made from a ram's horn, so that I will remember for you the binding of Isaac, son of Abraham, in whose stead a ram was sacrificed, and I will ascribe it to you as if you had bound yourselves before Me." Megillah 31a records that on the second day of Rosh Hashanah "they read 'And God tested Abraham' (Genesis 22), in order to mention the merit of the binding of Isaac on the day of God's judgment." Genesis Rabbah 56:10 gives Abraham's prayer at the place: "when Isaac's descendants encounter transgressions and wicked deeds, may this binding [of Isaac] be remembered for them." These are a living tradition's own readings, and this lesson reports them in their own terms. They are not a stage on the way to Christian ones.

## Literary Design

**Three calls, one answer.** God calls "Abraham!" (22:1), Isaac calls "My father!" (22:7), the angel calls "Abraham, Abraham!" (22:11), and each time Abraham answers "Here I am." The first call starts the journey, the second asks a question not yet answerable, and the third stops the knife. Rashi says the doubled name in 22:11 "is an expression of affection," and Ibn Ezra says it "expresses urgency." The pattern is checkable in any translation. What it signifies is the reader's judgment.

**What the narrator tells and withholds.** The reader knows from 22:1 that this is a test; Abraham, as far as the text says, does not. The text records nothing of what Abraham feels: no reply to the command, no word on the journey, and two sentences readers have long found ambiguous ("we will return," 22:5; "God Himself will provide," 22:8). Rashi says Abraham "prophesied that they would both return." Calvin calls the statement possibly "confusedly" spoken "in a matter so obscure." Ibn Ezra reports others who say "Abraham intended to return with Isaac's bones." The silence is a fact about the text. Each position below fills it in its own way.

**A second call from heaven (22:15-18).** Only the second speech has an oath. The Cambridge Bible, working with the documentary hypothesis, assigns 22:1-19 to the source it calls E "but Genesis 22:15-18 are, probably, from another source, possibly R." Readers who do not use that hypothesis read the second call as the climax of one narrative. This lesson reports the proposal and does not adopt it.

## Positions

Three questions follow. Each is reported through named readers in their own words. None is adjudicated, and nothing in any position should be read as this lesson's own conclusion.

**1. Why would God command this, and how should a reader weigh it?** The text gives the facts every reading must explain: God "tested" Abraham (22:1); the command was to "offer him there as a burnt offering" (22:2); Abraham "took the knife to slaughter his son" (22:10); the angel stopped him and said, "now I know that you fear God" (22:12); and the oath praises Abraham "because you have done this" (22:16). Elsewhere the Torah forbids child sacrifice ("Let no one be found among you who sacrifices his son or daughter in the fire," Deuteronomy 18:10; also Leviticus 18:21 and Deuteronomy 12:31), and Jeremiah calls the burning of children "something I never commanded, nor did it even enter My mind" (Jeremiah 7:31). Five readings follow.

- **A test of faith and obedience.** Hebrews 11:17-19 reads the chapter as faith: "By faith Abraham, when he was tested, offered up Isaac on the altar ... Abraham reasoned that God could raise the dead." Calvin follows Hebrews. For him the hardest part of the trial was not the loss of a son but that Abraham "conceives God to contradict Himself and His own word," and he "by hope, reconciled the command with the promise." He applies it with a limit: "The Lord, indeed, is so indulgent to our infirmity, that he does not thus severely and sharply try our faith." Augustine (*City of God* 16.32) holds both points together: "Of course Abraham could never believe that God delighted in human sacrifices; yet when the divine commandment thundered, it was to be obeyed, not disputed." The Catechism of the Catholic Church (§2572) calls the command "a final stage in the purification of his faith" and says that "the father of believers is conformed to the likeness of the Father who will not spare his own Son."
- **A test never meant to be completed.** Rashi on 22:2 reads the verb "offer him" as "bring him up." He says God "did not say, 'Slay him', because the Holy One, blessed be He, did not desire that he should slay him, but he told him to bring him up to the mountain." Genesis Rabbah 56:8 puts this in God's mouth when Abraham protests: "when I said to you: 'Take you your son,' I did not say: 'Slaughter him,' but rather, 'take him up.' ... You have taken him up and fulfilled My words, now take him down." The Babylonian Talmud (Taanit 4a) reads Jeremiah 19:5's three phrases about child burning ("which I did not command, and I did not speak, nor did it come into My heart") as three cases. The first is "the son of Mesha, king of Moab" (2 Kings 3:27), the second "Jephthah," and the third is "'Nor did it come into my heart,' this is referring to Isaac, son of Abraham." The Steinsaltz translation adds its own explanation, which is not part of the Talmud's text: "there was no intent in God's heart that he should actually do so; it was merely a test." Rashi on Jeremiah 7:31 says the same: "when I did speak to Abraham to slaughter his son, it did not enter My mind that he slaughter, but only to make his righteousness known." Some Christian readers reach a related view. Keil and Delitzsch write that "The issue shows, that God did not desire the sacrifice of Isaac by slaying and burning him upon the altar, but his complete surrender." MacLaren writes that "this command was given with the purpose that it should not be obeyed in fact, if the willingness to obey it was proved."
- **A story set against child sacrifice.** The Cambridge Bible's Special Note on the Sacrifice of Isaac holds that "The prohibition of the sacrifice of Isaac proclaimed a fundamental contrast between the God of Abraham and the gods of the nations round about," so that "human sacrifice could not any longer be thought to be acceptable to Him." On 22:12 it says that "Jehovah proclaims to Abraham and to his descendants His abhorrence of the cruelty of child sacrifice." It treats the ram as "the memorial of the abrogation of an inhuman system," and adds that "True sacrifice implied the surrender of self, not of another." The Pulpit Commentary reports that "The voice from heaven has been accepted as evidence of God's rejection of human sacrifices (Lange, Murphy)," then adds its own qualification, "only that is not assigned as the reason for Isaac's deliverance." Readers of this kind point to the Torah's bans and to Jeremiah, cited above.
- **A "teleological suspension of the ethical" (Kierkegaard).** Søren Kierkegaard's *Fear and Trembling* (1843), written under the pseudonym Johannes de silentio, refuses to soften the story. In Lee Hollander's 1923 translation: "the ethical expression for what Abraham did is that he wanted to murder Isaac; the religious, that he wanted to sacrifice him. But precisely in this contradiction is contained the fear which may well rob one of one's sleep. And yet Abraham were not Abraham without this fear." The book's first "Problem" asks whether there is a "teleological suspension of the ethical." Hollander's selection does not include that section, so the phrase is reported here through the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (John Lippitt and C. Stephen Evans, 2023). That entry also cautions that the pseudonymous author "claims to be an outsider to 'faith'," that taking his views as Kierkegaard's own "is a dangerous move," and that the book "should warn us off readings which suggest that the central message of the text—and of faith—is simply the importance of being obedient to God."
- **Jewish readings that register the cost.** Several Jewish sources dwell on the horror of the command without softening it. In Genesis Rabbah 56:4, Samael, the accuser, tells Abraham on the road, "Tomorrow He will tell you that you are a shedder of blood because you shed the blood of your son." In 56:8, Abraham calls the commands "nothing short of bewildering," and "his eyes were emitting tears and the tears were falling into Isaac's eyes." In 56:10 he says, "I suppressed my mercy to fulfill Your will." Others turn to Sarah. Genesis 23:2 follows the binding with her death, and Genesis Rabbah 58:5 explains the placement in Rabbi Yosei's words: "Sarah died as a result of grief over that incident. That is why the binding [of Isaac] is juxtaposed to 'Sarah's lifetime was.'" Rashi on 23:2 says that when she heard "that her son had been made ready for sacrifice and had almost been sacrificed," she "received a great shock ... and she died." Ibn Ezra, as reported above, thinks Isaac was bound "against his will." These readings do not reject the chapter. They keep its cost in view.

**Objections each reading faces.** Each reading has been answered from another. Against the faith-and-obedience reading, the Cambridge Bible notes that many readers "have been unable to reconcile the command to offer Isaac for a burnt-offering with their conception of a good God," and Kierkegaard asks whether, if faith is set aside, "only the brutal fact remains that Abraham wanted to murder Isaac." Against the never-meant-to-be-completed reading, Ibn Ezra reports Jewish teachers who held that "God's intention was not that Abraham actually sacrifice Isaac," and answers that "In reality all questions are removed by Scripture's first stating that God did prove Abraham." Keil adds that the command "was given in such a form, that Abraham could not understand it in any other way than as requiring an outward burnt-offering." Against the reading set against child sacrifice, MacLaren writes, "Nor can we regard the condemnation of such sacrifices as the main object of the incident," and Keil insists the command "was not a thought suggested by the sight of the human sacrifices of the Canaanites." Against Kierkegaard's reading, the *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry itself warns, as quoted above, against taking the pseudonym's paradoxes as Kierkegaard's own views. Against the readings that dwell on the cost, the text is silent about Abraham's feelings and about Sarah, and Ibn Ezra calls the midrash's age of thirty-seven "unacceptable" on logical grounds.

**2. Is Isaac a figure of Christ?** Robert Wilken (*Theological Studies*, 1976) observes that "Neither Hebrews nor James takes the Akedah to be a type of Jesus." He also notes that Romans 8:32 "may reflect the terminology of Genesis 22" and that John 3:16's "gave His only son" "may also be reminiscent of Genesis 22," but that "Paul offers no typology of Isaac and Jesus." Early Christian writers developed the comparison themselves.

- **Isaac and the wood, Christ and the cross.** Barnabas (chapter 7) speaks of "the type established in Isaac when he was offered upon the altar." Melito of Sardis (second century, fragments preserved in a Genesis catena) writes that Christ "carried the cross on His shoulders when He was led up to the hill to be slain, as was Isaac by his father," and adds at once, "But Christ suffered, and Isaac did not suffer: for he was but a type of Him who should suffer." Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 4.5.4) writes that believers, "taking up the cross as Isaac did the wood," follow Christ. Tertullian, in the treatise translated as *An Answer to the Jews* (chapter 10), says that Isaac, "himself bearing his own wood, was even at that early period pointing to Christ's death." Augustine (*City of God* 16.32) writes that "Isaac also himself carried to the place of sacrifice the wood on which he was to be offered up, just as the Lord Himself carried His own cross."
- **The ram and the crown of thorns.** Melito: "this bush represented the cross, and that place Jerusalem, and the lamb the Lord bound for slaughter." Augustine asks of the ram caught by its horns, "What, then, did he represent but Jesus, who, before He was offered up, was crowned with thorns by the Jews?" Calvin, a Christian reader, stops short of this. On 22:13 he writes, "I am not ignorant that more subtle allegories may be elicited; but I do not see on what foundation they rest."
- **The Jewish setting of the early readings.** Wilken argues that Melito wrote with a strong Jewish community in view at Sardis, one that read the Akedah as the ground of God's mercy to Israel. His "suggestion is that Melito was aware of the Jewish interpretation and that his own interpretation is an attempt to rescue Isaac for the Christians." Barnabas, he notes, "is an anti-Jewish tract which reflects greater familiarity with Jewish traditions than other early Christian writers." Tertullian's treatise is polemical too. A Jewish midrash uses a related image: Genesis Rabbah 56:3 says Isaac carried the wood "like someone bearing his own gibbet on his shoulder." The Hebrew word there, *tzelubo*, is from the root Jastrow defines "to hang, impale." The image of Isaac carrying the wood of his own execution belongs to both traditions. The sources read for this lesson do not settle which use came first. This lesson reports the Christian type as Christian readers' own synthesis. It does not present Jewish readings as a shadow that Christian readings replace, or endorse the polemical frame some early writers gave the type. The traditions above are reports of how communities read a shared text, not verdicts on one another.

**3. What the traditions reported here say about imitation and about "voices."** This is not another reading of the chapter, but what the sources say about taking the story as a pattern.

- **Jewish sources.** Beyond the Torah's bans and the readings of Taanit 4a and Rashi above, a variant reading in Genesis Rabbah 56:8 tells a parable of a king who invited a friend's son to his table, and the friend came "knife in hand": "Did I say to bring him up in order to eat him?" It applies Jeremiah 19:5, "which never entered my mind," to Isaac.
- **Catholic teaching.** The Catechism (§2258) teaches that "no one can under any circumstance claim for himself the right directly to destroy an innocent human being," and (§2261) that the law forbidding the murder of the innocent "obliges each and everyone, always and everywhere."
- **Reformation and later Protestant commentators.** Calvin writes that "the faithful are not in such haste to obey God, as to allow a foolish credulity to carry them away, in whatever direction the breath of a doubtful vision may blow." His next sentence turns back to Abraham: "But when it was once clear to Abraham, that he was called by God, he testified, by this answer, his prompt desire to yield obedience." Where Calvin does propose Abraham's example "for our imitation," on 22:8, it is to leave "the event to God" when "all avenues seem closed." Gill, on Jeremiah 7:31, writes, "The instance of Abraham offering up Isaac will not justify it." Ellicott writes that "human sacrifice was abhorrent to the nature of Jehovah," that "no subjective proof would be sufficient," and that "In after times many an Israelite was moved by deep religious fanaticism to give his firstborn ... (Micah 6:7); but instead of peace it brought only a deeper condemnation upon his soul. Had Abraham been moved only by an internal and subjective impulse, his conduct would have deserved and met with similar condemnation." He then defends Abraham's own case on two grounds: that "he became convinced that the command came from outside himself," and the act's "typical value." Without that value, he adds, "it would have been difficult for us to reconcile to our consciences a command which might have seemed, indirectly at least, to have authorised human sacrifices."
- **Augustine.** "Of course Abraham could never believe that God delighted in human sacrifices."
- **Kierkegaard.** *Fear and Trembling* imagines a preacher's listener who "goes home and wishes to do as did Abraham" with his own son, and has the preacher cry, "what devil possessed thee to wish to murder thy son?" The book's point is that an easy sermon had invited the misunderstanding: "the preacher's mistake was merely not knowing what he was talking about." Without faith, he says, "only the brutal fact remains that Abraham wanted to murder Isaac—which is easy for everybody to imitate who has not the faith."

*If any part of this is close to you.* No tradition or reader reported here takes this chapter as telling anyone today to harm a child or themselves. If you, or someone you know, feel that God or a voice is telling you to hurt a child or yourself, please treat it as an emergency and get help now: call your local emergency number (911 in the United States), or call or text 988 (the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline in the US). If you are worried about a child's safety, the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline in the United States answers at any hour at 1-800-422-4453 (1-800-4-A-CHILD), by call or text. Elsewhere, contact local emergency or child-protection services. A doctor or counselor can help with frightening thoughts or voices, and having them is nothing to be ashamed of. A pastor, priest or rabbi who knows you can help you think about what this chapter means for your own faith. A study note cannot do either.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, as a model of the shape, not an answer key. It deliberately does not build a bridge from the command, the binding or the knife; Positions (3) shows why.

**Passage element.** Genesis 22:13-14. After the ram is offered, "Abraham called that place The LORD Will Provide. So to this day it is said, 'On the mountain of the LORD it will be provided.'"

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** The narrator ties a place name to a saying still used "to this day"; for a first audience, the saying likely carried the memory of a rescue into later speech. Calvin reads the naming the same way, as Abraham leaving "a monument of his gratitude to posterity." Rashi, following the Targum, reads it differently: God "will choose and select for Himself this place to make His Shechinah reside in it." The text itself states no moral.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Naming and marking a time of deliverance, so that others can remember it, is a different act from moving on in silence.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a moment of rescue after fear, and a way of remembering it that others can share. Not carried forward: the command, the test, the altar, the sacrifice, the oath, and any idea that a person should put someone in danger in order to be rescued.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed. The words "The LORD Will Provide" name what Abraham said of that place. Nothing here promises any reader a rescue on the same terms.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family life. A family has come through a frightening month, such as a child's hospital stay that ended well. Afterward, life rushes back and the experience is not mentioned again.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: thanksgiving and remembrance. One faithful response is to mark the day in a way the family will keep: a meal on its anniversary, a note in a family Bible, or a word of thanks to the people who helped.

**Cautions.** The bridge does not suggest that frightening events are tests sent to a family, or that every crisis ends in rescue. Some do not, and grief is no failure of faith. If the experience left lasting fear or trauma, a counselor or pastor, not a study note, is the right next step. The bridge is an analogy the learner is testing and may find does not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. The reader learns in 22:1 that this is a test, and Abraham, as far as the text says, does not. How does knowing that change the way you read 22:2-10? Would the chapter mean something different if 22:1 were missing?
2. Abraham says "Here I am" to God, to Isaac and to the angel. Is the same answer doing the same work each time? What in the wording of 22:7-8 would you point to?
3. Isaac's name disappears after 22:9, and 22:19 says "they ... set out together" without naming him. Is that a clue, or is it ordinary Hebrew storytelling? What would you need to know to decide?
4. Which reading in Positions (1) stays closest to the words of 22:1, 22:12 and 22:16, and which depends most on texts outside the chapter? Does that settle anything?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 22:1-19 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) Give the three calls and Abraham's three answers. (c) State, in one sentence, what none of the traditions in this lesson draws from the chapter, and why that boundary matters (Positions 3).
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) sequence and key words (Context); (1:00-2:30) the five readings and their objections (Positions 1); (2:30-3:30) the Akedah in Jewish worship and the Christian figure of Isaac (Context; Positions 2); (3:30-4:30) the five connections and their grades (prompt 5); (4:30-5:00) the safety boundary (Positions 3).
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: the never-meant-to-be-completed reading lets the reader off too easily, since Abraham "took the knife to slaughter his son" and was praised "because you have done this." Check Rashi's reading of the verb before answering. A second candidate, running the other way: the faith-and-obedience reading takes too little account of the cost the midrash records, Sarah's death and Isaac's tears. What in the text would you point to on each side?
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) The chapter does not state Isaac's age or whether he agreed. Every answer in Context comes from later readers. (b) The chapter does not say that Moriah is the later temple mount. That identification rests on 2 Chronicles 3:1 and on readers, and the Cambridge Bible disputes it. (c) The chapter does not offer Abraham's act as a pattern for anyone else. Every tradition reported in Positions (3) says so in its own words.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** The reviewed connections attached to this lesson:

Choose one row and defend it, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` for a candidate. Five candidates this lesson chose not to author:

- **"your only son Isaac, whom you love" and John 3:16.** John has τὸν μονογενῆ ("only"), as Hebrews 11:17 does of Isaac; the Septuagint of 22:2 has τὸν ἀγαπητόν ("beloved"). Ellicott, the Cambridge Bible and Wilken hear Genesis 22 behind John 3:16, but no formula or shared Septuagint wording ties them: `no_warrant_yet`, or at most a plausible motif a named scholar would need to argue.
- **"God Himself will provide the lamb" (22:8) and John 1:29.** The Septuagint word in 22:8 is πρόβατον ("sheep"); John's is ἀμνός. The Cambridge Bible on John 1:29 takes the Lamb to be "the Lamb of Isaiah 53," with "an indirect allusion to the Paschal Lamb" possible, and cites Genesis 22:8 only for "of God": `no_warrant_yet`.
- **The wood on Isaac and the cross.** This comes from Barnabas, Melito, Irenaeus, Tertullian and Augustine (Positions 2), not from a New Testament text, and Wilken finds no New Testament typology of Isaac.
- **Moriah and the temple site.** 2 Chronicles 3:1 uses the name, and Rashi, Ibn Ezra and Calvin accept the identification, but the Cambridge Bible calls it "popularly, although inaccurately" applied. A reader could argue it as a plausible parallel.
- **Galatians 3:16, "and to your seed."** The existing row conn-gen12-seed-galatians3-16 reports C. John Collins's view that Paul "was using Genesis 22:18." But the exact Septuagint words Paul quotes, καὶ τῷ σπέρματί σου ("and to your seed"), occur in 13:15 and 17:8, while 22:18 reads ἐν τῷ σπέρματί σου ("in your seed"). Collins argues from grammar, not that wording, so this lesson does not add a second row.
