Genesis 25:1-18 is easy to skim. It has a second marriage told in one verse, two lists of names, a will, a death notice, a burial, and a second death notice in nearly the same words as the first. Yet these eighteen verses close the story of Abraham that began at 12:1, and they are where readers have asked some of their oldest questions. Who was Keturah, the wife who appears from nowhere (25:1)? What does it mean that Abraham "was gathered to his people" (25:8), a phrase that appears here for the first time in this app's BSB? Why does the text name Ishmael beside Isaac at the grave (25:9), when the last time the two boys appeared together, one of them was being sent away (21:14)? And what should a reader do with the chapter's last clause about Ishmael's descendants, "they lived in hostility toward all their brothers" (25:18), which has a long history of being used against a living people? This lesson reports what named readers, Jewish and Christian, have concluded on each question, without choosing among them. It also follows the chapter's names and phrases forward, to Isaac's death, to Isaiah's vision of the nations, and to Hebrews 11, and grades each link by its evidence. Every quotation attributed to the BSB below, here and in the five connection files, was printed from this app's shipped corpus by a script before drafting, and a second script confirmed each quoted fragment against the real verse text (see Sources and Limits).

## Context

**What happens, in order (25:1-18).** "Now Abraham had taken another wife, named Keturah" (25:1). She bears six sons, among them Midian, and the text lists some of their sons and grandsons, ending, "All these were descendants of Keturah" (25:2-4). "Abraham left everything he owned to Isaac" (25:5), but "while he was still alive, Abraham gave gifts to the sons of his concubines and sent them away from his son Isaac to the land of the east" (25:6). "Abraham lived a total of 175 years. And at a ripe old age he breathed his last and died, old and contented, and was gathered to his people" (25:7-8). "His sons Isaac and Ishmael buried him in the cave of Machpelah," in the field Abraham had bought from the Hittites, where "Abraham was buried there with his wife Sarah" (25:9-10). "After Abraham's death, God blessed his son Isaac, who lived near Beer-lahai-roi" (25:11). Then a new heading: "This is the account of Abraham's son Ishmael" (25:12). His twelve sons are named "in the order of their birth" (25:13-15), called "twelve princes of their tribes" (25:16). "Ishmael lived a total of 137 years. Then he breathed his last and died, and was gathered to his people" (25:17). His descendants "settled from Havilah to Shur... And they lived in hostility toward all their brothers" (25:18).

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens files Genesis 12 through Malachi under stage 5 (`chapters: ["1.12"]`). The cave of Machpelah was bought in Genesis 23, the unit on Sarah's death, and that purchase is not retold here. Ishmael's birth and the misuse of 16:12 are treated in the Genesis 16 lesson, and his expulsion in the Genesis 21 lesson. This lesson takes up only what chapter 25 adds. The next heading, "This is the account of Abraham's son Isaac" (25:19), begins the story of Isaac's sons.

**The ages, worked out from the text.** Abraham was seventy-five when he left Haran (12:4) and a hundred when Isaac was born (21:5), and Isaac was sixty when the twins were born (25:26). So Abraham lived a hundred years in Canaan (Matthew Henry: "just one hundred years after he came to Canaan") and was alive until Esau and Jacob were fifteen. Jamieson-Fausset-Brown note this: "he lived till Jacob and Esau were fifteen years." Abraham's death is told before their birth (25:24-26), so the chapter does not run strictly in time order. Ellicott calculates that at the burial "Isaac was now seventy-five years of age, and Ishmael eighty-nine."

**Key words, read as observations.**

- **"Another wife" and "concubines."** The BSB's "had taken another wife" renders a Hebrew verb Ellicott translates literally as "And Abraham added and took a wife." Keturah is called "wife" here (25:1), but 25:6 speaks of "the sons of his concubines," plural, and 1 Chronicles 1:32 calls her "Keturah, Abraham's concubine." The Cambridge Bible says her name means "incense" and that "The domestic tradition in these verses preserves the recollection of an early connexion between the ancestors of Israel and the clans or tribes on the borders of the North Arabian desert." Who she was is Positions (1).
- **Midian.** By script, Midian's first BSB occurrence is 25:2, and the name occurs in 66 verses in all. Midian is where Moses flees from Pharaoh and sits "beside a well" (Exodus 2:15). His father-in-law is "Jethro, the priest of Midian" (Exodus 18:1). Midianite traders appear in the Joseph story beside the Ishmaelites (Genesis 37:28), and Midian is Israel's enemy in Judges 6-8. The Cambridge Bible on Isaiah 60:6 sums up the range: traders in Genesis 37, in the Sinai peninsula in Exodus 2 and 18, east of the Jordan in Numbers 22, 25 and 31, and invaders "in Judges 6-8." Exodus never says that the Midian of Moses descends from the Midian of 25:2; that link is genealogical inference.
- **"The land of the east" (25:6).** The Cambridge Bible lists nine passages where "the children of the east" appear "as the nomad occupants" of the Syro-Arabian desert. Keil calls it "Arabia in the widest sense."
- **"Breathed his last... and was gathered to his people" (25:8).** By script over the BSB, 25:8 is the first verse in the BSB with either phrase. "Gathered to his people" occurs in seven verses, and with "their," "your" or "my" in eleven, all between Genesis 25:8 and Judges 2:10. Within Genesis, "breathed his last" and "gathered to" stand together in four verses only: Abraham (25:8), Ishmael (25:17), Isaac (35:29) and Jacob (49:33). The Hebrew of 25:8 ends *vayye'asef el-ammav* (checked on Sefaria); Barnes renders it "Gathered to his peoples."
- **"At a ripe old age... old and contented" (25:8).** "Ripe old age" occurs in four BSB verses, and the first is God's promise at 15:15, "be buried at a ripe old age." The Hebrew phrase is the same in both verses. "Old and contented" occurs only here in the BSB. Isaac dies "old and full of years" (35:29). Benson, Poole and Gill all note that the Hebrew has only "full" or "satisfied," without "of years"; the BSB's "contented" gives that sense.
- **Ishmael's name returns.** On a check of the Hebrew text of Genesis 17-25, the name Ishmael does not occur between 17:26, his circumcision, and 25:9, the burial. In chapter 21 he is "the boy" and "the slave woman's son" (the Genesis 21 lesson notes the BSB supplies the name at 21:11). By script over the BSB, Hagar's name occurs for the last time in Genesis at 25:12.
- **"Twelve rulers" and "twelve princes."** God promised of Ishmael, "He will become the father of twelve rulers" (17:20). The list closes "twelve princes of their tribes" (25:16). The two English words render one Hebrew phrase, the same in both verses. These are the only two BSB verses with either phrase.
- **"Settled... in hostility" (25:18).** Ellicott notes that the verb is literally "he fell—that is, his lot fell; he settled there." The phrase the BSB renders "in hostility toward" is the one it uses at 16:12, though the Hebrew verbs differ there. How it should be rendered, and how it has been used, is Positions (4).

## Literary Design

**Two death notices in the same words.** Abraham's notice (25:7-8) and Ishmael's (25:17) share their shape: a total of years, "breathed his last and died," "was gathered to his people." The repetition can be checked. Rashi and Ramban both report a rabbinic rule built on it, which Positions (2) takes up.

**Two "accounts," the side line first.** "This is the account of" introduces Ishmael's line at 25:12 and Isaac's at 25:19, back to back; by script the heading occurs in ten verses of Genesis. Keil says that "according to the settled plan of Genesis, this account precedes the history of Isaac," and Calvin that "what Moses has said concerning the Ishmaelites was incidental." The same order recurs with Esau's account (36:1) before Jacob's (37:2). Whether the order ranks the lines or only clears the side line first is a reader's judgment.

## Positions

Four questions follow. Each is reported through named readers in their own words. None is adjudicated, and nothing in a position's statement should be read as this lesson's own conclusion.

**1. Who was Keturah, and when did Abraham marry her?** The text gives a name, six sons and the word "wife" (25:1). Readers divide on two questions.

- **Keturah was Hagar.** In Genesis Rabbah 61:4, "Rav said: This is Hagar," explaining the name as "perfumed [mekuteret] with mitzvot and good deeds." The Targum known as Pseudo-Jonathan renders 25:1, "her name was Keturah; she is Hagar, who had been bound to him from the beginning." Rashi follows Genesis Rabbah: "This is Hagar. She was named Keturah because her deeds were as beautiful (sweet) as incense." On 25:6 he answers the plural "concubines" from the spelling: the word "is written deficient... because he had only one concubine, Hagar, who is identical with Keturah."
- **Keturah was another woman.** The same midrash records the objection at once: Rabbi Neḥemya answers Rav that the verse says Abraham took "another" wife, and he presses the plural of 25:6. Ibn Ezra: "Keturah is not to be identified with Hagar... because Scripture explicitly states, But unto the sons of the concubines." Ramban holds that Abraham took Keturah "unto himself as a wife," perhaps "from the daughters of Canaan." Radak says "Keturah was not a concubine." Calvin says the identification "is immediately refuted in the context." Ellicott notes that 1 Chronicles 1:32 distinguishes Keturah's children from "Hagar's one son, Ishmael." He adds that Hagar would by now "have passed the period of life when she could bear six sons."
- **When.** Calvin thinks it "most probable" that Abraham married Keturah while Sarah was alive, which he calls an "adulterous connection," and that Moses placed it late out of order. Barnes holds the opposite: "It seems to appear in the narrative in the order of time." Keil argues that there is "no firm ground" for the later date, which is "merely an inference" from the order of telling, though he grants that his own main argument against it "is not decisive." The Pulpit Commentary calls it "impossible to decide."

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the identification: Ibn Ezra, Calvin and Ellicott all cite the plural of 25:6, and Ellicott cites 1 Chronicles 1:32. Against the second reading: Rav answers in Genesis Rabbah 61:4 that "another" means only that Abraham remarried "on the basis of a divine command." The midrash, through Rashi, reads the plural as a deficient spelling. On timing: the early date must explain the order of the narrative, as Barnes argues. The late date must explain Abraham's age, which Calvin presses from Paul, who says "that the body of the father himself was dead" (Romans 4:19; the BSB reads "the decrepitness of his body").

**2. What does "gathered to his people" say about death (25:8)?** The phrase is distinct, in this verse, from both dying and burial: "breathed his last and died... and was gathered to his people," and only then, "His sons... buried him." Readers differ on what that distinction carries.

- **Continued personal existence, with the dead who went before.** Keil: the phrase "is constantly distinguished from departing this life and being buried, denotes the reunion in Sheol with friends who have gone before, and therefore presupposes faith in the personal continuance of a man after death." Barnes: "To be gathered is not to cease to exist, but to continue existing in another sphere." Calvin "gladly" accepts that "the state of our future life" is "pointed out in this form of expression." He adds a condition: "provided we do not restrict it... to the faithful only," for "mankind are associated together in death as well as in life." Matthew Poole, by contrast, reads it of "his godly progenitors... the congregation of the just in heaven" and notes that "this phrase is used of none but good men." Alexander MacLaren, more cautiously, finds in it "some dim hint of a future life." He points out that Abraham's own "'people' were buried in Mesopotamia, and his grave was far away from theirs."
- **The family dead in Sheol, the same as going to one's fathers.** The Cambridge Bible: "'His people' evidently has no local significance; but means those of his own family already dead, and now in Sheôl, 'the under-world' of departed spirits." It sees "no difference" between this phrase and the wording of 15:15 and 47:30 (in the BSB, "go to your fathers" and "lie down with my fathers").
- **Jewish readers: the soul's return, an idiom, or the death of the righteous.** Ibn Ezra gives two views without choosing. "Some say that this refers to the soul of life," which, in his words, "When it separates from the body it returns to its source." "Others say that and was gathered to his people is a mere idiom," since "One who follows in the footsteps of his ancestors is said, as it were, to be joining them." Radak reads it "to join the members of his family who had preceded him in death," and adds, "The Torah applies this expression to one's family regardless of whether they were righteous people or sinners." The Talmud (Bava Batra 16b) asks whether a death described with "expire" (*gevia*) "is the death of the righteous," and answers that the rule holds when *gevia* and *asifa* ("gathering") are used together. Ramban reports the same rule.
- **A Catholic statement on the righteous dead before Christ.** The Catechism of the Catholic Church does not comment on Genesis 25:8. It speaks of "the abode of the dead" as "Sheol in Hebrew," where "all the dead, whether evil or righteous," awaited the Redeemer, "which does not mean that their lot is identical," and of "these holy souls, who awaited their Savior in Abraham's bosom" (CCC 633).

**Objections each reading faces.** Against reading continued existence into the phrase, Ibn Ezra reports others who take it as "a mere idiom." Against the idiom reading, MacLaren argues that "It is then the equivalent neither of death nor of burial," since Abraham's own people lay in Mesopotamia. The Sheol readings (Keil, the Cambridge Bible, and the Catechism's account of the righteous dead) and the heaven readings answer each other: Gill reads the phrase as "the admission of his soul into the heavenly state," and Poole as "the congregation of the just in heaven." Against "of none but good men," Gill, on Ishmael's death, says "these phrases are used both of good and bad men," as do Radak and Calvin.

The New Testament adds two texts readers bring here, and neither quotes Genesis 25. Jesus argues for the resurrection from the words "I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob," adding, "He is not the God of the dead, but of the living" (Matthew 22:32). The commentators read for this lesson identify the quotation as Exodus 3:6, the burning bush, not Genesis. Hebrews says of Abraham and the others named with him, "All these people died in faith, without having received the things they were promised" (Hebrews 11:13). Keil and the Pulpit Commentary read 25:8 beside that verse (see the connections).

**If you are grieving.** This chapter is about a death and a burial, and it may touch a loss of your own. The readings above report how others have understood a phrase about death. They do not tell anyone how to grieve or what to believe about someone they have lost. A pastor, rabbi, priest or other faith leader you trust, or a grief counselor, is the right person to walk with you, not a study note. **3. What does Ishmael's presence at the burial show (25:9)?** The text says only, "His sons Isaac and Ishmael buried him," Isaac named first. Readers draw very different weight from that.

- **Repentance.** The Talmud (Bava Batra 16b) reports Rabbi Yoḥanan: "Ishmael repented in the lifetime of his father, as it is stated: 'And Isaac and Ishmael, his sons, buried him.'" Asked whether the order of the names might be "the order of their wisdom," it answers from Isaac's burial: "And Esau and Jacob, his sons, buried him" (35:29), where the elder comes first. So in 25:9 Ishmael let Isaac go first, "and since he made him his leader, learn from it that he repented." Rashi on 25:9: "From this we gather that Ishmael repented of his evil ways... and yielded the precedence to Isaac." Pseudo-Jonathan writes the repentance into its rendering of 25:8 and of 25:17 ("he was coverted in repentance," in Etheridge's spelling). Of the midrashic reasons for recording Ishmael's years, Ramban calls "The correct one among them" the reason "that he was righteous, a man of repentance."
- **Kindness or reconciliation, without a verdict on his life.** Genesis Rabbah 62:2 reads the order as courtesy: "here the maidservant's son... accorded honor to the mistress's son." Yet the same midrash, at 62:4-5, calls Ishmael "the wicked man." It says his years are recorded "because he came from the far reaches of the desert to perform kindness for his father." Ellicott pictures "the two old men, with their enmity long over," meeting as friends at the grave. Gill thinks "the difference was now made up, and they were reconciled, at least they agreed in this act." The Pulpit Commentary quotes Wordsworth: "Funerals of parents are reconciliations of children." Christopher Heard, as Croteau and Yates report him, says the two "are never in conflict as adults and join to bury their father in Gen 25:9."
- **Ordinary duty, nearness, or standing.** Calvin: Ishmael "was not utterly alienated from his father, because he performed the office of a son," and he did so rather than Keturah's sons "as being nearer." Keil: Ishmael, "although excluded from the blessings of the covenant, was acknowledged by God as the son of Abraham by a distinct blessing (Genesis 17:20)." Radak: "they were more honoured than his other sons." The Cambridge Bible offers a source-critical explanation: the expulsion of chapter 21 belongs to a different strand (E) and "was not apparently included in the narrative of P."

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the repentance reading, Gill notes that the death phrases of 25:17 "are used both of good and bad men," and Genesis Rabbah itself still calls Ishmael "wicked." Against reconciliation, the text records no meeting, words or feelings, only a joint act. Against the "duty" reading, the Talmud argues that the order of names is not accidental, since 35:29 orders by birth. What can be checked: both sons are named, Isaac first. The burial is at Machpelah, where "Abraham was buried there with his wife Sarah" (25:10). The text states no motive.

**4. Ishmael's twelve princes, 25:18, and how the chapter has been used against Arab peoples.** The Cambridge Bible calls the genealogy "The fulfilment of Genesis 17:20," and Calvin reads it as showing "the veracity of God," for "it was no common gift of God that Ishmael should have twelve sons." Then comes 25:18. Here the misuse documented in the Genesis 16 lesson has to be named again.

- **The rendering.** The BSB's "they lived in hostility toward all their brothers" is one choice. Ellicott, Poole, Gill and Keil take the verb as "settled" or "his lot fell," and read the phrase as "in the presence of" or "to the east of." Keil adds "the subordinate idea of keeping by force the place you have taken." Croteau and Yates note that the preposition "does have an adversarial sense in other passages (e.g., Job 1:11; 6:28)," which is why some English translations read conflict here. Rashi gives two readings, one plain ("he dwelt") and one from Genesis Rabbah 62:5 that contrasts Ishmael's settled life while Abraham lived with "he fell" after his death. Croteau and Yates argue that the phrase "has this spatial meaning in Gen 25:18 with reference to Ishmael's descendants" and that the parallel description in 16:12 "says nothing about violence or hostility."
- **The misuse.** Croteau and Yates name as an "urban legend" the claim that Arabs, "as the descendants of Ishmael, have inherited his rebellious" and violent qualities from 16:12. The legend holds that violent jihadism shows "the spirit of Ishmael" still at work among Arab peoples. The Genesis 16 lesson documents older commentaries that applied 16:12 to Arabs or Bedouin of their own day in derogatory terms. Classic notes also map this chapter's names onto peoples of their own day, not always in derogatory terms: Gill on Isaiah 60:7 takes Nebaioth and Kedar to "design the Turks and Saracens," in hope of their conversion.
- **What answered it.** Croteau and Yates, from within the Christian tradition, write that "the genealogical relationship between Ishmael and present-day Arabs is complex to begin with" and that 16:12 "does not characterize Arab peoples as violent." They quote Heard: "Although Christians commonly claim that Isaac's and Ishmael's descendants have fought constantly since Isaac's birth, it is hard to sustain that claim with biblical evidence."
- **What Genesis itself says and does not say.** Within Genesis, Ishmael's line is blessed and numbered (17:20; 25:16). The brothers bury their father together (25:9). Esau marries "Mahalath... daughter of Abraham's son Ishmael" (28:9), and Ishmaelite traders carry Joseph to Egypt (37:28). Genesis 25:18 describes where the Ishmaelite tribes of Genesis's own world settled and how they stood toward their kin. It names no later or living people and describes no people's character, and readers who apply it that way bring that to the verse.

A reader for whom these questions touch family, faith or community should take them to a rabbi, imam, pastor or teacher in their own tradition. A study note can report the readings but cannot settle them.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, a model of the shape and not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step, and a different element, situation or response would be just as legitimate. It deliberately does not build a bridge from the burial or from 25:8, since a worked "application" of a death could be heard as instruction for someone's grief.

**Passage element.** Genesis 25:5-6. "Abraham left everything he owned to Isaac. But while he was still alive, Abraham gave gifts to the sons of his concubines and sent them away."

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text stresses timing, "while he was still alive." Several readers say why. Sforno: "He did not wish to rely on a will, so he distributed his property while he was alive." Radak: "in order that they should not interfere with him after his death and could not quarrel with him about the inheritance." Inference: a first audience familiar with inheritance disputes would likely have heard a father settling matters in his own lifetime so that his sons would not fight over them.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Settling what you can about your estate while you are alive, and saying it plainly, can spare those you leave a quarrel after you are gone.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a parent with several heirs, property to divide, and the timing of the decision. Not carried forward: the covenant inheritance given to Isaac, the sending away of the other sons, concubinage, and any idea that one child should be favored. Calvin himself says the pattern does not carry over: "If any person should now select one of his sons as his heir, to the exclusion of the others, he would do them an injury."

**Application class and promise scope.** Principle carried by analogy, not a command. Promise scope: none claimed.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family. A widowed father in his seventies owns a house and some savings and has three adult children, one of whom has been his caregiver. He has no will.

**Response type and faithful response.** Settling a matter in good time and saying it openly. He meets with an attorney, makes a will, and tells all three children together what it says and why, while he can still answer their questions.

**Cautions.** Wills and estates are legal matters, and the rules differ by place. An attorney or other qualified person, not a study note, is the right next step. If an older person is being pressured, cut off or financially exploited by anyone, contact adult protective services or the police where you live. The bridge is an analogy the learner is testing, and it may not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. Genesis 25:8 separates dying, being "gathered to his people," and burial. Positions (2) reports readers who find continued existence in the middle term, readers who find an idiom, and readers who find a mark of the righteous. What in the verse's own order, and in 25:17's use of the same words for Ishmael, would you weigh first?
2. The Talmud reads the order "Isaac and Ishmael" (25:9) against "Esau and Jacob" (35:29) and finds repentance. Others find courtesy, reconciliation or simple duty. Does the order of two names in a list count as evidence about a person's heart? What else would you want to know?
3. The BSB's "in hostility toward" at 25:18 is one of several named renderings. How should a reader handle a verse whose translation has been used against a people, when the translation itself is disputed?
