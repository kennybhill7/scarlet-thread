Genesis 9:1-17 turns the flood narrative's ending resolution (8:21-22's "never again will I destroy all living creatures as I have done," BSB) into a formal, worded covenant with a stated sign. The unit falls into three recognizable movements: a renewed creation blessing and mandate that repeats part of Genesis 1:28's own wording and adds real new content (9:1-7); the covenant itself, stated in nearly identical terms twice and addressed, by its own repeated wording, to "every living creature" and not only to Noah's family (9:8-11); and the rainbow as that covenant's sign (9:12-17). Two pieces of this unit carry long-running, named disagreement this lesson reports rather than settles: whether 9:6's "by man his blood will be shed" delegates the punishment of murder to human courts or reserves it to God's own hand, and whether 9:9's "I now establish My covenant" and 9:17's "the covenant that I have established" -- both the identical Hebrew idiom, *heqim berith*, this curriculum's Genesis 6:9-7:24 lesson already flagged as disputed at 6:18 -- confirm an arrangement already standing or inaugurate a new one with Noah specifically, a question this chapter's own three uses of that idiom give fresh, checkable data for without settling. Every quotation attributed to the BSB below was checked directly against the shipped corpus (`web/public/bible/BSB/1.json`, and the corresponding files for Isaiah, Acts, and Romans) before this lesson was drafted, not drawn from memory or another translation -- see Sources and Limits for the exact method.

## Context

**What happens, in order (9:1-17).** God blesses Noah and his sons and repeats the command, "Be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth" (9:1). Every living creature will now fear and dread humanity, delivered into human hands (9:2). Every moving creature is now given as food, alongside the plants already given (9:3), with one exception stated immediately afterward: meat must not be eaten "with its lifeblood still in it" (9:4). God states He will require a reckoning for the taking of any life, animal or human, and specifically for human life taken by another human being: "by man his blood will be shed; for in His own image God has made mankind" (9:5-6). The blessing is restated a second time, closing this movement where it opened (9:7). God then addresses Noah and his sons with a formal statement, "I now establish My covenant with you and your descendants after you," extended explicitly to "every living creature that was with you" (9:8-10). The covenant's content is stated: never again will a flood destroy all life or the earth (9:11). The sign of this covenant is given -- the rainbow, set in the clouds, serving as something God Himself will see and be reminded by whenever clouds form (9:12-16). The unit closes with God restating to Noah that this is the sign of the established covenant between Himself and every creature on earth (9:17). This is a plain retelling of what happens, in the text's own sequence; what each piece means is the rest of this lesson's work.

**Where this passage sits.** This passage picks up exactly where this curriculum's Genesis 8:1-22 lesson left off: that lesson's own Questions to Carry named 9:11-17's covenant and rainbow sign as "the next passage's own territory," left open on purpose. This app's eleven-stage lens (`content/lens/eleven-stages.json`) names stage 3 "Genesis 6-9 -- The World Judged and Destroyed," with a machine-readable `chapters` field of `["1.6"]` placing all of chapters 6 through 9 inside one stage; this lesson's passage is filed there for the same reason the two preceding Genesis lessons already were. This lesson's own boundary ends at 9:17, just before 9:18-19 names Noah's three sons again and turns toward the incident of Noah's drunkenness and Ham's line (9:20-27) -- a different passage's own territory, not this lesson's.

**Key words, read as observations.**

- **הֵקִים** (*heqim*, the Hifil of קום, *qum*, "to rise, establish, confirm," Strong's 6965) is the verb behind "I now establish My covenant" at 9:9, 9:11, and 9:17 alike -- checked directly against each verse's own interlinear text, all three uses are the identical Hifil form of the same root. This is the same idiom, *heqim berith*, already named as disputed at Genesis 6:18 by this curriculum's Genesis 6:9-7:24 lesson: whether the idiom there means *making* a new covenant or *confirming* one already standing is a live, named grammatical dispute that lesson reported through Peter Gentry and Stephen Wellum (confirmatory-only, as Brian Collins represents their argument) against Collins's own reply (citing Leupold, Hamilton, Stuart, and Longman for a making sense). This chapter's three further uses of the identical idiom, never once the ordinary "cut a covenant" language (below), are a fresh, directly checkable data point for that same dispute, developed in Positions below without resolving it.
- **כָּרַת** (*karat*, "to cut," Strong's 3772) is the verb behind "the LORD made a covenant with Abram" at Genesis 15:18 (*karat berith*, "cut a covenant") -- the ordinary Biblical Hebrew idiom for inaugurating a covenant, named for the ancient practice of cutting sacrificial animals. Genesis 9 never uses this verb for the covenant with Noah; its only appearance in the chapter is at 9:11's own "Never again will all life be cut off" (*yikkareth*, the same root used in its ordinary "cut off/destroy" sense, not of covenant-making). That 9:11 uses *karat* for destruction in the very same verse where it uses *heqim* for the covenant is a checkable fact about this chapter's own vocabulary; it does not by itself argue for either side of the *heqim berith* dispute, and is named here only so that coincidence is not mistaken for evidence either way.
- **אוֹת** (*'oth*, "sign, mark, token," Strong's 226, about 79 occurrences) is the word behind "This is the sign of the covenant" (9:12, 17) and "it will be a sign of the covenant" (9:13). The identical word, independently checked here against both passages' own interlinear text, is the word translated "mark" in "the LORD placed a mark on Cain" (4:15) -- a connection this curriculum's own Genesis 4 lesson already named as a Question to Carry rather than a settled finding, and developed further below (Literary Design and the reviewed connection to 4:15).
- **נֶפֶשׁ חַיָּה** (*nephesh chayyah*, "living creature/soul") and its shorter form underlie "every living creature" (9:10, 9:12, 9:15, 9:16) and "every creature" (9:17) -- the covenant's own repeated address, checkable by counting: of the nine verses from 9:9 to 9:17, six name "every living creature" or an equivalent phrase explicitly, naming animals as party to, or at minimum beneficiary of, the same covenant stated to Noah, not merely mentioned alongside it once in passing.
- **צֶלֶם** (*tselem*, "image," Strong's 6754) is the word behind "in His own image God has made mankind" (9:6), reusing the identical word behind "God created man in His own image" (1:27). That 9:6 restates 1:27's own vocabulary as the stated reason a human life may not be taken without consequence is directly checkable against both verses; what that restatement is doing -- grounding a specific penalty, or simply recalling humanity's dignity in general terms -- is part of the dispute in Positions (1) below.

## Literary Design

**An inclusio bracketing the renewed blessing (9:1-7).** The unit opens, "Be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth" (9:1), and closes, "be fruitful and multiply; spread out across the earth and multiply upon it" (9:7) -- the same core command stated at both ends of the same seven-verse unit, with the unit's genuinely new content (the "fear and dread" mechanism of dominion in 9:2, the expanded diet and its blood exception in 9:3-4, and the human-justice clause in 9:5-6) held inside that frame. This bracketing is directly checkable by reading the unit's first and last verses side by side; whether it is a deliberate authorial technique or simply how a short unit that opens and closes on its own main topic tends to read is not settled by noticing the pattern itself, the same caution this curriculum's Genesis 8 lesson already applied to its own Noah-name wordplay observation.

**A renewed mandate, restated and revised, checkable clause by clause against Genesis 1:28-30.** "Be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth" (9:1) reproduces 1:28's opening command nearly word for word. What follows diverges in ways a reader can check directly rather than assume: 1:28's "rule over" (*radah*) the animals becomes 9:2's "fear and dread of you will fall on every living creature... delivered into your hand" -- a different stated mechanism for the same basic dominion, not a repetition of 1:28's own verb. 1:29-30 gave "every seed-bearing plant" to both humans and animals for food and said nothing about animal flesh; 9:3 now gives "Everything that lives and moves," immediately qualified by 9:4's blood exception, which 1:29-30 had no occasion to state at all. And 9:6's rationale for protecting human life, "for in His own image God has made mankind," reuses 1:27's own word for "image" (*tselem*) to ground a specific penalty clause that Genesis 1 itself never attaches to that word. Matthew Henry's commentary on this chapter reads the dominion change as a moral one: "man in innocence ruled by love, fallen man rules by fear" -- his own gloss on the checkable textual difference between "rule over" and "fear and dread," reported here as his tradition's reading of what the difference means, not as this lesson's own settled claim about it.

**The covenant's own address, repeated past what a single mention would require.** Beyond the "every living creature" count already given under Key Words, 9:9-17's own grammar keeps returning to a three-way address -- God, Noah's human line, and the animals -- in verse after verse, rather than naming the animals once and then speaking only to Noah for the rest of the unit. Whether this repetition is best read as making the animals a covenant party in some meaningful sense, or as simply extending the covenant's promised benefit to them without making them a party to any obligation (the covenant states no obligation on anyone at all, human or animal -- see Positions), is a real question this lesson's own research did not find a clean, named scholarly division on; it is left in Questions to Carry rather than forced into a Positions entry that would overstate how settled, or how disputed, the question actually is.

## Positions

Three separate questions. Each is reported through its own named positions, from their own stated reasoning; none is adjudicated, and no position's statement here should be read as this lesson's own conclusion.

**1. Does Genesis 9:5-6 institute, or merely describe, human capital punishment for murder -- and who is the stated subject of "by man his blood will be shed"?** This is a live, current, and specifically named scholarly dispute, not a settled question with one side simply not paying attention to the grammar.

- **The traditional reading: God delegates the punishment of murder to human courts, grounding civil government itself.** Keil and Delitzsch's commentary states that because murder "destroyed the image of God in man," the infliction of its punishment "was not to be left to the caprice of individuals, but belonged to those alone who represent the authority and majesty of God," and calls this clause a foundation "for all civil government." The aggregated classic commentaries on 9:6 report the same reading from several named interpreters: Gill's Exposition has the magistrate "pass the sentence of death upon him" once "the fact being clearly proved by witnesses"; Barnes' Notes calls the clause "the formal institution of civil government," with "the civil sword... committed to the charge of man"; Jamieson, Fausset, and Brown read it as attaching "an awful criminality" to the destruction of "the life of every man, even the poorest and humblest." Matthew Henry's commentary grounds the same reading in the image-of-God clause directly: "such remains of God's image are still even upon fallen man as that he who unjustly kills a man defaces the image of God." Timothy Finlay, replying to the next position below, reports that Ibn Ezra argues Genesis 9:6 obligates the descendants of Noah to execute a murderer, and that Targum Onqelos renders the clause, "He who sheds the blood of a human before witnesses, through sentence of the judges shall his blood be shed" -- both reported here as Finlay represents them, not independently read in their own primary texts for this lesson.
- **A minority, current reading: God alone reserves the right to avenge, with no delegation to human courts at all.** Nathan Mastnjak (Notre Dame Seminary), writing in 2023, argues the Hebrew preposition *b-* before "man" (*ba'adam*) is grammatically capable of expressing price or exchange rather than only agency, so that the clause can be read "for the human shall that person's blood be shed" rather than "by the hand of a human." On that reading, Mastnjak states plainly, "who will shed the blood of the murderer? God himself," concluding that "Genesis 9 gives no provision for humans carrying out the execution of murderers," and comparing the verse's own principle to Deuteronomy 32:35's "vengeance is mine." Checked directly against BibleHub's own interlinear text of 9:6, the preposition *b-* on "man" (בָּאָדָם) is real and is grammatically the same prefixed form used elsewhere for plain agency ("by/with") -- the grammatical fork itself is independently checkable, even though which sense this specific use carries is exactly what is disputed.

Both readings agree on the words themselves; they disagree on what the preposition is doing with them, and on whether the surrounding clause assumes a human judicial process the text never names outright (Numbers 35:30's later law, by contrast, does name witnesses and judges explicitly) or deliberately withholds one. This lesson does not decide between them.

**2. Is Genesis 9's own threefold use of *heqim berith* (9:9, 9:11, 9:17) evidence that 6:18's identical idiom confirms a covenant already standing, or only evidence that this chapter is being internally consistent with 6:18's earlier wording?** This curriculum's Genesis 6:9-7:24 lesson already reported the underlying dispute: Peter Gentry and Stephen Wellum (as Brian Collins represents their argument) read *heqim berith* as used throughout the Hebrew Bible only to confirm a covenant already in force, concluding that 6:18 confirms an unstated "covenant of creation"; Collins himself disputes that the idiom is exclusively confirmatory, citing H. C. Leupold, Victor Hamilton, Douglas Stuart, and Tremper Longman III for a reading in which the idiom can denote making a covenant that did not exist before. Genesis 9:9, 9:11, and 9:17 restate the identical idiom three more times, and never once use *karat berith* (the ordinary "cut a covenant" language used of Abraham at 15:18) for the covenant with Noah -- a fresh, directly checkable fact this chapter alone supplies, which neither side of the 6:18 dispute had yet needed to address from this chapter's own text. Whether three further uses of the same idiom, all within the passage the idiom's own covenant describes, make the confirmatory reading more likely (consistent use of a specialized, confirmatory-only idiom across the unit it governs) or do no independent work at all (a chapter restating its own earlier chapter's word choice is not new evidence about what that word choice originally meant) is not settled by the repetition itself, and no source consulted for this lesson addresses this chapter's own threefold repetition directly -- this lesson states the new data point and the two ways it could cut, without resolving which one it actually supports.

**3. Does the Jerusalem Council's instruction to Gentile believers to abstain "from blood" (Acts 15:20, 29) draw on Genesis 9:4's Noahic restriction specifically, or rest on a different basis?** Named interpreters, old and current, disagree about exactly this.

- **A direct echo of the Noahic restriction.** The aggregated classic commentaries on Acts 15:20 report Barnes' Notes citing "Genesis 9:4-6" directly for the blood prohibition's origin, stating the reason "existed before any ceremonial law"; Gill's Exposition likewise traces the restriction to the same chapter; and the Cambridge Bible states plainly, "the prohibition of blood was made as soon as animal food was given to men (Genesis 9:4), and it was frequently enforced in the Mosaic law." F. F. Bruce's commentary on Acts, reported here through a secondary citation (Bruce's own volume was not independently read for this lesson), states of the "blood" and "strangled" clauses together that "the prohibition goes back to Gen. 9:4."
- **A different basis: pagan cultic practice, not the Noahic text.** Ben Witherington's commentary on Acts, reported through the same secondary citation (not independently read for this lesson), is reported as reading the "blood" clause instead as a warning connected to blood consumed during pagan temple worship, and as reading the underlying word as potentially carrying the sense "murdering" -- a reading the secondary source reports as not itself anchored to Genesis 9 at all.

Both readings are reading the identical two words ("blood," "strangled") in the identical two verses; neither the Acts text itself nor any source consulted for this lesson supplies a citation formula connecting Acts 15 back to Genesis 9 the way, for example, Hebrews 11:7 names Noah directly. This lesson reports both named readings without ranking them.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key: the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step, and a different passage element, situation, or response would be equally legitimate.

**Passage element.** Genesis 9:12-16. The sign of the covenant is stated, by the text's own repeated wording, to function first and most explicitly for God's own remembering, not for Noah's family's reassurance: "I will remember My covenant" (9:15) and "I will see it and remember the everlasting covenant" (9:16).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: across 9:12-16, the stated function of the sign is given from God's own side three times -- "the sign of the covenant I am making" (9:12), "I will remember" (9:15), "I will see it and remember" (9:16) -- and the text never once states that the rainbow is meant to remind Noah's descendants of anything in particular. Inference: for an audience familiar with ordinary arrangements where a weaker party keeps some token to appeal to a stronger party's memory, hearing the stronger party commit, in His own stated words, to being reminded by a visible sign He Himself set in place would have marked this as a self-binding commitment, not a device the human side needed in order to trust the promise. The Context section above supplies what the text itself records; nothing in it lets this lesson say more about what first hearers took from the sign's own stated function than that.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** A commitment anchored to a visible, external reminder that the one making the commitment keeps or regularly encounters is a different, and in some ways a stronger, kind of commitment than one resting only on the promiser's own unaided memory or goodwill -- and naming that pattern honestly, when it is genuinely present, is a discipline a person can build on purpose.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a commitment is made; a visible, external marker is deliberately set up as that commitment's own reminder; the marker is kept by, or regularly encountered by, the party making the commitment, not held only by the party receiving it. Not carried forward: the cosmic scale of the promise, the specific instrument (a literal rainbow God Himself places in the sky), and any claim that a modern reminder device carries anything like the weight of a divine covenant.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed; nothing in 9:12-16 is treated here as a promise that any modern self-imposed reminder will function as reliably as God's own stated commitment, or that setting one up repairs trust by itself.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family. A parent who has broken a promise to a teenager before wants the next promise believed; rather than asking only to be trusted on his word, the parent writes the specific commitment down, dates it, and puts a copy somewhere both of them will actually see on a regular basis.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: a self-binding commitment paired with its own external reminder (not mere promise-making, and not a public performance aimed at an audience). One faithful response is to state the commitment in writing, choose a reminder both people will actually encounter on a recurring basis, and treat noticing that reminder as a prompt to check the commitment's status honestly, rather than treating the written note itself as proof the commitment has already been kept.

**Cautions.** This example does not claim a written note carries anything like a divine covenant's own weight, and it should not be used to suggest that any broken trust is fully repaired by a device like this on its own -- the teenager's doubt, in this analogy, is a reasonable response to a real pattern, not a defect a reminder note corrects by itself. The passage is not a family-communication text; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing, and the learner may find it does not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Genesis 9:1-7 repeats 1:28's blessing almost word for word but visibly changes 1:28's "rule over" into 9:2's "fear and dread... delivered into your hand," and adds both the blood-restricted diet and the human-justice clause, with no stated reason given for why these particular additions belong together in one speech. What in the text itself -- not Matthew Henry's or any other tradition's gloss -- would help decide whether these are one coherent revision of the original mandate or several separable concerns simply gathered into a single speech?
2. Positions (1) reports two sharply different readings of who carries out 9:6's "by man his blood will be shed" -- human courts, on the traditional reading, or God's own hand alone, on Mastnjak's reading -- turning on how the single preposition *b-* before "man" is read. What would a reader need to find, from the Hebrew Bible's own other uses of that same prefixed preposition in similarly life-and-death contexts, to judge between an agency sense ("by") and a price/exchange sense ("in place of"), rather than choosing whichever reading sounds more familiar?
3. Genesis 9:9, 9:11, and 9:17 all use the same idiom as 6:18 (*heqim berith*), and never once use the "cut a covenant" (*karat berith*) language used for Abraham at 15:18. Positions (2) reports that this idiom's own range is itself disputed. Does three uses of the idiom within one chapter make the "confirms a standing arrangement" reading of 6:18 more likely than one use there alone did, or does it only show that this chapter is staying consistent with 6:18's own earlier wording, without adding independent evidence either way?
4. This covenant's own stated content is a promise that water will never again destroy all life or the whole earth (9:11, 15) -- not a promise that no flood, storm, or regional disaster will ever happen again anywhere. Isaiah 54:9-10 (the reviewed connection, below) recalls this promise centuries later, applying it to a very different situation (exile, not flood). Reading only Genesis 9 and Isaiah 54 together, does Isaiah's own use of the promise stay inside the scope Genesis 9:11 and 9:15 actually state, or does it extend the promise further than those two verses themselves claim?

I am guilty of anything worthy of death, I do not refuse to die" -- a real narrative instance of someone accepting a government's legitimate life-and-death authority, but one that shares no vocabulary at all with Genesis 9:5-6 ("blood," "image," or any equivalent), only the general theme of a government's power over life and death that Romans 13 already carries more directly.
