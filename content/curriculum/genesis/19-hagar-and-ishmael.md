Genesis 16 is a short chapter about three people in one household, and it is hard to read. Sarai cannot have children. She gives her Egyptian maidservant, Hagar, to Abram "to be his wife" (16:3). Hagar conceives, and the household breaks apart: Hagar comes to "despise her mistress" (16:4), Sarai blames Abram, Abram hands Hagar back to Sarai's control, and "Sarai treated Hagar so harshly that she fled from her" (16:6). In the desert, "the angel of the LORD" finds her. This is the first time that figure appears in Scripture. He sends her back, and he also gives her a promise, a son, and a name for the son. Then Hagar does something nobody in the Bible has done before her: she gives the LORD a name. The chapter does not ask a reader to admire everyone in it, and it does not tell the reader what to think of anyone in it. This lesson tries to do both of the things the chapter makes hard. It keeps every person's cost in view: Sarai's years of childlessness and the humiliation she names, Abram's passivity, and above all Hagar's position as an enslaved foreign woman with no say in what was done with her body. And it reports what named readers, Jewish and Christian, ancient and modern, have concluded about all three, without choosing among them. Every quotation attributed to the BSB below, here and in this lesson's four connection files, was printed from this app's own shipped corpus by a script before drafting, and a second script confirmed each quoted fragment is a literal substring of the real verse text. See Sources and Limits.

## Context

**What happens, in order (16:1-16).** "Now Abram's wife Sarai had borne him no children, but she had an Egyptian maidservant named Hagar" (16:1). Sarai speaks first: "the LORD has prevented me from bearing children. Please go to my maidservant; perhaps I can build a family by her." The narrator adds, "And Abram listened to the voice of Sarai" (16:2). The timing is stated: "after he had lived in Canaan for ten years," Sarai "gave her to Abram to be his wife" (16:3). Hagar conceives, and once she knows she is pregnant, "she began to despise her mistress" (16:4). Sarai turns on Abram: "May the wrong done to me be upon you! I delivered my servant into your arms... May the LORD judge between you and me" (16:5). Abram answers, "your servant is in your hands. Do whatever you want with her," and Hagar flees (16:6). "Now the angel of the LORD found Hagar by a spring of water in the desert," on the road to Shur (16:7). He addresses her as "Hagar, servant of Sarai" and asks, "where have you come from, and where are you going?" She answers only the first question: "I am running away from my mistress Sarai" (16:8). He tells her, "Return to your mistress and submit to her authority" (16:9). He promises, "I will greatly multiply your offspring so that they will be too numerous to count" (16:10). He names the child she is carrying: "you have conceived and will bear a son. And you shall name him Ishmael, for the LORD has heard your cry of affliction" (16:11). And he describes the son: "He will be a wild donkey of a man, and his hand will be against everyone, and everyone's hand against him; he will live in hostility toward all his brothers" (16:12). "So Hagar gave this name to the LORD who had spoken to her," and the name is "You are the God who sees me." The well is named for the event, "Beer-lahai-roi" (16:13-14). Hagar bears the son, and "Abram gave the name Ishmael to the son she had borne." He is eighty-six (16:15-16). That is the text's own sequence. What it means is the rest of this lesson's work.

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens (`content/lens/eleven-stages.json`) puts all of Genesis 12 through Malachi in stage 5, "Genesis 12 -- Malachi -- Israel: The Twelve Tribes" (machine-readable `chapters: ["1.12"]`). The chapter right before this one ends with a promise to Abram that has not yet come true: "one who comes from your own body will be your heir" (15:4). Notice what that promise names and what it leaves out. It names Abram's body. It does not name Sarai. Genesis 16 is the household's attempt to act in that gap. The story of Hagar and Ishmael continues past this lesson. In 17:18-21 Abraham asks for Ishmael to "live under Your blessing," and God answers about both sons. In 21:8-21 Hagar and Ishmael are sent away and "the angel of God" meets her in the wilderness a second time. Those chapters belong to later units. This lesson quotes them only where its own passage points to them.

**The custom behind 16:2, and what the comparison can and cannot show.** Sarai's plan, a barren wife giving her own slave woman to her husband so that the children can count as hers, has known parallels in ancient Near Eastern legal texts. The Cambridge Bible on 16:2 cites the Code of Hammurabi (through S. A. Cook): "the wife might give her husband a maidservant." For much of the twentieth century, though, the parallel most often cited came from Nuzi, a mid-second-millennium BC site east of the Tigris. M. J. Selman's peer-reviewed Tyndale Biblical Archaeology Lecture (*Tyndale Bulletin* 27, 1976, read directly in full) lays out both the claim and its critics. He quotes E. A. Speiser's claim that some patriarchal customs "cannot otherwise be explained either from the local background or from any but Hurrian sources." He then reports that "more fundamental questions have been asked by the recent contributions of T. L. Thompson and J. van Seters," who wrote, respectively, *The Historicity of the Patriarchal Narratives* (1974) and *Abraham in History and Tradition* (1975). On this custom in particular, Selman notes that the one Nuzi tablet usually cited (HSS 5 67) "is the sole example of this custom in Nuzi." He adds that "there are examples of the same practice in cuneiform texts outside Nuzi," including the Laws of Hammurabi §144 and probably §163. He also disputes van Seters's proposal that a seventh-century Neo-Assyrian marriage contract is the closer parallel. That proposal comes from van Seters's earlier journal article on childlessness in Near Eastern law. Selman's footnote says van Seters's 1975 book "was published after the delivery of this lecture, and therefore has not been taken into account here." Two facts are reported here and nothing more. First, the custom is attested in ancient Near Eastern law, so Sarai's proposal was not unheard of in its world. Second, how far such parallels can date or authenticate the Genesis narratives is a real, named dispute. Thompson's and van Seters's own books were not read for this lesson; their positions are given as Selman summarizes them. Neither side of that scholarly argument settles anything about the moral questions in 16:4-6. On those, see Positions (2).

**Key words, read as observations.**

- **One Hebrew root, three English renderings: "treated... so harshly," "submit," "affliction."** At 16:6 the BSB says Sarai "treated Hagar so harshly." Ellicott's note on the verse says the Hebrew verb "is translated afflicted in Exodus 1:11." The Cambridge Bible calls it "the same word as that rendered 'afflict' (Genesis 15:13)." In the BSB, 15:13 reads "they will be enslaved and mistreated four hundred years." Shai Held's article on TheTorah.com, read directly, prints the Hebrew of 16:6, 16:9, and 16:11 and translates the root at 16:9 as "allow yourself to suffer." In other words, the same root sits behind Sarai's harsh treatment (16:6), the angel's "submit to her authority" (16:9), and "the LORD has heard your cry of affliction" (16:11). The BSB uses three different English words for one repeated Hebrew root. A reader working only in English would not see the repetition. What it is doing in the story is taken up in Positions (3) and in the Exodus connection below.
- **"Hand."** Abram says, "your servant is in your hands" (16:6). The angel says of Ishmael, "his hand will be against everyone, and everyone's hand against him" (16:12). In Held's translation, the Hebrew at 16:9 also has "under her hands," which the BSB renders as "submit to her authority." Held reads Ishmael's hand "against everyone" as "the inverse" of Hagar being sent back under Sarai's hand. That reading is Held's. The repetition of the word is checkable.
- **Who calls Hagar by name.** The narrator names her (16:1, 16:3, 16:4, 16:15). In this chapter's dialogue, Sarai calls her "my maidservant" (16:2) and "my servant" (16:5), and Abram calls her "your servant" (16:6). Neither of them speaks her name. The first character to say "Hagar" aloud is the angel: "Hagar, servant of Sarai" (16:8). Held makes this observation. He also reports Phyllis Trible's qualification that the added phrase "maid of Sarai" means Hagar "remains a servant in the vocabulary of the divine" (Trible's *Texts of Terror*, 1984, quoted by Held; the book was not read for this lesson). You can check the pattern against the BSB yourself.
- **"The angel of the LORD" appears here for the first time.** Checked by script against this app's own BSB text: the word "angel" does not occur anywhere in Genesis 1-15. Genesis 16:7 is the first verse in the corpus with "the angel of the LORD," and the phrase occurs 51 times across the whole BSB. Two classic commentators make the same point on 16:7: Gill ("This is the first time that mention is made of an angel in Scripture") and Benson ("the first mention we have in Scripture of an angel's appearance"). The text also does something worth noticing without yet deciding what it means. The angel speaks in the first person of what only God promises, "I will greatly multiply your offspring" (16:10). And the narrator calls the speaker "the LORD who had spoken to her" (16:13). Who this figure is, is Positions (1).
- **"You are the God who sees me" (16:13) and the name El Roi.** The BSB renders Hagar's name for God as "You are the God who sees me." Ellicott, the Cambridge Bible, and the Pulpit Commentary all give the underlying Hebrew as *El Roi*. They disagree about how to read it. The Pulpit Commentary names two groups. One reads "the God of my vision," that is, the God who lets himself be seen (it lists Gesenius, Keil, Kalisch, and others). The other reads "the God who sees all things, and therefore me" (it lists the Septuagint, the Vulgate, Calvin, Delitzsch, and others). The Cambridge Bible says the familiar "Thou God seest me" is, "with our present text, incorrect." Rashi, citing Genesis Rabbah 45:10, reads it as "One who sees the humiliation to which people are subjected by others." The second half of the verse, "Here I have seen the One who sees me!", is hard Hebrew. The Cambridge Bible says "the difficulty of the passage was realized at a very early time" and reports emendations proposed by Wellhausen and Ball. Most of the readings named above hear in it some form of surprise at having seen God and lived (compare Genesis 32:30, "I have seen God face to face, and yet my life was spared," and Judges 13:22, "for we have seen God!"). The BSB's rendering is one defensible choice among several, not the only possible translation.
- **Is Hagar the first person in Scripture to name God?** On this lesson's own check of the BSB, 16:13 is the first verse in canonical order where a person is said to give the LORD a name: "So Hagar gave this name to the LORD who had spoken to her." Before this, people "call upon the name of the LORD" (4:26; 12:8; 13:4), and Melchizedek blesses by "God Most High" (14:19), but no one is narrated as giving God a name. Shai Held goes further and calls Hagar "the only character in the Bible to do so." This lesson's own check covers Genesis 1-16 only, so "the only" is reported as Held's claim, not checked here. One complication should be named, too. Elsewhere people name places and altars with the LORD's name in them: "The LORD Will Provide" (Genesis 22:14), "The LORD Is My Banner" (Exodus 17:15), "The LORD Is Peace" (Judges 6:24). Whether those count depends on whether naming a place or altar for the LORD is the same act as naming the LORD.
- **Beer-lahai-roi recurs.** The well named for Hagar's encounter (16:14) comes back later in Genesis as the place where Isaac lives: "Isaac had just returned from Beer-lahai-roi" (24:62), and Isaac "lived near Beer-lahai-roi" (25:11). The text offers no comment on the fact that the son of promise settles at the well named for the slave woman's meeting with God. It is a checkable recurrence and nothing more is claimed for it here.

**Ishmael and Hagar in Islamic tradition, briefly.** Hagar (Arabic *Hājar*) is not named in the Qur'an, according to Wikipedia's survey article (a tertiary source), but she is honored in Islamic tradition as the mother of Ishmael (*Ismāʿīl*). A hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari (no. 3364, read directly) narrates that "Ishmael's mother" ran seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwa looking for water for her son. It reports the Prophet as saying, "This is the source of the tradition of the walking of people between them," the walk that is part of the Hajj and Umrah pilgrimages. That narration corresponds more closely to the wilderness scene of Genesis 21 than to Genesis 16. It is mentioned here so that a reader knows Hagar is a revered figure for a large living tradition. This lesson does not compare the accounts.

**A reading of 16:12 that must be named.** "He will be a wild donkey of a man" has a long history of being applied, in classic Christian commentaries, to Arab peoples as a whole, often in plainly derogatory terms. On the commentary page fetched for this lesson, Benson's commentary calls Ishmael's descendants "infamous for theft, pillage, robbery, revenge, and murder." The Pulpit Commentary speaks of "the turbulent and lawless character of the Bedouin Arabs and Saracens for upwards of thirty centuries." Gill writes that "such the wild Arabs are to this day." This lesson reports those sentences as a documented feature of how the verse was used, not as description. Four things are checkable against the text itself. (1) 16:12 speaks of one person, Ishmael, and his relations with "his brothers." It names no nation and no ethnicity. (2) Not every reader takes the image as an insult. Ellicott calls the wild ass "a very noble creature" marked by "an untamable fondness of liberty." Held quotes Jon Levenson: "The fierce independence of the Ishmaelites will vindicate the humiliating thralldom of their matriarch's life." (3) The last clause, which the BSB renders "in hostility toward all his brothers," is translated in at least three named ways in the Pulpit Commentary's own survey: "to the east of," "everywhere before the eyes of," and "independently of." So the hostility in that clause is partly a translator's decision. (4) A description of a man's freedom and conflicts, spoken by an angel to his mother, gives no textual warrant for a verdict about the character of a living people. Readers who apply it that way are bringing something to the verse that it does not say.

## Literary Design

**Hearing and seeing.** The chapter turns on two senses, and both are built into names. Ishmael's name is explained as "for the LORD has heard your cry of affliction" (16:11). The Cambridge Bible and Ellicott both gloss the name as "God heareth" (Ellicott: "That is, God heareth"). Hagar's name for God is "the God who sees me" (16:13). The well's name, Beer-lahai-roi, carries the same root of seeing (16:14). One name says God has heard. The other says God has seen. The person who receives both names, a pregnant runaway slave, is the person in the household nobody else in the chapter calls by name. That pattern can be checked in the names and their stated explanations. Whether the narrator built it deliberately is a separate question this lesson does not settle.

**Two questions, one answer.** The angel asks two things: "where have you come from, and where are you going?" (16:8). Hagar answers only the first. Held notes the half-answer and suggests Hagar "may be uncertain about having any future at all." That is a reading. What can be checked is that the text records no answer to the second question from Hagar. Instead, the angel's next three speeches (16:9, 16:10, 16:11-12) supply a future for her: return, offspring, a son.

**Three speeches from the angel.** 16:9, 16:10, and 16:11 each open with their own introduction ("So the angel of the LORD told her," "Then the angel added," "The angel of the LORD proceeded"), and Hagar does not speak between them. Rashi, citing Genesis Rabbah 45:7, explains the repeated introduction this way: "For each statement... a different angel was sent to her." Held reads the same silence as Hagar not responding until she speaks at 16:13. The repetition is in the text. Both explanations are readings of it.

## Positions

Five questions follow. Each is reported through named readers in their own words. None is adjudicated, and nothing in a position's statement should be read as this lesson's own conclusion.

**1. Who is "the angel of the LORD" (16:7)?** The text gives the facts every reading has to account for. The figure is called "the angel of the LORD" (16:7, 16:9, 16:10, 16:11). He promises in the first person what only God promises (16:10). The narrator calls him "the LORD who had spoken to her" (16:13). The Pulpit Commentary on 16:7 lays out the main division in one place, with named holders on each side.

- **A divine figure, often identified as the pre-incarnate Son.** The Pulpit Commentary holds this reading and names its holders: "the Fathers, the Reformers, Hengstenberg, Keil, Lange," and others. It reasons that the figure "explicitly identifies himself with Jehovah (Ver. 10)" and that those he meets "recognize him as Divine." Gill reads the angel at 16:7 as "not... a created angel, but... a divine Person... the Logos or Son of God," and links him to "the Angel of the covenant" (Malachi 3:1). Matthew Poole and Matthew Henry hold the same view (Henry: "The declaration of the Angel, I will, shows this Angel was the eternal Word and Son of God"). An early named source for this reading in general, read for this lesson, is Justin Martyr's *Dialogue with Trypho*, read directly in chapters 56-60. Justin argues that there is "another God and Lord subject to the Maker of all things; who is also called an Angel, because He announces to men whatsoever the Maker of all things... wishes to announce to them," and that "He who is called God and appeared to the patriarchs is called both Angel and Lord." Note that Justin builds that argument from Genesis 18, Jacob's visions, and the burning bush. In the chapters read for this lesson he does not discuss Hagar or Genesis 16, so applying his reading to 16:7 is a step later readers take, not one Justin is shown taking here.
- **A created angel, sent by God.** The same Pulpit note names holders of this reading, while arguing against it: Augustine, Origen, Jerome, Hofmann, Baumgarten (printed "Bamngarten" on the page, evidently a scanning error), Tholuck, Delitzsch, and Kurtz. It summarizes their reasons: "angel" normally names a class of created spirits, and the New Testament's "angel of the Lord" (Matthew 1:20; Luke 2:9; Acts 12:7) is "always a created angel." In Jewish interpretation, Rashi on 16:9, citing Genesis Rabbah 45:7, takes the three introductions in 16:9-11 to mean that "a different angel was sent to her" for each statement, a reading in which these are plainly messengers. Gill reports, secondhand, that "many of the Jewish writers take this angel to be a man sent of God." He names Gersom (Gersonides) as calling him one of the prophets, and some rabbis as identifying him with Shem.
- **God's own presence or self-manifestation, without identifying it with a second divine person.** The Cambridge Bible calls the angel "the personification of Jehovah," notes that "in Genesis 16:10 He identifies Himself with Jehovah," and treats the figure as marking "an intermediate stage" in "the development of religious thought" between earlier, more physical pictures of God and later, more abstract ones. Keil and Delitzsch, whom Pulpit counts on the first side, phrase the experience in similar terms: "In the angel, Hagar recognised God manifesting Himself to her, the presence of Jehovah."

The New Testament never identifies the angel of Genesis 16 with Christ. That is why this lesson does not author such a connection (see Teach-Back 5). It is also why the first reading above is reported as a theological synthesis its holders make, not as a cross-reference the Bible itself states.

**2. How should a reader judge Sarai, Abram, and Hagar in 16:4-6?** The text gives each person's words and actions and passes no verdict on any of them. Named readers have placed the fault in different places.

- **Sarai, and Abram with her.** Ramban (Nachmanides, thirteenth century; Chavel translation, read directly) is blunt: "Our mother did transgress by this affliction, and Abraham also by his permitting her to do so." He reads Ishmael's future as the consequence: God "gave her a son who would be a wild-ass of a man, to afflict the seed of Abraham and Sarah." Held reports that Radak (David Kimhi) also faults Sarah but "seems to exonerate" Abraham. Radak was not read directly for this lesson. On Abram's "Do whatever you want with her," the Pulpit Commentary names four readings: a peacemaking concession and a sign he had recognized his mistake (both credited to Calvin), "an indication of weakness" (Willet, Bush), and "an unjustifiable wrong inflicted on the future mother of his child" (Candlish). On what Sarai actually did, it names two readings: "thrusting her back into the condition of a slave" (Lange, Candlish), possibly with "stripes or maltreatment of some sort in addition" (Ainsworth, Bush). Rashi, citing Genesis Rabbah 45:6, says, "She compelled her to work hard." The Cambridge Bible says the verb "evidently means 'persecute,' 'ill-treat.'"
- **Hagar.** Other classic Christian commentators place the fault mainly with Hagar. Matthew Henry writes, "Hagar forgot that she herself had first given the provocation, by despising her mistress," and calls her "out of the way of her duty" when the angel found her. Matthew Poole says she fled "contrary to God's command." Barnes' Notes splits the fault: "The contempt of her maid was unjustifiable. But it was the natural consequence of Sarai's own improper and imprudent step." The Cambridge Bible takes the other side: "The character of Hagar is depicted as high-spirited and courageous, as well as independent. There is no evidence that her conduct was insolent."
- **Sarai's own cost.** Readers on every side above note that Sarai is not simply a villain in the text. She is the one who names her own pain: "the LORD has prevented me from bearing children" (16:2) and "May the wrong done to me be upon you!" (16:5). Rashi, citing Genesis Rabbah 45:2, reads her "build a family by her" as reflecting that "a person who has no children is not firmly established." Keil and Delitzsch say Sarai's plan "seemed a judicious one, and according to the customs of the East, there would be nothing wrong in carrying it out." They add that "Sarai, the originator of the plan, was the first to experience its evil consequences."

What can be checked: Sarai proposes, Abram agrees, and Hagar is the object of the proposal, not a party to it (16:2-3). The text records no words from Hagar until 16:8. It reports Hagar's contempt (16:4), Sarai's charge (16:5), Abram's hand-off (16:6), and Sarai's harsh treatment (16:6), and it states no verdict on any of them.

**3. Why does the angel send Hagar back (16:9)?** This is the hardest verse in the chapter for many modern readers. The same root that described Sarai's harsh treatment (16:6) appears in the angel's command (see Key Words). Named readers answer very differently.

- **A divine command that cuts against the Exodus.** Phyllis Trible, as Held quotes her, says the angel's instructions "bring a divine word of terror to an abused, yet courageous, woman" and "strike at the heart of Exodus faith," because God orders a runaway slave "to return not only to bondage but also to affliction." Held himself sets the verse beside Deuteronomy's law of the escaped slave, which the BSB renders "Do not return a slave to his master if he has taken refuge with you" (Deuteronomy 23:15). He notes that the angel's command "is at variance with the ethos" of that law, and he leaves the question open before coming back to it.
- **Return for the child's sake.** Keil and Delitzsch read the command through the child: "As the fruit of her womb was the seed of Abram, she was to return to his house and there bear him a son," who would be "honoured for Abram's sake with the blessing of an innumerable posterity."
- **Return as correction of a wayward servant.** Matthew Henry reads Hagar's flight as straying and the command as mercy: "It is a great mercy to be stopped in a sinful way, either by conscience or by providence."
- **A God who sustains survival where liberation is not yet given.** Delores S. Williams, an American Presbyterian theologian whose 1990 Union Theological Seminary dissertation and 1993 book *Sisters in the Wilderness: The Challenge of Womanist God-Talk* helped found womanist theology (biographical facts from Wikipedia, a tertiary source), reads Hagar's story beside the experience of enslaved and exploited Black women in America, especially their experience of coerced surrogacy. In LaRyssa D. Herrington's review in the *National Catholic Reporter* (2022, read directly), Williams is quoted describing the "wilderness experience" as "a near-destruction situation in which God gives personal direction to the believer and thereby helps her make a way out of what she thought was no way." She is also quoted stating her interpretive principle: "Liberation is an ultimate, but in the meantime survival and prosperity must be the experience of our people." Williams's book was not read for this lesson. Her words are given as the review reproduces them.

The text gives a reader the command (16:9) and, right after it, the promise (16:10-12). It states no reason for the command. Every position above supplies one.

**4. What is Paul doing with Hagar and Sarah in Galatians 4:21-31?** Paul names Hagar, calls the two women "two covenants," and says "Hagar stands for Mount Sinai in Arabia" (Galatians 4:24-25). The shipped BSB renders his key word at 4:24 "These things serve as illustrations." Other translations render it "allegory" (as in the King James wording the commentaries below quote, "Which things are an allegory"). The Greek, which Meyer and Barnes give as *allēgoreō*, occurs nowhere else in the New Testament, according to Meyer. Named commentators disagree about what kind of reading Paul is doing.

- **Allegory, kept distinct from typology.** Meyer's NT Commentary warns, "We must be on our guard against confounding the idea of the allegory with that of the type," and names Calvin, Tholuck, and Wieseler as having done so. Gill points out that Philo, a Jewish writer before Paul, had already allegorized the two women: "Sarah he makes to signify virtue, and Hagar the whole circle of arts and sciences."
- **Typology under the word "allegory."** The Cambridge Bible paraphrases 4:24 as "Now all these things may be regarded as an allegory," but adds that "the facts are historical, but they are types." Ellicott says "St. Paul does not use the word here in this strict sense," quotes Calvin that the chief events of Abraham's house "are so many types for us," and adds that the passage's "argumentative force" rests "upon the apostolic assertion of Christian liberty, not upon the logical cogency of the inference."
- **Illustration, not a claim about Moses' intent.** Barnes' Notes says Paul does not mean "that Moses meant this to be an allegory," that "No such design is apparent in the narrative of Moses," and that Paul uses the account "as he would any other historical fact that would illustrate the same principle."

Two checkable facts sit under this dispute. First, the only Genesis text Paul actually quotes in the passage is from chapter 21: "Expel the slave woman and her son" (Galatians 4:30, citing Genesis 21:10). Second, in Paul's figure Hagar stands for slavery, while in Genesis 16 she is the person the LORD sees and hears. A reader can hold both texts and notice that Paul uses Hagar for a purpose Genesis 16 does not state.

**5. Is the claim that Hagar is the first person to name God a point of doctrine, or a point of reading?** It is reported here as a position only because writers draw different weight from it. Held treats Hagar's naming as the climax of the chapter: she "breaks her silence, and responds, by giving God a name." The textual fact under it (Key Words, above) holds on this lesson's own check for Genesis 1-16. What it signifies is a reader's judgment.

Where this chapter touches a reader's own life (enslavement, coerced surrogacy, abuse inside a household, being told to go back), the positions above are reports of how others have read a text. They are not guidance for anyone's situation. A reader working through such things for themselves needs a pastor, not a study note. If you are in a household or workplace where you are being abused, controlled, or forced to work, a qualified person, not a study note, is the right next step: a local domestic-violence or anti-trafficking hotline, or emergency services if you are in danger.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step, and a different passage element, situation, or response would be just as legitimate. This example deliberately does not build a bridge from 16:9 (the command to return). Positions (3) shows how sharply named readers disagree about that verse, and a worked "application" of it could be heard as advice to someone in danger. It works from 16:8 and 16:13 instead.

**Passage element.** Genesis 16:2-8, 16:13. In the household, Hagar is never called by her name: "my maidservant," "my servant," "your servant" (16:2, 16:5, 16:6). The first speaker to use her name is the angel, "Hagar, servant of Sarai" (16:8), and he asks her a question about herself. Hagar's response at 16:13 is to name the God "who sees me."

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the chapter makes a checkable contrast between how the household speaks of Hagar (by role) and how the angel first addresses her (by name, then role, then a question). Inference: for a first audience used to households in which slaves were spoken of by their function, a story in which God's messenger speaks to the slave by name, asks for her own account, and is answered with a name for God would likely have registered the slave as someone God deals with directly. The text states no such moral in its own words. The inference rests on the contrast itself.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** How a person is addressed, by name or only by role, and whether anyone asks for their own account, is a real part of whether they are treated as a person. Asking someone "where have you come from?" and listening to the answer is a different act from deciding about them in their absence.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a person whose situation is being decided by others, who is spoken of by role, and whose own account has not been asked for. Not carried forward: slavery, the theophany, the command of 16:9, and any claim that the modern person's situation is like Hagar's in its severity. The bridge holds only where those features match.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed. The promises of 16:10-12 were spoken to Hagar about Ishmael, and nothing here extends them to the reader.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. A department is reorganizing, and a long-time administrative assistant's role is being cut back. In planning meetings she is referred to only as "the admin position," and no one has asked her what her job actually involves.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: restoring a person's own voice to a decision being made about them. One faithful response is for the manager to meet her, use her name, tell her plainly what is being considered, and ask for her own account of the work before the decision is final. Not every outcome will be what she wants, but she will be addressed as a person rather than a line on a chart.

**Cautions.** This bridge is about ordinary workplace dignity, not abuse. If a workplace or household involves coercion, threats, withheld pay or documents, or physical harm, the right response is not a better conversation but protection: a qualified person, not a study note, is the right next step. The passage is not a management text. The bridge is an analogy the learner is testing, and the learner may find it does not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. The same Hebrew root sits behind "treated... so harshly" (16:6), "submit" (16:9), and "affliction" (16:11), and the BSB uses three different English words for it. Having seen the repetition, does it change how you hear the angel's command in 16:9? What would you need to know about the root's range of meaning before letting it carry much weight?
2. Positions (2) reports readers who place the fault with Sarai and Abram (Ramban, Candlish), with Hagar (Matthew Henry, Poole), or with the plan itself (Keil and Delitzsch). The text states no verdict. What in the text's own wording, not any commentator's gloss, would you point to first, and does it point to one person or to the household as a whole?
3. Genesis 16 gives Hagar a name for God and Galatians 4 makes Hagar stand for "slavery." Can a reader hold both at once? What would it take for Paul's figurative use and the chapter's own portrait to conflict, rather than simply serve different purposes?
4. The angel's description of Ishmael (16:12) has been read as an insult, as a portrait of noble freedom, and as a vindication of his mother. What in the verse itself, read alone, supports each of those readings, and what has to be brought to it from outside?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate Genesis 16:1-16 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) State, in the text's own words, what the angel tells Hagar to do (16:9), what he promises (16:10-12), and what Hagar names God (16:13), before saying what any reader concludes about any of it. (c) Name one place elsewhere in Scripture where this chapter's people, words, or pattern reappear, and say what kind of connection it is.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the plain sequence of 16:1-16, keeping all three people's costs in view (Context); (1:00-1:45) the custom behind 16:2 and the named dispute over what ancient Near Eastern parallels can show (Context: Selman, Speiser, Thompson, van Seters); (1:45-2:45) the key words, including the one root under three English words and the first appearance of "the angel of the LORD" (Key Words); (2:45-4:00) the named positions on the angel's identity, the household's conduct, the command to return, and Paul's figurative reading, held open on purpose (Positions); (4:00-4:30) where this chapter travels later in Scripture, naming each connection's evidence label (prompt 5); (4:30-5:00) the misuse of 16:12 and the boundary (prompt 4).
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first; the point of this prompt is that you do the forming. If you are stuck, here is one worked candidate, stated at full strength: reporting Matthew Henry's "a great mercy to be stopped in a sinful way" next to Trible's "a divine word of terror," without choosing between them, treats as an open question whether a runaway slave woman was at fault for running. That gives a reading that blames the victim the same standing as one that does not. Before answering, weigh what Positions (3) actually reports. Each reading is attributed in its own words, and the text itself (16:9) states no reason for the command. Ask whether the lesson's restraint is about the text's silence or about the moral question of slavery itself. A second candidate, running the other direction: reporting Trible and Held's "at variance with" Deuteronomy may read later law and modern moral intuitions back into a chapter whose first audience may not have shared them, and so understate how readers such as Keil and Delitzsch, who read the command through the promised child, actually reasoned.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Genesis 16 does not state who "the angel of the LORD" is beyond what 16:10 and 16:13 say. Identifying him with the pre-incarnate Son, a created messenger, or God's self-manifestation is a step each tradition takes from wider premises (Positions 1), and no New Testament text identifies the angel of this chapter. (b) Genesis 16:12 does not name any nation or ethnicity or describe any people's character. It speaks of one man and "his brothers," and its last clause has several named translations (Context). (c) Genesis 16 does not itself state the figurative meaning Paul gives it in Galatians 4. The only Genesis text Paul quotes there is from chapter 21.
