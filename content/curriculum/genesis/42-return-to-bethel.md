Genesis 35 is a chapter of endings. God tells Jacob, "Arise, go up to Bethel" (35:1), and Jacob's household gives up its "foreign gods" and "earrings," which he buries "under the oak near Shechem" (35:4). Rebekah's nurse Deborah dies and is buried below Bethel (35:8). God appears again, names Jacob Israel a second time, and repeats the promise of nations, kings and land (35:9-12). On the road south Rachel dies giving birth: "with her last breath—for she was dying—she named him Ben-oni. But his father called him Benjamin" (35:18). Reuben "slept with his father’s concubine Bilhah, and Israel heard about it" (35:22). The chapter lists the twelve sons and closes with Isaac's death and burial by "his sons Esau and Jacob" (35:29). This lesson counts what the chapter shares with other texts and reports, without choosing, how named readers fill its gaps.

## Context

**What happens, in order (35:1-29).** The call to Bethel (35:1); the household purified and its gods and earrings buried near Shechem (35:2-4); "a terror from God fell over the surrounding cities" (35:5); an altar at Luz, "that is, Bethel," called El-bethel (35:6-7). Deborah dies (35:8). God appears, renames and blesses Jacob, who sets up a pillar, pours a drink offering and oil, and names the place Bethel (35:9-15). Rachel dies near Ephrath and is buried with a pillar (35:16-20). Israel camps beyond the Tower of Eder; Reuben and Bilhah (35:21-22a). The twelve sons are listed by mother (35:22b-26). Jacob comes to Isaac at Mamre; Isaac dies at 180 and is buried (35:27-29).

**Key words, read as observations.** Counts were made by this lesson's own script over the app's BSB text, case-insensitive and on word boundaries.

- **Bethel.** "Bethel" occurs in 11 verses of Genesis, 7 of them in this chapter (35:1, 3, 6, 7, 8, 15, 16); "El-bethel" occurs only at 35:7. "Oil" occurs in Genesis only at 28:18 and 35:14 (per the Bethel row), and "drink offering" only at 35:14.
- **"Foreign gods" and "earrings" (35:2-4).** "Foreign gods" occurs in 13 BSB verses; the whole command "get rid of the foreign gods" occurs only in 35:2 and Joshua 24:23. "Earrings" occurs in Genesis only at 35:4. The Hebrew there is *nezamim*, "rings" (Strong's 5141); Brown-Driver-Briggs lists the same Hebrew phrase (the rings that were in their ears) for the gold earrings Aaron collects in Exodus 32:2-3, and the same noun for Rebekah's ring in 24:22.
- **Two oaks.** The BSB says "oak" in 35:4 and 35:8, but Bible Hub's interlinear shows two Hebrew nouns: *ha'elah* (Strong's 424) at Shechem, *ha'allon* (437) at Bethel. Deborah's tree is named *Allon-bachuth*, "oak of weeping"; the verb is "he called" (3rd masculine), which the BSB renders "Jacob named it."
- **"Rachel’s tomb."** The phrase occurs in two verses only, 35:20 and 1 Samuel 10:2. "Ephrath" occurs at 35:16, 35:19, 48:7 and 1 Chronicles 2:19; "Bethlehem" occurs in Genesis only in the parenthetical notes of 35:19 and 48:7. "Zelzah" occurs only at 1 Samuel 10:2. Rachel is named in 47 verses: 43 in Genesis, then Ruth 4:11, 1 Samuel 10:2, Jeremiah 31:15 and Matthew 2:18.
- **Ben-oni.** The name occurs once (35:18). Bible Hub's Strong's entry derives it from *ben* and *'aven* and glosses it "son of my sorrow." The same consonants, *'oni*, appear in Jacob's words to Reuben, "the beginning of my strength" (49:3), where the interlinear assigns Strong's 202, *'on*, "strength."
- **The midwife's words.** "Do not be afraid" occurs in 8 verses of Genesis; at 35:17 it is the midwife's, "Do not be afraid, for you are having another son." "Another son" also occurs at 30:24, where Rachel named Joseph: "May the LORD add to me another son."
- **Israel.** The narrator first calls Jacob "Israel" in the course of the story at 35:21 ("Israel again set out"); 32:28 and 35:10 give the name, and 33:20 and 34:7 use it otherwise. "Israel heard" occurs in four verses; at 35:22 Bible Hub's interlinear prints a paragraph mark (*pe*) after "and Israel heard," inside the verse, before "Jacob had twelve sons." "Twelve sons" occurs in the BSB only here.
- **"kings shall descend from you" (35:11).** See conn-gen35-kings-gen17. In English it nearly matches 17:6 (17:6 "will," 35:11 "shall"); in Hebrew 17:6 has "from you," 35:11 "from your loins." "A company of nations" (35:11) is unique; Isaac's blessing had "a company of peoples" (28:3), and Bible Hub shows *qehal goyim* and *qehal 'ammim* respectively.

**Isaac's death (35:27-29).** Isaac dies at 180 and is buried by "his sons Esau and Jacob" (35:29), the only BSB verse with "Esau and Jacob." The parallel with 25:9 is already authored. Rashi notes that the narrative is not in chronological order here: "the sale of Joseph preceded Isaac’s death by twelve years."

**The places.** This app's places data (OpenBible.info) has Bethel, Luz, El-bethel, Allon-bacuth (its spelling), Ephrath and Bethlehem, Eder, Mamre and Hebron for this chapter, and Ramah for Jeremiah 31:15. It has no entry for Rachel's tomb and none for Zelzah. Ephrath is marked a "likely" identification with Bethlehem, "Based on a single source in the dataset"; this lesson adds no places.

## Literary Design

**Three deaths, two pillars.** The chapter buries Deborah, Rachel and Isaac. Two of the burials leave a name or a marker: the "oak of weeping" (35:8) and a pillar that "marks Rachel’s tomb to this day" (35:20). Jacob raises two pillars in the chapter, one where God spoke (35:14) and one on a grave (35:20). Benson puts it this way: "Jacob set up a pillar in remembrance of his joys, (Genesis 35:14,) and here he sets up one in remembrance of his sorrows." That is a reader's pairing; the narrator does not comment.

## Positions

Six questions follow. Each is reported through named readers in their own words and is not adjudicated. Nothing in a reading's statement is this lesson's own conclusion.

**1. The earrings and the burial under the oak (35:2-4).** Whose gods these were is the subject of conn-gen31-household-gods-gen35 (Rachel's teraphim, or spoil from Shechem); this lesson leaves it there. Two further questions remain.

- **What were the earrings?** *Charms bearing idols' images.* Targum Pseudo-Jonathan says they "had been in the ears of the inhabitants of the city of Shekem, in which was portrayed the likeness of their images." Radak: the silver and gold looted from Shechem "had idolatrous images engraved upon them, as had the various pieces of jewelry." The Cambridge Bible: "rings worn as charms, and amulets, having symbols of heathen deities." Keil and Delitzsch: "worn as amulets and charms." Ellicott: "regarded as talismans or amulets. Hence it was from their earrings that Aaron made the golden calf." Calvin: "The earrings were doubtless badges of superstition." Gill distinguishes: "not the earrings that women wore in common, such as Abraham's servant gave to Rebekah," but those worn "in a superstitious way." *Possibly ordinary jewelry, given up as a precaution.* Poole offers that they were surrendered either because they had been "abused to idolatry and superstition" or "for fear they should be so abused," and Benson repeats the two options.
- **Why bury them rather than destroy them?** Ramban raises the problem: an idol is not something "for which burial suffices." His answer is that the items from Shechem had already been nullified, so "Burial was thus sufficient," and Jacob removed them "for the sake of the purity of holy things." Radak: Jacob buried them "so as not to become the indirect cause of someone retrieving the items." The Pulpit Commentary thinks Jacob hid them "having probably first destroyed them" (citing Hughes); Gill thinks he "broke to pieces, perhaps" the idols before burying them. Calvin reads it as "in token of his detestation." Genesis Rabbah 81:3 tells of a Samaritan on Mount Gerizim and a rabbi who accuses him of being "eager for" the mountain because "idols are interred under" it.

Among the sources read, the one direct disagreement is over the tree. Keil and Delitzsch call it "probably the very tree under which Abraham once pitched his tent (Genesis 12:6)," regarded as sacred "in Joshua's time"; the Cambridge Bible notes that "Joshua, under the same 'oak' of Shechem (Joshua 24:26), testified against the primitive worship of strange gods." Gill answers: "of that there can be no certainty, since Jacob, as it is highly probable, laid these images alone, and never intended any should know anything of them where they were." This lesson does not decide between these readings.

**2. The "oak of weeping": one mourning or two (35:8)?** The verse names Deborah, "Rebekah’s nurse," whom Genesis has mentioned only unnamed (24:59, "her nurse"). Rebekah's own death is never narrated.

- **A mourning for Deborah herself.** Calvin: she was a "holy matron, and whom the family of Jacob venerated as a mother; for the name given in perpetuity to the place, testifies that she was buried with peculiar honor, and with no common mourning." Keil and Delitzsch: "the grief of Jacob's house on account of her death"; Ellicott: "How dear she was to them is shown by their calling the tree under which she was buried the oak of weeping."
- **A second mourning, for Rebekah.** Genesis Rabbah 81:5 (Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥman): "In the Greek language, alon means other. While he [Jacob] was still observing the mourning for Deborah, the report that his mother had died came to him." It reads the next verse's blessing (35:9) as "the blessing of the mourners." Rashi and Radak report this; Targum Pseudo-Jonathan writes it into its rendering, "there it was told Jakob concerning the death of Rivekah his mother; and he called the name of it, The other weeping." Ramban adds a reason from the text: "the weeping and anguish could not have been such for the passing of the old nurse that the place would have been named on account of it," and notes that 35:27 (in his rendering, "Isaac his father") names no mother. He quotes a Tanḥuma midrash that Rebekah's bier was carried out at night so that people would not curse "the breast that gave suck" to Esau.

Readers also ask how Deborah came to be with Jacob. Rashi reports, from Rabbi Moses Hadarshan, that Rebekah sent her to call Jacob home (27:45). Ramban calls this "improbable," and Calvin writes, "I do not see what probability there is in the conjecture." The Pulpit Commentary lists four explanations from Delitzsch, Lange, Keil and Kalisch. No source read here argues directly against the Rebekah reading; the Christian commentators read simply do not adopt it. This lesson does not decide between these readings.

**3. A second appearance, a second "Bethel," a second "Israel" (35:9-15).** God "appeared to him again" (35:9), renames him (35:10), and Jacob again "called the place ... Bethel" (35:15; compare 28:19). The 32 to 35 naming parallel is already authored; this Position adds what readers say from this side, with comparable weight to each.

- **A confirmation.** Keil and Delitzsch: God appeared "again," this time "by daylight in a visible form," and its "reality is thereby established, in opposition to the conjecture that it is merely a legendary repetition of the previous vision." The Pulpit Commentary: the renewed name "was probably designed as a confirmation of the former interview with God," comparing "the twice-given name of Peter." Calvin: "a second vision, by which Jacob was confirmed." Ramban on 35:15: "He called it so time after time," to show the place is "truthfully and properly a House of G-d." Genesis Rabbah 82:2 has an angel foretell the appearance at Bethel, reading Hosea 12:5.
- **A renewal after a decline.** Barnes: the renaming implies that Jacob's spiritual life "had been declining in the interval between Penuel and Bethel, and had now been revived." The Pulpit Commentary reports the same view from Murphy before preferring "confirmation."
- **A name added, not a name replaced.** Ibn Ezra: "You shall no longer be called only Jacob but also Israel." Ramban: the name Israel was given "in addition to the name Jacob." Calvin takes the opposite view of the grammar: "the old name, having in it less of splendor, should give place to the second," though "a double name was given."
- **Two places.** Rashbam reads two Bethels: "he named a second place Bet El," El-bethel being "the site where he had had the dream," outside Luz.
- **Two sources.** The Cambridge Bible assigns the passage to the Priestly writer: "P ignores the whole J and E narrative since the departure from Haran," and the phrases of 35:11 "are characteristic of P’s style."

This lesson collects no objections for this question; readers can weigh the readings directly. This lesson does not decide between them.

**4. "Ben-oni" and "Benjamin" (35:16-18).** The text gives Rachel's naming and Jacob's, in that order, and no comment. *A note for readers who are grieving:* every reading below is a reader's, and none of them is a word about any particular death or about how anyone should name or mourn. A pastor, chaplain or grief counselor is the right companion for that, not a study note.

Genesis gives no cause beyond "her labor was difficult" (35:16). Some readers supply one: Calvin thinks God took Rachel to correct Jacob's excessive love for her, and he places the fault in Jacob, not Rachel; Genesis Rabbah and Rashi tie it to Jacob's oath (see the Genesis 31 lesson); the Pulpit Commentary sees it as "a pathetic commentary on Genesis 30:1." Against reading any death in childbirth as caused, Ibn Ezra (quoted in the Genesis 31 lesson) asks who then caused the death of Phinehas' wife. On the names:

- **Jacob replaced a name of grief.** Calvin reads Jacob's renaming as a judgment that Rachel's grief was excessive and the name ill-omened; in his words, Jacob "corrected the error." Benson: Jacob would "not renew the sorrowful remembrance of his mother’s death every time he called his son by name." Ellicott sees Jacob's choice as given "not merely that the child might-bear no ill-omened title, but to mark his sense of the value and preciousness of his last born son," and of Rachel writes, "doubtless, her feeling was that the life of her offspring was purchased by her own pain and death."
- **Jacob kept her word and turned it.** Ramban: Rachel meant "the son of my mourning," but "his father understood the word oni in the sense of 'my strength,'" as in "the first-fruits of 'oni'" (49:3), so "Jacob wanted to call him by the name his mother had called him ... and he thus rendered it to good and to strength." Genesis Rabbah 82:9 reads the change as a matter of language: Ben-oni is "the son of my travail in the Aramaic language," Benjamin "in the sacred tongue" (Ramban says he does not understand this, "for it is all the Sacred Language").
- **What "Benjamin" means.** "Son of the right hand," that is, especially beloved (Radak, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown); "son of the south," the one son born in Canaan (Rashi; Ramban disputes the geography); "son of his old days" (Rashi's alternative; the Pulpit Commentary reports it from the Samaritan, and the Testament of Benjamin has "the son of days"); "son of good fortune" (Keil, from Arabic).

Among the sources read, the direct answers are Ramban's, to Rashi on "south" and to Genesis Rabbah on "the sacred tongue." Calvin and Ramban differ on whether Jacob set Rachel's name aside or kept it, but neither addresses the other. This lesson does not decide between them.

**5. Where is Rachel's tomb? (35:19-20; 48:7; 1 Samuel 10:2; Jeremiah 31:15).** Genesis says she was buried "on the way to Ephrath (that is, Bethlehem)" (35:19; also 48:7). Samuel tells Saul he will find two men "at Rachel’s tomb in Zelzah on the border of Benjamin" (1 Samuel 10:2). Jeremiah hears Rachel weeping "in Ramah" (31:15), a town of Benjamin. This is an old crux; Genesis Rabbah 82:9 records a heretic asking, "Is Tzeltzaḥ not on the border of Benjamin, and Rachel’s tomb on the border of Judah?"

- **Near Bethlehem; the other texts read loosely or figuratively.** In Genesis Rabbah 82:9 Rabbi Yonatan reorders Samuel's sentence; of the two versions the midrash gives, it calls this one "accurate": "at the border of Benjamin in Tzeltzaḥ, you will find two men by Rachel’s tomb." Poole and Benson on 1 Samuel 10:2: the grave "might be either in Judah or in Benjamin; for the possessions of those two tribes were bordering upon one another." The Pulpit Commentary on 1 Samuel 10:2 reports Wilson placing Zelzah "at Beit-jala, to the west of Bethlehem." Ellicott on Jeremiah 31:15 allows that *ramah* may mean "from a mountain height," not the town. The Pulpit Commentary on Genesis 35:20 sees "no reason to question the tradition which from the fourth century has placed it" north of Bethlehem.
- **North, on Benjamin's border; "that is, Bethlehem" is a gloss.** The Cambridge Bible on Genesis 35:19: "There is clearly, therefore, a discrepancy. Perhaps two traditions were current respecting the sepulchre," and "the same is Beth-lehem" looks "like a gloss, erroneously inserted into the text." It adds that the gloss would account "for the Christian tradition of Rachel’s tomb N. of Bethlehem." The Cambridge Bible on 1 Samuel 10:2 (a separate volume) reports Thenius's view that Ephrath was "a town in the neighbourhood of Ramah and Gibeah," which "involves rejecting" the Genesis gloss and "the modern site of the tomb."
- **Both: near Bethlehem, in Benjamin's land.** Ramban combines both: he "saw with my eyes that there is not even a mile between Rachel’s grave and Bethlehem," and holds Jacob buried her "within the border of her son Benjamin," citing the Sifre, "Rachel died in the portion of Benjamin." He reads Jeremiah as a figure: "Rachel wept so loudly and bitterly that her voice was heard from afar in Ramah."

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the near-Bethlehem reading, the Cambridge Bible on 1 Samuel 10:2 says the traditional site "is at least 4 miles S. of the southern border of Benjamin" and that it is "hard to see why Saul should be sent so far out of his way home." Against the gloss reading, the Pulpit Commentary on 35:19 calls the claim of a later interpolation "unfounded (Kalisch, Kurtz)." The Cambridge Bible on 1 Samuel 10:2 weighs Thenius's relocation and Keil's alternative, calls "most plausible" a reading that keeps the traditional site (Samuel "purposely sent Saul out of his way"), and concludes: "the true solution must remain uncertain." Against Ramban's figurative reading of "in Ramah," the Cambridge Bible on Jeremiah 31:15 holds that "The geographical connexion of Ramah and Bethlehem cannot be maintained." This lesson does not decide where the tomb was.

**6. Reuben and Bilhah (35:22).** The verse is one sentence: "Reuben went in and slept with his father’s concubine Bilhah, and Israel heard about it." Bilhah does not speak here or anywhere in Genesis, and the text records no word of hers and nothing about her will. The Genesis 30 lesson on Bilhah and Zilpah handled her silence the same way. Two questions follow.

- **What did Reuben do?** *What the words say.* Radak: "he went to Bilhah’s tent and slept with her," adding that she was legally Jacob's wife and "totally out of bounds to him." Poole: "a horrid incest." The Pulpit Commentary: "an act of incest (Leviticus 18:8) for which he was afterwards disinherited (Genesis 49:4; 1 Chronicles 5:1)." Rashbam offers no other sense and says the verse is told so that 49:4 can be understood. Tikva Frymer-Kensky (Jewish Women's Archive) notes that "perhaps that is why Bilhah is called his secondary wife (pilegesh) in this one text—to diminish the gravity of the act." *He disturbed the bed, and Scripture counts it as if he had sinned.* Shabbat 55b (Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani for Rabbi Yonatan): "Anyone who says that Reuben sinned with Bilhah is nothing other than mistaken," because the verse goes on to count all twelve sons as equal; Reuben "rearranged his father’s bed in protest" that Jacob had moved his bed to Bilhah's tent rather than Leah's after Rachel's death. Rashi follows it; Targum Pseudo-Jonathan renders the verse "confounded the bed of Bilhah"; Ibn Ezra: "Our sages explained this beautifully." The same page of the Talmud records that the sages disagreed: "Rabban Gamliel and Rabbi Elazar HaModa’i are of the opinion that Reuben did not sin. Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yehoshua held that he did." Ramban, "In line with the simple meaning of Scripture," gives a different motive: Reuben acted because "he feared that she might give birth again," costing him part of a firstborn's inheritance.
- **How have readers treated Bilhah?** Genesis records no word, act or consent of Bilhah's at 35:22, and none anywhere in the book. Some Christian commentators nonetheless counted her a party to the act: Calvin speaks of her "adultery" and "incest," and Benson suggests she may have been the more guilty, though he turns at once to Reuben's loss of the birthright. Two early retellings tell it otherwise. In Jubilees 33 (R. H. Charles) Reuben finds her "sleeping alone"; she "awoke ... seized him, and cried out," "lamented because of this thing exceedingly," and told Jacob, "I was asleep, and did not discover until he uncovered my skirt and slept with me." In the Testament of Reuben (Ante-Nicene Fathers) Reuben says she "was drunk, and lay asleep uncovered in her chamber" and that he left "her sleeping"; that work goes on to draw warnings against women from the story, which this lesson does not repeat. Rabbinic tradition, Tamar Kadari reports (JWA), ruled the verse among those "read but without targum," a sign of "displeasure at this act."

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the bed-moving reading, the plain verb (Radak), the Talmud's own dissenters (Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yehoshua), and the word "defiled" in 49:4 and 1 Chronicles 5:1. Against the plain reading, Shabbat 55b argues from "Jacob had twelve sons" and from Deuteronomy 27:20: could Reuben's descendants pronounce that curse on their own ancestor? Against reading Bilhah as a party to the act: Genesis gives her no word or consent, and Jubilees and the Testament of Reuben say she was asleep. Calvin and Benson give no textual argument for her guilt; they assume it. Genesis itself gives one sentence and records that Israel heard. On the plain reading, the verse describes a sexual act done to a woman who, in those two early retellings, was asleep and could not consent. *If this is close to your own experience,* the RAINN line above is free and confidential at any hour.

**And Israel heard.** Ramban sees "Jacob’s humility": he did not remove Reuben "from inclusion among his sons." Calvin: Moses "conceals his grief ... because his grief was too great to be expressed." The Pulpit Commentary: the break in the text "may ... have been designed to express Jacob's grief." Radak: "he stopped living with Bilhah as a result of this." Frymer-Kensky: "Jacob hears about what Reuben did and does nothing at the time."

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, as a model of the shape, not an answer key. It does not build a bridge from Rachel's death, the names or 35:22; Positions 4-6 show how sharply readers disagree.

**Passage element.** Genesis 35:8 and 35:20: a tree named "Allon-bachuth" for a death, and a pillar that "marks Rachel’s tomb to this day."

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: twice in one chapter a death is marked with something that lasts and has a name. Inference (this lesson's own): for a first audience, these markers kept losses in the family's memory and on the map; 1 Samuel 10:2 shows a later generation still finding Rachel's tomb.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Grief can be given a named place and a lasting marker.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a loss marked deliberately, with a name and a place. Not carried forward: any rule about how long or how anyone should mourn, any claim about why a person died, or any reading of the names in 35:18.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a narrative pattern applied by analogy, not a command. Promise scope: none. Nothing in 35:8 or 35:20 promises comfort or explains a death.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family. A family has lost a grandmother who helped raise the children, and the youngest ask where she "is."

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: remembrance. One faithful response is to choose, together, a place or object that will carry her name (a planted tree, a marked grave, a written page) and to visit it.

**Cautions.** This is not grief counseling. Families mourn in different ways and traditions differ on memorials. If grief becomes more than someone can carry, a pastor, chaplain or grief counselor is the right next step; in a crisis, use the numbers at the top of this lesson.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. The BSB says "oak" twice (35:4, 35:8) for two Hebrew words. What does an English reader lose when a translation smooths a difference like that?
2. Rachel named her son and Jacob renamed him (35:18). What would you need from the text before saying Jacob rejected her name, or kept it?
3. Genesis 35:19, 1 Samuel 10:2 and Jeremiah 31:15 place Rachel in three ways. Which of the readings in Position 5 asks you to assume the least?
4. Bilhah never speaks. What would it mean to read 35:22 with her in view, without inventing words the text does not give her?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 35:1-29 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) Give the two names in 35:18 and say what each is usually taken to mean. (c) Name the three places 35:19, 1 Samuel 10:2 and Jeremiah 31:15 connect with Rachel.
2. **Five-minute outline.** (0:00-1:00) the plot; (1:00-2:00) key words and counts; (2:00-2:45) the earrings and the oak; (2:45-3:30) the "oak of weeping" and the second naming; (3:30-4:15) Ben-oni and the tomb crux; (4:15-5:00) Reuben and Bilhah, with how readers treat Bilhah.
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: "Shabbat 55b says Reuben only moved a bed, so the verse is not about a sexual act." Weigh it against the Talmud's own record that Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yehoshua "held that he did," and against "defiled" in 49:4. A second candidate, running the other way: "The plain sense is obvious, so the rabbis were simply evading it." Weigh it against Shabbat 55b's stated textual argument from "Jacob had twelve sons" and Deuteronomy 27:20.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) It does not say why Rachel died beyond "her labor was difficult" (35:16-17). (b) It does not say what Bilhah said, wanted or did. (c) It does not narrate Rebekah's death; the "second mourning" is a reading of 35:8. (d) It does not say which kings 35:11 means.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** The reviewed connections attached to this lesson:

Choose one row and defend it, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` for a candidate. Three candidates this lesson chose not to author:

- **Genesis 35:16-20 to Matthew 2:18 directly.** Gill on 35:19 says Rachel is represented "with great pertinency" as weeping when Herod killed the children near the place she was buried. But Matthew cites Jeremiah, not Genesis, and that citation is already a row. The thread runs Genesis 35 to Jeremiah 31 (this lesson's row) and Jeremiah 31 to Matthew 2 (the existing row). A direct Genesis-to-Matthew row would duplicate the two: `no_warrant_yet`.
- **The Tower of Eder (35:21) and the shepherds of Luke 2:8.** Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on 35:21 calls it "the place from whence ... the King Meshiha will be revealed at the end of the days." Alfred Edersheim, citing that Targum, writes that a Mishnah passage "leads to the conclusion, that the flocks, which pastured there, were destined for Temple-sacrifices," and links them with the shepherds of Luke 2. Luke names no tower; the site is uncertain (the Pulpit Commentary: "the site of which is uncertain"); and Micah 4:8's "watchtower of the flock" shares the Hebrew but not the BSB's English. A popular link that rests on a Targum and an inference: `no_warrant_yet`.
- **1 Samuel 4:19-22 (the wife of Phinehas).** Genesis Rabbah 82:7 lists "three who encountered difficulty in childbirth and died after giving birth": Rachel, the wife of Phinehas and Michal. In 1 Samuel 4:20 the women attending say, "Do not be afraid, for you have given birth to a son!" and the dying mother names the child for a loss. A real parallel, best authored from a 1 Samuel lesson.
