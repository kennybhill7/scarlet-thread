Genesis 1:1-2:3 is the passage every reader of this app has already half-formed an opinion about before opening it, and that is exactly why it is a good place to practice the same study skill Genesis 3 began with: telling apart a cited fact, a textual observation anyone can check, and a named position a tradition or scholar concludes from the text. The "days" of Genesis 1 are the clearest test case in the whole Bible for this. Real, named, currently-publishing scholars and long-standing traditions read the Hebrew word for "day" differently from one another, for reasons each can state in their own words. This lesson reports those readings side by side. It does not pick one for you.

## Context

**What happens, in order (1:1-2:3).** In the beginning God creates the heavens and the earth; the earth is formless and void, and the Spirit of God hovers over the waters (1:1-2). Across six marked days God speaks, and what he commands comes to be: light, separated from darkness and named "day" and "night" (day one, 1:3-5); an expanse separating waters above from waters below, named "sky" (day two, 1:6-8); the waters below gathered so dry land appears, named "earth" and "seas," and the land produces vegetation (day three, 1:9-13); lights in the expanse to separate day from night and to mark seasons, days, and years -- the sun, the moon, and the stars (day four, 1:14-19); sea creatures and flying creatures, blessed and told to multiply (day five, 1:20-23); land animals, and then humankind, made in God's own image, male and female, blessed and given dominion over the earth and its creatures, and given every seed-bearing plant for food (day six, 1:24-31). God then looks on everything made and calls it "very good" (1:31). The heavens and the earth, with everything in them, are completed; on the seventh day God rests from his work, and he blesses the seventh day and sets it apart, because on it he rested from all the work of creating that he had done (2:1-3). This is a plain retelling of the text's own order. What the "days" themselves are -- the question this lesson's Positions section exists to report -- is a separate question from this paragraph.

**Where Genesis 1 sits.** Modern commentary on Genesis is close to unanimous in treating chapters 1-11 as a distinguishable literary unit, commonly labeled the "Primeval History," before the patriarchal narratives of chapters 12-50 begin -- Bill T. Arnold's *Genesis* (New Cambridge Bible Commentary, 2008) is structured around exactly this division, and David L. Petersen, writing for the Society of Biblical Literature's Bible Odyssey project, describes the Primeval History as running from creation to the birth of Abraham. What kind of material this opening unit *is* -- and consequently how literally its sequence and duration should be pressed -- is itself a live, current disagreement among named, published scholars and traditions, not a settled question; this lesson's Positions section below reports that disagreement for the "days" specifically rather than resolving the broader genre question here.

**Ancient Near Eastern background.** Genesis 1 is routinely read by scholars alongside the *Enuma Elish*, the Babylonian creation epic in which the god Marduk defeats the sea goddess Tiamat and fashions the cosmos from her body, going on to organize the heavens, set the stars in their courses, and create humanity to perform the gods' labor. Both accounts open with a watery, unformed state; both move toward an ordered cosmos with sky, luminaries, and humanity in a broadly similar sequence; Bryan Windle's survey (citing Joseph Holden and Norman Geisler's *Popular Handbook of Biblical Archaeology*) catalogues these parallels alongside the differences both sides of the comparison report: Genesis presents one God who speaks the cosmos into being by command rather than by violent combat among many gods, and humanity is made in God's own image and given dominion rather than created as menial labor for the gods' rest. One frequently repeated claim deserves a direct caution: that the Hebrew *tehom* ("deep," 1:2) is linguistically derived from the Babylonian goddess-name Tiamat. David Toshio Tsumura's linguistic study argues instead that *tehom* reflects a common Semitic root shared by Akkadian, Ugaritic, and Eblaite cognates, and that nothing in the Hebrew text requires dependence on the Babylonian conflict myth. This lesson's access to Tsumura's own argument was limited to a secondary report rather than the full text (see Sources and Limits, below), so the word's relationship to Tiamat is flagged here as disputed rather than settled either way.

**Key words, read as observations.** Two pairs of Hebrew words in this passage are worth slowing down on, because the lexical facts themselves are teachable independently of what any position concludes from them:

- **יוֹם** (*yom*, "day") is the word every named position in this lesson's Positions section argues about, and the argument is possible because the word itself has a real range of ordinary senses across the Hebrew Bible -- the period of daylight as distinguished from night, a 24-hour civil day, and a longer or less definite stretch of time (as in "the day of the LORD" or "in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens," Genesis 2:4, which uses *yom* for the entire creation week). Which sense fits 1:1-2:3 is exactly what is contested; the lexical fact itself -- that the word carries more than one attested sense elsewhere in Scripture -- is not.
- **טוֹב** (*tov*, "good") forms the refrain "and God saw that it was good," which can be checked directly against the text: it appears after day one (1:4), day three (1:10, 1:12 -- twice), day four (1:18), day five (1:21), and day six (1:25, then intensified to "very good" at 1:31) -- seven occurrences across six days. It is conspicuously absent after day two (1:8), the only day that lacks it. A classical Jewish reading, Genesis Rabbah 4 (per Rabbi Samuel bar Nahman, as reported secondhand here -- see Sources and Limits), proposes that the work begun on day two (separating the waters) was not completed until day three, which is why day three alone receives the refrain twice. Whether that or some other explanation accounts for the gap, the gap itself is a directly checkable textual fact, independent of any explanation offered for it.
- **בָּרָא** (*bara*, "create") and **עָשָׂה** (*asah*, "make") are both used of God's activity in this passage -- *bara* at the cosmos (1:1), at the first living creatures (1:21), and at humanity (1:27); *asah* at, among other places, the expanse (1:7), the lights (1:16), and the land animals (1:25). Terry Mortenson's survey of the two verbs, while arguing from a young-earth position elsewhere in the same piece, states plainly that the Hebrew Bible elsewhere uses *bara* and *asah* interchangeably for the same objects, and concludes that "the verbs alone cannot tell us how God created and how long He took to create." The occurrence pattern above is directly checkable; what it shows about the author's intent, if anything, is not something this lesson resolves.

## Literary Design

These notes are about how Genesis 1:1-2:3 is built, not about what its construction is meant to teach theologically.

**A widely noted correspondence, not a settled authorial intent.** Each of the six days follows a recognizable pattern -- a divine command ("And God said..."), the thing commanded coming to be, in several days a naming or blessing, the evaluation "and God saw that it was good" (missing only on day two, as the Context section above notes), and the closing formula "and there was evening, and there was morning -- the *n*th day." Within that repeated pattern, a further correspondence is widely reported in the literature: the works of days one through three (light/darkness; sky/sea; dry land and vegetation) are commonly described as regions or domains being formed out of the "formless and void" state of 1:2, while the works of days four through six (sun/moon/stars; sea and flying creatures; land animals and humankind) are commonly described as those same domains being filled with the things that inhabit them -- day and night filled with sun and moon (1 with 4); sky and sea filled with flying and swimming creatures (2 with 5); land filled with land animals and, distinctly, with humankind (3 with 6). Meredith Kline's own statement of this reading, in the article that is also this lesson's primary source for the literary-framework position below, puts the correspondence this way: "As frequently noticed, the two triads run in parallel with obvious correlation of their successive members" -- Kline's own wording attributes the observation to the wider field rather than claiming to originate it. Two days do not fit a strict one-item-per-day version of this scheme: day three both gathers the waters *and* brings forth vegetation, and day six both forms land animals *and* forms humankind, so each of those two days carries two distinct acts rather than one. Whether that doubling is itself part of the pattern's design (an intensification toward the two climactic acts of the whole account, humanity's creation and the land's provision) or a sign that the "neat" two-triad scheme is being imposed on a text that resists it this cleanly is a judgment call this lesson leaves to the reader; the correspondence itself -- which days pair with which, and where the scheme does and does not fit evenly -- is checkable directly against the text above.

**The refrain's own shape.** Besides the "good" refrain already noted in Context, the "evening and morning" formula itself is worth observing directly: it closes every one of the six days (1:5, 1:8, 1:13, 1:19, 1:23, 1:31) and then is conspicuously absent from the account of the seventh day (2:1-3), which has no "evening and morning" closing line of its own. That absence is a plain textual fact; what, if anything, follows from it is a question the Positions section below shows real readers answering differently.

**The chapter's close as an inclusio.** 1:1 opens "the heavens and the earth"; 2:1 closes "the heavens and the earth were completed in all their vast array" -- the same word pair bracketing the whole unit. 2:2-3 then shifts the passage's own verbs: where days one through six are built on "God said" and "God made" (or "created"), the seventh-day paragraph is built on "God rested," "God blessed," and "God sanctified" -- no new creative act is narrated on the seventh day, only cessation, blessing, and setting apart. That shift in verbs is itself the textual basis for this passage's connection to Exodus 20:8-11 and Hebrews 4, both reported in this lesson's reviewed connections.

## Positions

Four named readings of what the "day" (*yom*) of Genesis 1 refers to are reported below, each from its own tradition's or scholar's own stated argument. None is ranked or adjudicated by this lesson.

**Young-earth / ordinary 24-hour day.** The Westminster Confession of Faith states that it pleased God to create the world "in the space of six days" (WCF 4.1); the Confession's own text does not define the length of those days, and later interpreters within the Reformed tradition have disagreed about what its drafters intended by the phrase. What is directly attested is that a substantial line of post-Reformation Reformed theologians read it, and read Genesis 1 itself, as six ordinary days -- a 2010 Ordained Servant (Orthodox Presbyterian Church) survey reports John Calvin, Louis Berkhof, Robert L. Dabney, E.J. Young, and Robert L. Reymond each arguing in their own published work for literal, calendar-length days. The contemporary young-earth case is argued at length by Answers in Genesis; Simon Turpin's statement of the position for that ministry points to the days being numbered and consistently paired with "evening" and "morning," and to Exodus 20:11's "six-plus-one" pattern (reported in this lesson's Exodus 20:8-11 connection), as textual grounds for reading *yom* as an ordinary day throughout the passage.

**Day-age.** Hugh Ross, founder of the old-earth creationist ministry Reasons to Believe, states in his own published statement of the position that the Hebrew noun *yom* has "four distinct literal definitions, one of which is a long, finite period of time," and that this sense is "clearly implied in Genesis 2:4" -- the same verse the Context section above notes uses *yom* for the entirety of the creation week rather than for a single day. On Ross's reading, the "evening and morning" formula marks that each named period had a definite beginning and ending, not that each period was itself short, so the days remain sequential and non-overlapping, as the young-earth reading also holds, while not being understood as ordinary 24-hour days.

**Literary framework (non-chronological).** Meredith Kline's own statement of this position, in the article already cited in Literary Design above, holds that "the sequence of the days is ordered not chronologically but thematically" and that "the creation 'week' is to be understood figuratively, not literally." On Kline's reading the six days are a literary arrangement -- the two parallel triads already described -- rather than a record of the actual temporal sequence or duration of God's creative acts; the "evening and morning" formula belongs to the literary register of the account rather than to ordinary chronological time. Kline's view (developed in a 1958 *Westminster Theological Journal* article and restated with Lee Irons in *The Genesis Debate*, 2001) is reported by Matthew H. van Luik's survey as the position's principal modern proponent, with Bruce Waltke and Mark D. Futato also named as holding versions of the same framework reading.

**Gap (ruin-reconstruction).** A fourth position, now a minority view but historically significant and still held by some, reads a time gap between 1:1 ("In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth") and 1:2 ("the earth was formless and void"), with the six days of 1:3 onward describing a re-forming of an already-existing but ruined earth rather than an original creation from nothing. The Christian Research Institute's survey traces the view's modern form to the Scottish theologian Thomas Chalmers (1814), its popularization to G.H. Pember's *Earth's Earliest Ages* (1876), its wide circulation through the *Scofield Reference Bible* (1909), and a later restatement by Arthur C. Custance (1970); the same survey reports that the view's influence declined after the rise of young-earth, six-day creationism associated with Henry Morris in the 1960s. On this reading the length of the six days themselves is a separate question from the position's main claim, which is about what 1:2 describes, not primarily about how long each subsequent day lasted.

**Where these readings agree and differ.** All four hold that God is the sole creator of the heavens and the earth and that the six-day, one-Sabbath structure of the text is meaningful, not incidental. They differ on what kind of claim the word *yom* is making in 1:3-31: an ordinary day (young-earth), a long era (day-age), a literary unit not keyed to ordinary time at all (framework), or a question that is secondary to what, if anything, 1:2 describes as already existing before the six days begin (gap). Readers weighing these against each other have the lexical fact already reported in Context -- that *yom* itself carries more than one attested sense in the Hebrew Bible -- as one piece of evidence among several each position brings to the text.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key: the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Genesis 2:1-3. The heavens and the earth are "completed," and "by the seventh day God had finished the work He had been doing; so on that day He rested from all His work" (2:2, BSB). God then "blessed the seventh day and sanctified it, because on that day He rested from all the work of creation that He had accomplished" (2:3, BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the passage marks the end of work with a declared evaluation ("very good," 1:31) before it marks a designated cessation (2:2-3) -- completion is stated, then rest follows, rather than rest arriving because the work ran out or the worker was exhausted. Inference: for readers whose own working lives were not structured around guaranteed rest, a text that pictures God himself declaring work finished and then resting by design, not by necessity, would have been a notable claim about how work and rest relate to each other. The Context section above supplies what is directly in the text; nothing in it lets this lesson say more about the first audience's own working patterns than that.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Declaring a stage of work complete, on purpose, before moving to what is next -- rather than letting work run together undifferentiated until exhaustion forces a stop -- is itself part of doing the work well, not a concession made after the work is done.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a deliberate evaluation of work as finished, followed by a deliberate, planned cessation. Not carried forward: any claim about the length of the "days" (the Positions section above reports that as unsettled), and any claim that this passage by itself commands the reader to a weekly rest pattern -- that command is Exodus 20:8-11's, reported in this lesson's reviewed connections, not this passage's own.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, drawn from narrative pattern, not a command addressed to the reader by this passage itself. Promise scope: none claimed; nothing in 2:1-3 is treated here as a promise to the reader.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. A small software team finishes a stage of a project. Before starting the next stage, the team lead reviews the finished work against the stated acceptance criteria and marks it done -- not perfect, not final for all time, but good enough to build on -- and the team has a standing, scheduled day each week with no on-call rotation and no deployments, set apart in advance rather than taken only when someone happens to be too tired to keep working.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: ordering of work (a wisdom pattern, not a command). One faithful response is to hold the review and the rest as two separate, both deliberate acts -- naming a stage of work good enough to build on is not the same act as stepping away from it, and neither should be skipped in favor of the other.

**Cautions.** This is one analogy, tested by the learner, not a general claim that all work must be staged the way software projects are, and not a claim that every worker controls their own schedule enough to set apart a weekly rest day by choice -- shift workers, caregivers, and others whose rest is constrained by circumstances outside their control are not in view here, and this example should not be used to suggest that an irregular rest pattern is itself a failure. The passage does not address those situations, and a reader using this bridge with someone in one of them should say so rather than press the analogy past where it holds.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. The *tov* ("good") refrain appears seven times across the six days but is absent after day two (1:8). What would you need to see to decide whether that gap is a meaningful compositional choice or an incidental feature of the text?
2. If *yom*'s range of attested senses (ordinary daylight, a 24-hour day, a longer indefinite period) is a genuine lexical fact, what besides the word itself would have to settle which sense applies in Genesis 1 -- and is that evidence inside this text or outside it?
3. The forming/filling correspondence pairs day one with day four, day two with day five, and day three with day six -- but days three and six each contain two distinct creative acts where the other four days contain one. What would you need to decide whether that doubling is the pattern's own design or a strain on a tidy scheme imposed from outside the text?
4. Exodus 20:11 is the earliest later text in Scripture to state a reason tied to the creation week. If you set aside every position reported above and asked only what Exodus 20:8-11 itself requires a reader to believe about the creation week, what would that be -- and is it more, or less, than Genesis 1:1-2:3 states on its own?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate what happens across the seven days of Genesis 1:1-2:3 in order, saying what happens, not what the "days" themselves are. (b) State, in the text's own words, what closes each of the first six days and what is different about the seventh. (c) Name one place elsewhere in Scripture where this passage's language or pattern visibly reappears, and say what kind of connection it is: a quotation, a named or explicit reference, an echo, or something else.

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** Outline how you would teach this passage in five minutes, with at least one point in each segment you could cite to a source or a verse. A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) where the passage sits and the plain sequence of the six days and the seventh (see Context); (1:00-2:00) how the passage is built -- the day-by-day refrain, the forming/filling correspondence stated as a widely noted observation, and the places where that scheme does and does not fit evenly (see Literary Design); (2:00-3:30) the crux word, *yom*, read first for its own lexical range and then through the four named positions, without picking one for the class (see Context and Positions); (3:30-4:30) where this passage's language and pattern travel later in Scripture, naming each connection's evidence label, not just listing the reference (see the reviewed connections in prompt 5); (4:30-5:00) the boundary, stated on purpose as the last thing taught (prompt 4).

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first; the point of this prompt is that you do the forming, not that you pick from a list. If you are stuck, here are two worked candidates running in different directions, each stated at full strength. First: the literary-framework and day-age readings are late accommodations to modern geology and cosmology, imported into the text rather than drawn from it, and the plain, numbered, "evening and morning" structure of the passage was read as ordinary days by nearly every interpreter, Jewish and Christian, before the nineteenth century raised the question of the earth's age -- so the burden of proof sits with the non-literal readings, not the literal one. Before you answer it, note what the young-earth position's own sources (Turpin; the Ordained Servant survey of Calvin, Berkhof, Dabney, Young, and Reymond) actually claim, and what, if anything, they claim about pre-nineteenth-century interpreters outside the Reformed tradition this lesson did not survey. Second, running the other direction: the ordinary-day reading treats *yom*'s range of senses as settled by the "evening and morning" formula alone, but Genesis 2:4 uses the same word for the entire creation week in the very next paragraph, which is at minimum evidence that this author was capable of using *yom* non-literally within the same composition -- so the formula cannot by itself carry the weight the ordinary-day reading puts on it. Before you answer this one, decide whether 2:4's use of *yom* is doing the same kind of work as 1:3-31's six uses, or a different kind -- the Context section above states the lexical fact but does not make that argument for you.

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Genesis 1:1-2:3 does not, by its own wording, state how many years ago creation occurred. That figure, where it is calculated at all, comes from later genealogical material and the chronological assumptions brought to it -- a separate exercise from reading this passage's own words. (b) Genesis 1:1-2:3 does not adjudicate among the four positions reported above. The text itself does not use a phrase equivalent to "these are ordinary days" or "these are long ages" to settle the question; each position's case is built from this passage together with other textual and extratextual evidence, reported in the Positions section as each position's own argument, not as this passage's own stated verdict.

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection from this passage and defend it: name its type (for example quotation, explicit reference, allusion, motif, parallel, doctrinal synthesis), give its evidence label (explicit, strong, plausible, or devotional), and point to the words in both texts that support that grade. The reviewed connections attached to this lesson sit at different tiers, which is the point of the exercise:
