Genesis 30:1-24 is a list of births told as a contest. Rachel, still childless, says to Jacob, "Give me children, or I will die!" (30:1), and he answers in anger (30:2). The sisters give Jacob their servants Bilhah and Zilpah and name the four sons as if keeping score: "I have wrestled with my sister and won" (30:8). Mandrakes are traded for a night with Jacob (30:14-16), Leah bears two more sons and a daughter, Dinah, and then "God remembered Rachel" (30:22): she names her son Joseph. This lesson prints each naming speech, checks which echoes of other passages are really in the text, and reports four questions on which named Jewish and Christian readers differ. It continues this curriculum's Genesis 29 lesson ("Rachel and Leah") and does not repeat it. The chapter touches infertility and grief, and two women who are given to a man without a recorded word of their own. If that is close to your own life, the note at the end of Position 2 lists resources. If grief has become thoughts of ending your life, in the United States you can call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org, at any hour. Every BSB quotation was checked by script against this app's shipped corpus (see Sources and Limits).

## Context

**What happens, in order.** Rachel's demand and Jacob's answer (30:1-2); Bilhah bears Dan and Naphtali (30:3-8); Leah, who "had stopped having children" (30:9; compare 29:35), gives Zilpah, who bears Gad and Asher (30:9-13); the mandrakes are traded for a night (30:14-16); Leah bears Issachar, Zebulun and Dinah (30:17-21); Rachel bears Joseph (30:22-24).

**Where this unit sits.** Stage 5 of this app's eleven-stage lens (Genesis 12 through Malachi; machine-readable `chapters: ["1.12"]`). Leah's first four sons were born in 29:32-35. Jacob's request to leave Laban (30:25) begins the next unit, and Benjamin's birth and Rachel's death come in 35:16-20.

**The eight births and the words given with them.** Each line is the BSB text. Seven of the eight children receive a spoken explanation; one does not.

**Key words, read as observations.** Every count below was made by this lesson's own script over the BSB, case-insensitively and on word boundaries.

- **"Or I will die" (30:1).** Bible Hub's interlinear parses the Hebrew as a participle with "I" (*metah anoki*). The Pulpit Commentary renders it "literally, and if not, I am a dead woman." The BSB phrase "or I will die" occurs in only one other verse, Jeremiah 37:20, in an unrelated plea.
- **"Am I in the place of God?" (30:2).** The same three Hebrew words (*hatachat Elohim anoki*, with a different form of "I" at 50:19) return when Joseph says to his brothers, "Do not be afraid. Am I in the place of God?" (50:19). In the BSB the phrase "in the place of God" occurs only in these two verses. The Cambridge Bible links them from both ends and adds 2 Kings 5:7 ("Am I God, killing and giving life...?"). See the connections.
- **"Build a family" (30:3).** Rachel's "so that through her I too can build a family" repeats Sarai's "perhaps I can build a family by her" (16:2). In the BSB these are the only two verses with "build a family." Bible Hub's interlinear shows the same Hebrew form in both, *'ibbaneh* (Strong's 1129, Niphal), "I shall be built up." The Cambridge Bible: "The same figure of a house is used by Sarah, referring to Hagar in Genesis 16:2." The BSB also leaves out a phrase the Hebrew has in 30:3: Bilhah will bear "on my knees" (*'al birkay*). The Cambridge Bible calls it "the trace of a primitive ceremony of legitimatization and adoption."
- **"Wrestled" (30:8).** The noun and verb are *naphtulei Elohim niphtalti* (Strong's 5319 and 6617), which Bible Hub's interlinear glosses word by word as "With wrestlings," "of God," "I have wrestled." The Cambridge Bible gives two senses for "of God": an intensive ("mighty wrestlings") or "strugglings in prayer for God's blessing," and adds, "The original meaning has probably been lost." Bible Hub summarizes the verb (6617, five occurrences) as "To twist, to be cunning, to wrestle."
- **"Hire" (30:16, 30:18).** Leah tells Jacob, "I have hired you with my son's mandrakes" (30:16), and names Issachar from the same root (*sakhar*, Strong's 7936 and 7939): "God has rewarded me" (30:18). Ellicott calls the name a "double play" on both events.
- **"God" and "the LORD."** In 30:1-23 every divine name in the BSB is "God." "The LORD" appears once, in Rachel's last words, "May the LORD add to me another son" (30:24). Leah's naming speeches in 29:32-35 used "the LORD" three times. What readers make of this shift is Position 4.
- **"Remembered" (30:22).** In the BSB the exact phrase "God remembered" occurs in three verses: Genesis 8:1 (Noah), Genesis 30:22 (Rachel) and Revelation 16:19 (Babylon). In Genesis, God is the subject of a past-tense "remembered" at 8:1, 19:29 ("He remembered Abraham") and 30:22. The Cambridge Bible on 8:1 lists 19:29 and 30:22 as places where "The same expression occurs." In Hannah's story the wording is "the LORD remembered her" (1 Samuel 1:19); Bible Hub's interlinear shows the same verb, *zakhar* (Strong's 2142), there and at 30:22.
- **Joseph's name (30:23-24).** Rachel's two sentences give the name two sounds. "Has taken away" is *'asaph* (622), and "May add" is *yoseph* (3254), which the interlinear parses as a jussive, a wish; that is why the BSB words 30:24 as a wish. The Cambridge Bible calls the two "traditional interpretations." Keil and Delitzsch derive the name "taking away" and "adding" at once. Gill: "the name Joseph is composed of two words."
- **Dinah.** "Dinah" occurs in nine BSB verses, all in Genesis: 30:21, seven verses of chapter 34, and 46:15. She is the only daughter of Jacob named at birth. Her birth notice gives no reason for her name.
- **Bilhah and Zilpah.** Neither woman speaks anywhere in the BSB. The narrator names them (Bilhah as a person in ten verses, Zilpah in seven), and their mistresses name their sons.

**Mandrakes and Song of Songs 7:13.** The Hebrew word is *duda'im* (Strong's 1736). Bible Hub's concordance lists seven occurrences: five in Genesis 30:14-16, one at Song of Songs 7:13 ("The mandrakes send forth a fragrance"), and one at Jeremiah 24:1, where the same form names "two baskets of figs." The Brown-Driver-Briggs lexicon on the same page glosses the Genesis and Song uses as "mandrakes, as exciting sexual desire, and favouring procreation." Ellicott says the plant's Hebrew name "arose from the popular belief that it was a specific against barrenness." In BSB English, "mandrake" occurs in four verses, the three of Genesis 30:14-16 and Song 7:13. What the plant was, and why Rachel wanted it, is Position 3.

## Literary Design

**A contest in names.** The sisters' words read as a running score. Rachel: "God has vindicated me" (30:6) and "I have wrestled with my sister and won" (30:8). Leah: "How fortunate!" (30:11) and "the women call me happy" (30:13). Benson reads the first pair as victories "at law" and "in battle." Matthew Henry: "she takes pleasure in giving them names that carry in them marks of rivalry with her sister." Poole answers Rachel's "won" with the count: it "was not true; for her sister exceeded her both in the number of her children, and in her propriety in them." The text records the claims and does not score them.

**A bargain inside the contest.** In the one scene with dialogue between the sisters (30:14-16), Leah speaks the line that sums up the rivalry from her side: "Is it not enough that you have taken away my husband?" (30:15). Genesis Rabbah 72:3 gives Leah's line as a proverb, "will you take from my beard for my grandfather?", which the translator's note explains as Leah saying, "Jacob loves you more than he loves me." Calvin, on the same verse, thinks the sisters were perhaps not quarrelsome by nature, but that God "suffered them to contend with each other, that the punishment of polygamy might be exhibited to posterity." The Genesis 29 lesson reported readers on the polygamy itself (its Position 4); that is not repeated here.

**Opened wombs, both sisters.** The chapter's frame repeats one phrase. Of Leah: "When the LORD saw that Leah was unloved, He opened her womb; but Rachel was barren" (29:31). Of Rachel: "He listened to her and opened her womb" (30:22). Between the two, every human scheme is tried: the servant women, the mandrakes, the night that was bought. Keil and Delitzsch read the sequence as the narrator's point: after "Rachel continued barren in spite of the mandrakes, the writer justly observes (Genesis 30:17), 'Elohim hearkened unto Leah,' to show that it was not from such natural means as love-apples, but from God the author of life." That is a reading of the order of events. The order itself can be checked: Rachel receives the mandrakes in 30:15, Leah conceives in 30:17, and Rachel conceives only in 30:23, with the conception credited to God (30:22).

**Dinah's single line.** After seven sons and seven explanations, "Leah gave birth to a daughter and named her Dinah" (30:21). Jamieson-Fausset-Brown: "The inferior value set on a daughter is displayed in the bare announcement of the birth." Benson says she is mentioned "because of the following story concerning her, chap. 34"; the Pulpit Commentary agrees and adds that she "may not have been Jacob's only daughter." The Cambridge Bible notes that Genesis 32:22 counts Jacob's "eleven children" (BSB "eleven sons") without her and reports the suggestion "that her name here is a later editorial insertion to harmonize the list of children with the story of ch. 34." Rashi, following the Talmud (Berakhot 60a), reads her name from *dan*, "judge": Leah "set herself up as judge" against herself, reasoning that if the child were a son, "my sister Rachel cannot be even the equal of any of the handmaids"; she prayed, "and its sex was changed." Genesis Rabbah 72:6 tells a version in which "Rachel's prayer" changed the child. The text gives no explanation of its own.

**A wish answered later.** Rachel's "May the LORD add to me another son" (30:24) is answered in 35:16-18, where she dies giving birth to Benjamin. Keil and Delitzsch: "The fulfilment of this wish is recorded in Genesis 35:16." Ellicott: "And God did add to her another son, but the boon cost her her life."

## Positions

Four questions follow. Each is reported through named readers in their own words, and none is decided here.

**1. Rachel's demand and Jacob's anger (30:1-2).** The narrator says Rachel envied her sister and Jacob "became angry" (30:1-2). The text gives no verdict on either.

*What Rachel meant by "or I will die."*
- *A proverb.* Genesis Rabbah 71:6 lists "one who has no children" among four "considered as though they were dead," citing this verse, and Rashi follows it. Ellicott: "There is an Oriental proverb that a childless person is as good as dead; and this was probably Rachel's meaning, and not that she should die of vexation."
- *Grief that could end her life.* Benson: "I shall fret myself to death." Gill says she meant that "her life would be a burden to her," and adds "not that she should destroy herself, as some have imagined." Ramban reads it as a threat made in grief: if Jacob did not pray until she had children, "she would mortify herself because of grief. In her envy she spoke improperly."
- *Impatience, set against Hannah.* Jamieson-Fausset-Brown: "Rachel's conduct was sinful and contrasts unfavorably with that of Rebekah ... and of Hannah." Benson: "Rachel envied, Hannah wept ... Let Hannah be imitated, and not Rachel." Ellicott, in the same note, calls her words a "painful petulance" but adds, "she was very young, and a spoiled wife." Rashi, following Genesis Rabbah 71:6, softens "envied": "She was envious of her because of her good deeds."

*Whether Jacob's anger was right.*
- *A just rebuke.* Calvin: "This was a holy indignation, by which Jacob maintained the honor due to God, while he corrected his wife." Poole: anger "for the sin against God, in which case anger is not only lawful, but necessary." Matthew Henry: "Jacob loved Rachel, and therefore reproved her for what she said amiss. Faithful reproofs show true affection." The Pulpit Commentary: "not without just cause."
- *The wrong answer to a person in distress.* Genesis Rabbah 71:7 has God answer Jacob: "Is that the way one answers the distressed? As you live, your sons are destined to stand before her son." (The translator's note identifies "her son" as Joseph.) In the same passage Rachel asks whether Isaac did not pray for Rebekah, and Jacob replies, "My father had no children, but I have children." Rashi gives the same exchange. Ramban asks, "Do not the righteous pray on behalf of others?" and cites the midrash as taking Jacob "to task."
- *A failure of prayer on both sides.* Keil and Delitzsch say that Rachel, instead of praying "as Rebekah had done," spoke "in passionate displeasure," and that Jacob "wanted the power which he might have possessed, the power of prayer, in firm reliance upon the promise of the Lord."
- *Anger at a threat, or after prayer had failed.* Ramban gives two readings of his own. In the first, Rachel "spoke in the manner of yearning women who are loved, thus attempting to frighten him with her death," and Jacob answered "in order to admonish her and shame her"; then Rachel, "seeing that she could not rely upon Jacob's prayer, then went to pray on her own behalf," which is why 30:22 says God "listened to her." In the second, which he calls "a correct interpretation," "it is impossible to think that Jacob did not pray on behalf of his beloved wife who was barren, however his prayer was not accepted," and his anger answered her claim that his prayer must succeed.

These readers differ on whether Jacob's words were a rebuke she needed or a wound she did not.

**2. Bilhah and Zilpah: how readers judge the arrangement (30:3-13).** The text says Rachel "gave Jacob her servant Bilhah as a wife, and he slept with her" (30:4), and that Leah "gave her servant Zilpah to Jacob as a wife" (30:9). Neither servant speaks, in this chapter or anywhere in the BSB. No word of hers is recorded, and nothing is said of what she wanted. Laban had given each of them to his daughter (29:24, 29:29). The narrator still counts the sons as theirs: "the sons of Jacob born to Bilhah" (46:25), "born to Zilpah" (46:18). Later the narrator calls them Jacob's wives (37:2) and once Bilhah his "concubine" (35:22). This curriculum's Genesis 16 lesson handled Sarai's gift of Hagar the same way.

- *A recognized custom of adoption.* The Cambridge Bible takes "bear upon my knees" as a rite in which "the child being received on the knees of the parent was regarded as being accepted into the family." Ellicott: "All that can be said in her defence is, that the custom existed, and, perhaps, because it was distasteful to the wife, was looked upon as meritorious." Genesis Rabbah 72:5, on Leah, reports Rabbi Abahu: God "saw that her intention was exclusively for the sake of Heaven, to produce tribes."
- *The servants' children as the mistress's property.* Poole, on 30:3: "for as servants, so their work and fruit, were not their own, but their masters'." As in Genesis 16, this explanation states plainly the slave-holding premise behind the arrangement.
- *A wrong, repeated from Sarai.* The Pulpit Commentary says Rachel was "resorting to the sinful expedient of Sarah," which "in the case of her distinguished relative, had been so palpably condemned." Calvin calls the union with Bilhah "an adulterous connection" and says it "obtained the honor of wedlock" only "by special privilege"; he adds that "it was his will that they who were born from this faulty connection, should yet be reckoned among the legitimate children." Jamieson-Fausset-Brown: "The evil lies in the system, which being a violation of God's original ordinance, cannot yield happiness." Matthew Henry: "admire the wisdom of the Divine appointment, which joins together one man and one woman only."
- *A reward the mother misread.* Leah names Issachar because "God has rewarded me for giving my maidservant to my husband" (30:18). Poole: "Thus she mistakes the answer of her prayers for a recompence of her error." Calvin: "Leah ignorantly boasts that her son was given to her as a reward of her sin." Ellicott takes her side: "the shame ideally was that she should have been forced thus to buy her husband's attentions."

What can be checked: Rachel and Leah propose, Jacob agrees, and Bilhah and Zilpah are the objects of the proposals, not parties to them. Every judgment of their part above is a reader's, and no reader quoted here asks what the two women wanted.

**3. What were the mandrakes, and why did Rachel want them (30:14-16)?** The text says only that Reuben "found some mandrakes in the field" and that Rachel asked for them (30:14).

- *A fertility charm.* Barnes: the plant "is to this day supposed to promote fruitfulness of the womb. Rachel therefore desires to partake of them." Keil and Delitzsch say they "were used by the ancients, as they still are by the Arabs, as a means of promoting child-bearing." The Cambridge Bible: "It was supposed to act as a love-charm." The Pulpit Commentary calls the belief an "Oriental superstition" and adds that Rachel was "not exempt from the prevailing superstition."
- *Delight, not medicine.* Ramban rejects the fertility reading for Rachel: "The correct interpretation is that Rachel wanted the duda'im for delight and pleasure, for Rachel was visited with children through prayer, not by medicinal methods." He reports Ibn Ezra's doubt about the belief ("I do not know it") and allows only that the stem, which Reuben did not bring, is what "people say is an aid to pregnancy": "And if the matter be true, it is by some peculiar effect, not by its natural quality." Gill thinks they were "lovely and delightful flowers the boy picked up" and finds "no foundation" for the fertility notion, "for Rachel, who had them, did not conceive upon having them."
- *A plant no one can name with certainty.* Calvin: "Respecting the kind of fruit mentioned, I leave nothing certain to adduce. That it was fragrant is gathered from Canticles 7:13." Genesis Rabbah 72:2 records three rabbis' answers: mandrakes, barley, or "the hackberry tree." Rashi says "violets" and calls it jasmine in Arabic; Ramban corrects him and prefers Onkelos's "mandrakes."
- *A trade that cost and gained.* In Genesis Rabbah 72:3, Rabbi Shimon says that because Rachel "demeaned the righteous one," "she will not enter the grave with him." Rabbi Elazar says Leah "lost mandrakes and gained two tribes and primacy." Genesis Rabbah 72:5 reports Rabbi Levi: "Come and see how pleasing the mediation of mandrakes was," since through them "two great tribes in Israel were produced – Issachar and Zebulun," and it cites Song of Songs 7:13 (7:14 in Hebrew numbering).

*A reading aimed at a people.* Calvin draws a lesson from this episode aimed at Jews. He writes that "the Jews glory in extolling the origin of their own nation," grants that "they do excel in the dignity of their ancestors, as Paul testifies," and says that the Spirit "purposely aimed at beating down this arrogance" by telling of so "mean and abject" a beginning. Genesis 30 says nothing about later Jews. Genesis Rabbah 72:2, on the same verse, reads the episode as praise of Reuben, who took only "from the ownerless." The Second Vatican Council, in general terms that do not cite this chapter, taught that "the Jews should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God, as if this followed from the Holy Scriptures" (*Nostra Aetate* 4, 1965, addressed to Catholic teaching and preaching).

**4. Why "God" through 30:23, and "the LORD" at 30:24?** The count is in Key Words. Readers explain it in three ways.

- *The women's spiritual state.* Keil and Delitzsch: "It is to be noticed, that Rachel speaks of Elohim only, whereas Leah regarded her first four sons as the gift of Jehovah." Of Leah's later sons they say her use of Elohim shows a heart from which the covenant God's grace "had been forced out" by jealousy. Barnes says that at Joseph's birth "the thankful Rachel rises from Elohim, the invisible Eternal, to Yahweh, the manifest Self-existent." Ellicott reads Rachel's earlier "Elohim" partly as a sign that "she was herself half an idolater (Genesis 31:19)."
- *The plans' origin.* The Pulpit Commentary, following Hengstenberg, explains "Elohim" at Dan's birth by Rachel's "consciousness that in a large measure her handmaid's son was rather the fruit of her own impious device than the gift of Jehovah." It also reports, and calls "undue severity," the view of Vatablus and Calvin that Rachel used God's name like a hypocrite.
- *Two written sources.* The Cambridge Bible explains the double meaning of Joseph's name by two source documents: "According to E, the name means âsaph Elohim, 'God hath taken away'; according to J, it means yôsêph Jehovah, 'may Jehovah add.'" The Pulpit Commentary reports this view ("a trace of the Jehovistic pen (Tuch, Bleek, et alii)") and answers it: "rather an outcome of the higher spiritual life of Rachel." Keil and Delitzsch reply that the double derivation "may be explained, without the hypothesis of a double source," by Rachel looking first back and then forward.

The first two readings answer the third directly: Keil and Delitzsch and the Pulpit Commentary each name the source hypothesis and set their reading against it. The Cambridge Bible does not argue against theirs. This lesson does not decide between them.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge. It models the shape; it is not an answer key. It deliberately does not build from 30:3-4 or 30:9, where two women are given to a man.

**Passage element.** Genesis 30:1-2 (BSB). Rachel: "Give me children, or I will die!" Jacob: "Am I in the place of God, who has withheld children from you?"

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the narrator also credits God with opening wombs (29:31, 30:22), so Jacob's sentence agrees with the narrator, and it answers a cry of grief with a statement of limits. Inference: a first audience might have heard both the agreement and the cost. Genesis Rabbah 71:7 hears the cost; Calvin hears the rebuke. The narrator states no moral.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** A true statement about what you cannot do may still be the wrong first answer to someone in distress.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a person in grief asking another for what he cannot give, and an answer that is accurate and angry. Not carried forward: infertility as such, the marriage, the rivalry, and any claim about what God withholds or gives.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a narrative pattern applied by analogy; not a command. Promise scope: none. Nothing here promises that a gentler answer will remove anyone's grief.

**Modern domain and situation.** Friendship. A friend whose job application has failed calls you, upset, and says you must get them hired at your company. You have no say in hiring there.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: compassion with honesty. One faithful response: say first that you are sorry and stay on the call; then say plainly that you cannot decide the hiring, and offer the help you can give, such as reading their next application.

**Cautions.** This is about an ordinary disappointment between friends. It does not compare anyone's loss to infertility. If a friend in distress speaks of not wanting to live, the 988 Lifeline (call or text 988) is the right next step, not a study note.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. Rachel's "build a family" (30:3) repeats Sarai's (16:2) in English and in Hebrew. What does the repetition let a reader compare, and what does Genesis 30 leave out that Genesis 16 tells (Hagar's flight, the angel)?
2. Genesis Rabbah 71:7 has God rebuke Jacob, and Calvin calls his anger "a holy indignation." What in 30:1-2 itself could each reader point to?
3. Bilhah and Zilpah never speak. Is a reader's silence about them a choice, too? What would it look like to read 30:3-13 with them in view, without inventing words the text does not give them?
4. Keil and Delitzsch read the mandrake scene as proof that children came from God, "not from such natural means as love-apples"; Ramban thinks Rachel never wanted the plant as medicine. Which details in 30:14-23 does each reading rely on?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 30:1-24 in about eight steps, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) Name the eight children in order with their mothers, and say which child receives no explanation. (c) Give the two Hebrew words Rachel's speeches play on in Joseph's name (30:23-24).
2. **Five-minute outline.** (0:00-1:30) 30:1-13 and the 16:2 echo; (1:30-3:00) the mandrakes, Dinah and Joseph; (3:00-4:30) Positions 1-4, with the pastoral note as written; (4:30-5:00) the connections.
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: reading Jacob's answer as unkind ignores that he states a truth the narrator confirms in 30:22. Weigh it against Genesis Rabbah 71:7 and Ramban. A second candidate, running the other way: calling his anger "holy" (Calvin) makes a grieving woman the only one at fault. Weigh it against Ramban's second reading, in which Jacob had prayed and failed.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) It does not record what Bilhah or Zilpah thought, said or wanted; every view in Position 2 is a reader's. (b) It does not say whether the mandrakes had any effect, or what Rachel believed about them. (c) It does not explain Dinah's name or why her birth notice is so short. (d) It does not say why the divine name changes at 30:24; each reading in Position 4 is an explanation, not the text's own statement. (e) It does not connect Naphtali's "wrestled" to Jacob's wrestling in Genesis 32; see Prompt 5.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** The connections attached to this lesson:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genesis 16:2-3 | motif | strong | "Build a family" in only these two BSB verses, the same Hebrew form, and a wife giving her servant; Genesis 30 does not name Sarai |
| 1 Samuel 1:1-20 | motif | strong | A loved, childless wife, a rival with children, prayer, "remembered" (the same Hebrew verb), a son; commentators and Genesis Rabbah 73:1 pair them, 1 Samuel does not name Rachel |
| Genesis 50:19 | motif | strong | The same three Hebrew words, "Am I in the place of God?", from Rachel's son; the Cambridge Bible links them both ways |
| Luke 1:25 | allusion | plausible | Elizabeth's "taken away my disgrace" uses the Septuagint's verb and noun from 30:23; but the idiom recurs (Isaiah 4:1), so the source cannot be fixed |

Choose one row and defend it, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` for a candidate. Three candidates were considered and not authored. (a) **Naphtali and Genesis 32:24-28.** Rachel's "won" (30:8) and Jacob's "you have prevailed" (32:28) render the same verb (*yakhol*, 3201), and *Elohim* stands in both verses. But the wrestling verbs differ: 30:8 uses *patal* (6617), and 32:24-25 use *'abaq* (79), which Bible Hub lists only in those two verses. Barnes reads "wrestlings of God" as "with God, in prayer," without citing chapter 32, and Ellicott rejects the link: "the connection of the two ideas of wrestling and prayer is taken from Genesis 32:24, where an entirely different verb is used." No source read for this lesson argues for the link, so it stays a question. (b) **Song of Songs 7:13.** It shares the word *duda'im* and the fragrance, and Calvin and Genesis Rabbah 72:5 cite it, but it uses mandrakes for a lover's delight and says nothing of Rachel, Leah or children. (c) **Matthew 2:18.** "Rachel weeping for her children" is Matthew's quotation of Jeremiah 31:15, already carried by conn-matt2-jeremiah-rachel; it concerns Rachel as the mourning mother of later Israel, not this birth, and is not duplicated here.

## Sources and Limits

- **BSB text and counts.** Every BSB quotation in this lesson and its four connection files was printed by a Node script from `web/public/bible/BSB/*.json` before drafting and checked afterward by a second script asserting that each quoted fragment is a literal substring of the verse cited. Every count in Key Words was made by script, case-insensitively and on word boundaries, and the script output is kept with the lesson's working files.
- **Hebrew and Greek.** Word-level Hebrew comes from Bible Hub's interlinear pages for Genesis 30, 16:2, 32:24, 32:28, 50:19 and 1 Samuel 1:19, and its lexicon pages for 1736, 6617 and 79. The Septuagint of Genesis 30 and Isaiah 4 and the Greek editions of Luke 1:25 were read on Bible Hub. No critical edition or printed lexicon was consulted.
- **Christian commentaries.** Calvin (his editor's notes are not used), Matthew Henry (Concise), Barnes, Benson, Poole, Gill, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, Ellicott, the Cambridge Bible, the Pulpit Commentary and Keil and Delitzsch were read on Bible Hub pages, fetched raw, saved and searched for each quoted string. Hengstenberg, Vatablus, Tuch, Bleek, Kalisch and Delitzsch are reported only as those commentaries cite them. On the Bible Hub page for 30:1, an unlabeled note after Jamieson-Fausset-Brown calls Rachel's words "bordering upon blasphemy"; because its author could not be identified on the page, it is not used.
- **Jewish sources.** Rashi (Rosenbaum and Silbermann), Genesis Rabbah 71-73 (The Sefaria Midrash Rabbah, 2022) and Ramban (Chavel) were read through Sefaria's API. The translator's note to Ramban locates the rebuke of Jacob at Bereshith Rabbah 71:10; in the Sefaria edition of Genesis Rabbah it is 71:7. The Talmud passage Berakhot 60a is reported only as Rashi cites it. Translators' bracketed notes are not quoted as the sources' words.
- **Not read.** No modern critical commentary on Genesis (Westermann, Wenham, Sarna, Alter), no Eastern Orthodox source and no Catholic commentary on this chapter was read. That is a gap in sourcing, not a claim about those readers. No source read argues for the Naphtali and Genesis 32 link; Ellicott implies some commentators make it but names none.
- **Safety resources.** RESOLVE's free community and virtual support groups were confirmed on resolve.org, RAINN's call, text and chat routes on rainn.org (both read in a browser, since automated requests are blocked), and the 988 Lifeline's call, text and chat routes on 988lifeline.org, all on 2026-10-08. These are US services. The lesson gives no medical advice about infertility or mandrakes.
- **Scope.** Jacob's wages and flocks (30:25-43), the departure from Laban (Genesis 31), Benjamin's birth and Rachel's death (35:16-20), Dinah's story (Genesis 34) and Jacob's blessings (Genesis 49) belong to their own units.
