Twenty years after Esau said, "I will kill my brother Jacob" (27:41), the brothers meet. Jacob sees Esau "coming toward him with four hundred men" (33:1), lines up his family, and bows to the ground seven times. Then "Esau, however, ran to him and embraced him, threw his arms around his neck, and kissed him. And they both wept" (33:4). The rest of the chapter is talk: Jacob calls Esau "my lord" and himself "your servant," presses a gift on him, says that seeing his face "is like seeing the face of God" (33:10), declines to travel with him, and promises to come to him at Seir. Esau goes to Seir; Jacob goes the other way, to Succoth and then to Shechem, where he buys land and sets up an altar. The narrator says almost nothing about what either brother felt or intended. Readers have supplied it in very different ways, beginning with a scribal mark over the word "kissed him." This lesson checks the chapter's wording by script and in the Hebrew, reports five disputed questions in named readers' own words without settling them, and grades four connections, among them the plot at Shechem that Joshua 24:32 names again and John 4:5 recalls.

## Context

**What happens, in order (33:1-20).** Jacob sees Esau and his four hundred men and divides the children among Leah, Rachel and the two maidservants, putting "the maidservants and their children in front, Leah and her children next, and Rachel and Joseph at the rear" (33:1-2). He goes ahead and bows seven times (33:3). Esau runs to him, embraces and kisses him, and they weep (33:4). Esau asks about the women and children, who come forward and bow (33:5-7). Esau asks about the droves Jacob sent; Jacob says they were sent "To find favor in your sight, my lord"; Esau says, "I already have plenty, my brother"; Jacob insists and Esau accepts (33:8-11). Esau proposes to travel together, then to leave some men; Jacob declines both, citing frail children and nursing animals, and says he will come "to my lord at Seir" (33:12-15). Esau returns to Seir, and Jacob goes to Succoth, builds a house and shelters (33:16-17), then arrives "safely at the city of Shechem," buys "the plot of ground where he pitched his tent" from "the sons of Hamor, Shechem's father, for a hundred pieces of silver," and sets up an altar he calls El-Elohe-Israel (33:18-20).

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens puts Genesis 12 through Malachi in stage 5. The chapter follows this curriculum's lesson on Genesis 32 (the wrestling at Peniel), which already authored the connection between 32:30 and 33:10 (conn-gen32-face-gen33-10), and the lesson on Genesis 27 (the stolen blessing), which covers how readers judge Jacob's taking of the blessing. The events at Shechem in Genesis 34 belong to the next lesson. Genesis 33 has the same verse numbers in Hebrew and English Bibles, but Genesis 32 does not: Sefaria and Ramban cite it one verse ahead, and every reference here is given in BSB numbering.

**Key words, read as observations.** Every BSB count below was made by this lesson's own script over the app's text, case-insensitive and on word boundaries. Hebrew counts were made by script over Sefaria's Hebrew text after removing vowel points.

- **"My lord" and "your servant."** In Genesis 32-33 the BSB has "my lord" six times (32:18; 33:8; 33:13; twice in 33:14; 33:15) and "my master" twice (32:4, 32:5). The Hebrew has the same word, *adoni*, in all eight places. "Your servant" occurs five times in the two chapters, four times to Esau (32:4, 32:18, 32:20, 33:5) and once to God (32:10), and Jacob calls himself "his servant" once more in 33:14. Esau's one form of address in chapter 33 is "my brother" (33:9).
- **Bowing (33:3, 33:6-7).** The BSB has "bowed" four times in chapter 33: Jacob "bowed to the ground seven times" (33:3), and the maidservants, Leah and her children, and Joseph and Rachel "bowed down" (33:6-7). The Hebrew has one verb in all four places, *hishtachavah* (Strong's 7812 in Bible Hub's interlinear for 33:3). "Seven times" occurs in Genesis only at 33:3.
- **The dotted word (33:4).** In the Hebrew on Sefaria every letter of *vayishakehu*, "and he kissed him," carries a dot above it. The Cambridge Bible gives the Masoretic note as "All of it punctuated," and Positions 1 reports what readers make of it.
- **"face" (33:10).** The BSB has "face" twice in chapter 33, both in 33:10, and "face of God" occurs in the BSB only there. The Hebrew run of *panim* from Genesis 32 into 33:10 is traced in this curriculum's Genesis 32 lesson.
- **"My blessing" (33:11).** Where the BSB has "Please accept my gift," the Hebrew word in Bible Hub's interlinear is *birkhati*, "my blessing" (Strong's 1293). The Cambridge Bible: "The 'gift' is the material side of the 'blessing'," and the word is used for a gift elsewhere (it lists Joshua 15:19, 1 Samuel 25:27 and 2 Kings 5:15). Ramban explains the usage: a gift is called a blessing "because he sends it from that with which G-d had blessed him."
- **"safely" (33:18).** Jacob had vowed to "return safely" (28:21); the Hebrew differs (*be-shalom* there, *shalem* here), but Keil hears in 33:18 "an evident allusion" to the vow. The Septuagint, Calvin and the King James Version ("Jacob came to Shalem," as Ellicott prints it) take *shalem* as a place name.
- **A hundred *qesitah* (33:19).** The BSB renders the price "a hundred pieces of silver." The Hebrew coin, *qesitah* (Strong's 7192), occurs, as Poole and the Cambridge Bible note, only here, in Joshua 24:32 and in Job 42:11. Its value is unknown. The Septuagint has "a hundred lambs" (ἑκατὸν ἀμνῶν); Onkelos has a word that Rashi glosses "good ones" and Radak "young lambs"; Genesis Rabbah 79:7 records "One hundred precious stones, one hundred sheep, one hundred sela."

## Literary Design

**The order of the family (33:1-2).** Jacob puts "Rachel and Joseph at the rear." Rashi, following Genesis Rabbah 78:8: "The more behind — the more beloved." Radak: "the most beloved he placed last, hoping that Esau's anger had been blunted by the time he met them." Calvin says Jacob "offers himself as a victim" by going first, but also that "it is not to be denied, that he was excessively indulgent to Rachel."

**Reunions in Genesis.** The gestures of 33:4 recur. Laban "ran out to meet him. He embraced him and kissed him" (29:13). Joseph "threw his arms around his brother Benjamin and wept" (45:14), and later "embraced" his father "and wept profusely" (46:29). Ibn Ezra uses the Joseph scenes as evidence for the plain sense of 33:4 (Positions 1).

**Two destinations.** The chapter ends with the brothers going different ways: "So that day Esau started on his way back to Seir" (33:16), "but Jacob went on to Succoth" (33:17). They appear together once more, at their father's burial: "his sons Esau and Jacob buried him" (35:29). Then Esau "moved to a land far away from his brother Jacob," because "the land where they stayed could not support them because of their livestock" (36:6-7).

## Positions

Five questions follow. Each is reported through named readers in their own words, and none is adjudicated. Nothing in a position's statement is this lesson's own conclusion.

**1. The kiss (33:4): sincere, false, or a doubtful word?** The narrator gives the action and the tears but no motive. The dots over *vayishakehu* are the oldest prompt to read more into it.

- **Sincere (some add that the dots mark it as remarkable).** Genesis Rabbah 78:9 records Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar's rule for dotted words. Where the letters and dots are equal, as here, "it teaches that at that moment he was overcome with mercy and he kissed him with all his heart." Sifrei Bamidbar 69, which Rashi cites, gives the same view in the name of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai, in Sifrei's words: "It is a known halachah that Esav hates Jacob, but his mercy gained the ascendancy at that time and he kissed him with all his heart." (The two collections name different rabbis for nearly the same words; this lesson reports both names.) Ibn Ezra reads the plain meaning the same way: "It is obvious from the plain meaning of the text that Esau did not intend to harm Jacob, the proof being and they wept, as Joseph did with his brethren." Ellicott: "Whatever may have been Esau's intention when he started, no sooner does he see his brother than the old times of their childhood return to his heart, and he is overcome with love." Gill takes the dots as "directing the attention of the reader to it, as something wonderful and worthy of observation."
- **Not wholehearted, or hostile.** Sifrei Bamidbar 69 opens the passage with the contrary view: "He did not kiss him with all his heart." In Genesis Rabbah 78:9 Rabbi Yannai answers Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar: "If so, why is it dotted over it? Rather, it teaches that he did not come to kiss him, but rather to bite him," and Jacob's neck "was transformed into marble." The weeping then becomes "this one wept over his neck, and that one wept over his teeth." Targum Pseudo-Jonathan (Etheridge's translation) reflects the story in its account of the weeping: "Esau wept on account of the pain of his teeth which were shaken; but Jakob wept because of the pain of his neck." Its text has no bite and no marble.
- **A doubtful reading in the text.** The Cambridge Bible: "Probably the text was at an early date uncertain." The Pulpit Commentary reports, citing Rosenmüller and Keil, that Origen appears "not to have found it in his codices."

**Objections each reading faces.** *Against the sincere reading,* Rabbi Yannai's question stands in the midrash: if the kiss was simple, "why is it dotted over it?" *Against the hostile reading,* Ellicott: "For such an ill-natured supposition there is no warrant whatsoever"; Keil and Delitzsch, quoting Delitzsch, say the dots question "the genuineness of this kiss; but without any reason"; and Ibn Ezra says the midrash on the dots "is good for them that are drawn from the breasts" (his translator explains: children). *Against the textual reading,* the rabbinic sources and Gill treat the dots as deliberate marks to be interpreted, and Barnes says the dots, "seemingly intimating a doubt of the reading or of the sincerity of Esau, are wholly unwarranted." This lesson does not choose between them.

**2. "It is like seeing the face of God" (33:10): flattery, sincerity, or theology?** Jacob's words in the BSB are "I have seen your face, and it is like seeing the face of God, since you have received me favorably." The link between this verse and 32:30 is graded in conn-gen32-face-gen33-10 and is not repeated here. The question here is what Jacob meant by saying it to Esau.

- **A courtly compliment or exaggeration.** The Cambridge Bible: "an elaborate compliment, such as is found in 1 Samuel 29:9, 2 Samuel 14:17, where David is compared to an angel of God." Ellicott: "There is much of Oriental hyperbole," but it "clearly conveyed the idea that Esau was using his power as generously and lovingly as is the wont of God." Radak calls it "an exaggeration, of course," comparing the woman of Tekoa's words to David.
- **A sincere statement.** Calvin first states the difficulty, that Jacob "does wrong to God, in comparing Him with a reprobate man; and that he speaks falsely," and answers that "he does not speak feignedly, nor pretend something different from what he has in his mind." Keil and Delitzsch: "Jacob might say this without cringing," since, in the words of a writer they quote without naming, he "must have discerned the work of God in the unexpected change in his brother's disposition towards him."
- **A statement about God's favor.** Keil and Delitzsch: "In thy countenance I have been met with divine (heavenly) friendliness." Barnes: Esau's kindness "was a type and proof of the kindness of the All-provident." Calvin: "Esau being thus changed, was no obscure figure of the favor of God." The Pulpit Commentary lists further readings, including Quarry's: "as he had already seen the face of God and his life was preserved, so now he had seen the face of Esau, and the anticipated destruction had not been inflicted on him."
- **A message meant to deter.** Rashi has Jacob say, "I have seen your guardian angel," so that Esau "should be afraid of him." Radak likewise says Jacob "alluded to that nocturnal encounter in order to make Esau afraid to harm him," and Genesis Rabbah 75:10 says Jacob would mention God "to scare him and to frighten him."

Readers also differ on the verse's last clause, "since you have received me favorably." Rashi reads it as "You are reconciled with me" and "you have agreed to pardon my offence." Ramban rejects that reading, since "it was not advisable for Jacob to bring iniquity to remembrance," and takes the clause as Esau's pleasure in accepting the gift.

This lesson does not collect objections for this question; readers can weigh the positions directly.

**3. Jacob's deference: does it undo Isaac's blessing?** Isaac blessed Jacob: "May you be the master of your brothers, and may the sons of your mother bow down to you" (27:29). In Genesis 33 the bowing runs the other way, with the same Hebrew verb (Context; conn-gen27-bow-gen33-3). "Master" in 27:29 is *gevir*, not *adoni*. Readers judge the deference in four ways.

- **A fault.** Genesis Rabbah 75:11: "At the moment that Jacob called Esau 'my lord,' the Holy One blessed be He said to him: 'You abased yourself and called him "my lord" eight times. As you live, I will establish eight kings from his descendants before your descendants,'" citing Genesis 36:31. (The eight matches this lesson's Hebrew count of *adoni* in chapters 32-33.) Ramban reports another rebuke from Genesis Rabbah 75: Esau "was going his way, and you send him messengers, and say to him, Thus saith thy servant Jacob!"
- **Courtesy, to an elder brother or to a powerful chief.** Ramban: the respect Jacob showed by saying "my lord" and "thy servant" "was due to it being the custom of the younger brother to give recognition and respect to the firstborn as if he were his father," so that he was "acting towards Esau as if the effect of that sale was nil," "in order to remove the hatred from his heart." Genesis Rabbah 75:5 tells of Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi correcting a letter to the emperor to read "From your servant Yehuda," and answering, "In what way am I better than my ancestors? Did he not say this: 'So says your servant Jacob'?" Keil and Delitzsch call the address "simply an act of courtesy suited to the circumstances, in which he paid to Esau the respect due to the head of a powerful band," adding that Jacob "could not conscientiously have maintained the attitude of a brother, when inwardly and spiritually, in spite of Esau's friendly meeting, they were so completely separated the one from the other." The Pulpit Commentary: "possibly politeness and humility will suffice."
- **Humility before the brother he wronged.** Keil and Delitzsch on 33:3: "He humbled himself before him as the elder, with the feeling that he had formerly sinned against him." The Pulpit Commentary reports that Kalisch and Alford took "my lord" as "the sign of a guilty conscience," though it judges that reading "perhaps too much." Ellicott: Jacob sought "to propitiate him; for the cause of the quarrel had been Jacob's usurpation of Esau's right of precedence as the first born."
- **The blessing deferred, not undone.** Calvin on 27:40: "Jacob was so far from having obtained dominion over his brother, that on his return from Padan-aram, he suppliantly tendered him his obedience," and so the prophecy concerns "things at that time far distant." On 33:3 he puts the objection, "that in this manner he depreciated his right of primogeniture," and answers that Jacob "knew that the effect of the benediction was deferred to its proper season, and was, therefore, now like the decaying seed under the earth." Calvin also proposes that the bows were worship of God as well as, "not ... simply," honor to his brother; he adds that Jacob "also approached as a suppliant." His nineteenth-century editor disagrees in a footnote, quoting Rivetus: "This, therefore, was civil reverence ... which did not derogate from the spiritual right and prerogative of the covenant entered into with Jehovah."

This lesson does not collect separate objections for this question; the readings already answer one another, and readers can weigh them directly.

**4. "until I come to my lord at Seir" (33:14): deception, prudence, or a promise kept later?** Jacob says he will follow slowly "until I come to my lord at Seir." The next verses send Esau to Seir and Jacob to Succoth and Shechem (33:16-18). Genesis never records Jacob going to Seir.

- **A pretext.** Calvin: "In these words he promises what he was not intending to do," and "truth is so precious to God, that he will not allow us to lie or deceive, even when no injury follows." The Geneva Bible's note: "He promised that which (as it would seem) he did not plan to do."
- **Prudence with a courteous form.** Rashi: "He mentioned a much longer journey, for he really intended to go only as far as Succoth," reasoning, "If he means to do me harm let him wait to do so until I reach his abode at Seir." In the same comment Rashi adds the future reading given below. Ramban: "This was not a vow on the part of Jacob that he will come to him," and the rabbinic reading that he meant to keep his distance "also was wise counsel." The Cambridge Bible on 33:12: "It was wiser to separate, while they were still amicable. The natures remain the same; Esau's thoughtless, Jacob's calculating."
- **A real intention, carried out later or unrecorded.** Keil and Delitzsch: "these words are not to be understood as meaning that he intended to go direct to Seir; consequently they were not a wilful deception for the purpose of getting rid of Esau," since Jacob "may have thought of paying a visit to Esau in Seir." Gill: "more probably he did go thither then, or quickly after; though the Scripture makes no mention of it." Barnes: on reaching Canaan "it will be his first duty to return the fraternal visit of Esau." Calvin's editor, in a footnote: "There appears no reason to doubt that Jacob said what he meant."
- **A promise for the future.** Genesis Rabbah 78:14: "Rabbi Abbahu said: We reviewed the entire Bible and did not find that Jacob ever went to Esau at Mount Seir. Is it possible that Jacob, [who] was truthful, [nevertheless] deceived him? When, then, did he go to him? In the future." It cites Obadiah 1:21 (in the BSB, "The deliverers will ascend Mount Zion to rule over the mountains of Esau"). Rashi and Ramban repeat this as a midrash beside their plain readings.

**Objections each reading faces.** *Against the pretext reading,* the Pulpit Commentary calls it an "almost incredible hypothesis" (naming Calvin), and Gill says "that he should tell a lie is not likely." *Against prudence,* Calvin says the excuses Jacob gives "are true; nevertheless he introduces them under false pretexts." *Against an unrecorded visit,* Benson: "Certainly it is very doubtful whether he ever did," and Rabbi Abbahu's starting point is that no such journey is written. *Against the future reading,* Poole prefers the view that Jacob "did perform this promise, though the Scripture be silent of it, as it is of many other historical passages." This lesson does not decide between them.

**5. What does "El-Elohe-Israel" (33:20) name?** The BSB reads, "There he set up an altar and called it El-Elohe-Israel," and Sefaria's English glosses the name "El, God of Israel." Bible Hub's Strong's-linked KJV line tags the phrase as one name (Strong's 415); its interlinear parses three words (410, 430, 3478).

- **A name for the altar that praises God.** Rashi: "It does not mean that the altar was named 'The God of Israel'," but Jacob named it so "that the praise of God might be mentioned when people called it by its name," like Moses' altar "The LORD Is My Banner" (Exodus 17:15). Ibn Ezra and Radak read it the same way, and Calvin calls the altar "a memorial and pledge of all the visions and promises of God." Keil and Delitzsch: "God (the mighty) is the God of Israel," given "with reference to the name given to him" at Peniel. Rashbam: "the same G'd Who had been the G'd of someone called Yaakov, continued in His role as the G'd of Yisrael."
- **Jacob called upon God there.** The Septuagint (Swete) has ἐπεκαλέσατο τὸν θεὸν Ἰσραήλ, "called upon the God of Israel" (this lesson's rendering). Onkelos in Sefaria's English: "[worshiped upon it before] the Almighty is [the] God of Yisrael." Ibn Ezra reports that Saadiah read "He called to the Lord," and Poole gives "called upon El-elohe-Israel." The Pulpit Commentary disagrees: "not invoked upon it ... but named it."
- **God called Jacob "El."** The Talmud (Megillah 18a) reads the verse "And He, i.e., the God of Israel, called him, Jacob, El," because otherwise "it should have specified the subject of the verb and written: And Jacob called it El." Rashi reports it and keeps to the plain sense; Ramban accepts Rashi's plain sense and also embraces the Talmud's reading "by way of the Truth," his term for the mystical tradition. In Genesis Rabbah 79:8 Reish Lakish reads it as Jacob's claim, "You are God of the heavenly, and I am the ruler of the earthly," and Rav Huna answers in his name with a rebuke that ties the claim to the next chapter.
- **A standing stone behind the altar.** The Cambridge Bible notes that the verb "set up" is used elsewhere "not of an altar, but of a 'pillar' or upright stone," and that "many prefer here to read 'pillar'." It suggests that "The origin of some sacred stone, well known to the Israelites, was thus accounted for," and also calls the name "a profession of faith in the one true God made at the moment when Jacob comes to dwell among the heathen Canaanites."

This lesson does not collect objections for this question beyond the disagreements quoted; readers can weigh the positions directly.

**Esau in later tradition.** This chapter was read by later Jews living under Rome. Genesis Rabbah 78:15 says that "Rabbeinu" ("our teacher"), going up to the imperial court, "would look at this portion and would not take a Roman with him" (the translator notes that the text says "Aramean"). Ramban, telling the same story of Rabbi Yannai, explains that "this was the section of the exile," whose counsel the sages followed. Malka Simkovich (TheTorah.com) traces how Esau became a figure for Rome and later for Christianity, and observes that the figure means that "Rome, and even more powerfully, Christianity, is a sibling to Judaism." She adds: "when we last see Esau in the Hebrew Bible, he has reconciled with his brother Jacob, and they live near one another, if not among one another, in peace." Genesis last shows the brothers together at Isaac's burial and then living apart (Literary Design). The Christian reading of Esau as the Jews, and the Second Vatican Council's general rejection of presenting Jews as rejected or accursed, are reported in this curriculum's Genesis 25 lesson and is not repeated here.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, as a model of the shape, not an answer key. It does not build on the kiss, the bows or the promise at Seir, whose meanings Positions 1, 3 and 4 show readers do not agree on.

**Passage element.** Genesis 33:9-11 and 33:16-17. Esau says, "I already have plenty, my brother" and "Keep what belongs to you." Jacob answers, "Please accept my gift that was brought to you, because God has been gracious to me and I have all I need," and Esau accepts. Then Esau goes to Seir and Jacob to Succoth.

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the brother who lost the blessing declines the gift and calls Jacob "my brother"; the brother who took it insists on giving it anyway; and the meeting ends with each going his own way. Inference (this lesson's own): for a first audience, the scene shows a meeting after a breach that ends without violence, without an apology or pardon stated in plain words (Rashi reads a pardon into 33:10; Positions 2) and without the brothers living together. Whether it shows a full reconciliation is disputed (Positions 1 and 4).

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When siblings meet after a serious breach, what each one gives or refuses, and how far apart they then live, may be their own to decide; the meeting does not have to settle everything.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: two adult siblings, an old dispute over an inheritance, an attempt to give something back, and a parting. Not carried forward: Esau's four hundred men, the angels, the altar, the land at Shechem, and any judgment on which modern sibling is "Jacob" or "Esau."

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a narrative pattern applied by analogy, not a command. Promise scope: none. "God has been gracious to me" (33:11) is Jacob's statement about himself; it promises no outcome to anyone else.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family. Years ago one brother persuaded their mother to change her will in his favor, and the two have not spoken since. Their mother's death brings them into the same room.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: honest restitution without pressure. One faithful response is for the brother who gained to offer back what he took, plainly and in writing, to say once what he did, and then to let his sibling decide whether to accept the offer and what contact, if any, should follow.

**Cautions.** The bridge does not say that the wronged sibling must accept, forgive or resume closeness; Esau's choices are his own, and this curriculum's lesson on Matthew 18:15-35 reports how readers distinguish forgiveness from reconciliation. Questions about wills and estates need a licensed attorney, not a study note. If a family relationship involved abuse, safety comes before any meeting. The Hotline's page on forgiveness asks, "Does forgiveness mean I have to see the abuser or reconcile with them?" and answers, "No, it does not." The National Domestic Violence Hotline notes that "a survivor is not going to be in a place to forgive abuse if they are still in danger," and suggests "making a safety plan first." In the United States the Hotline is free and confidential: call 1-800-799-7233, text START to 88788, or visit thehotline.org. A pastor or counselor you trust can also help.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. Jacob says "my lord" and Esau says "my brother" (33:8-9). What does each form of address let you say about how each brother saw the meeting, and what does it not let you say?
2. The narrator reports that "they both wept" (33:4) and gives no reason. Which readings in Positions 1 need to supply a reason, and which do not?
3. Jacob prays, "Please deliver me from the hand of my brother Esau" (32:11), and then says Esau's face is "like seeing the face of God" (33:10). Read the two verses side by side. How does each reading of 33:10 in Positions 2 account for both?
4. Rabbi Abbahu says Genesis never shows Jacob at Seir. What would count as evidence for or against a visit the text does not record?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 33:1-20 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) Say what is unusual about the Hebrew word for "kissed him" in 33:4. (c) Give the two names for the place of 33:18 ("safely" and "Shalem") and why they differ.
2. **Five-minute outline.** (0:00-1:00) the meeting, the gift and the parting; (1:00-2:00) the key words: "my lord," the bows, "my blessing," the *qesitah*; (2:00-3:30) the kiss and the face of God (Positions 1 and 2); (3:30-4:15) the deference and Seir (Positions 3 and 4); (4:15-5:00) El-Elohe-Israel and the four connections.
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own first. If you are stuck, here is one: calling 33:14 a deception ignores that Jacob never says when he will come, so no promise was broken. Weigh it against Calvin's words and Ramban's "This was not a vow." A second candidate, running the other way: calling it prudence ignores that the next two verses send Jacob in the opposite direction. Weigh it against Keil and Delitzsch's note on Jacob's destination.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) It does not record an apology. Jacob speaks of favor and a gift (33:8-11); the BSB has no "birthright" or "blessing" in the chapter, though the Hebrew of 33:11 calls the gift "my blessing." (b) It does not say Esau forgave. He runs, kisses and weeps, but no form of "forgive" occurs in the BSB of Genesis 32-33 (script count), though Rashi reads "you have received me favorably" (33:10) as "you have agreed to pardon my offence," and Ramban rejects that reading. (c) It does not say Jacob dug a well at Shechem; "Jacob's well" is first named in John 4:6.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** The connections attached to this lesson:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Joshua 24:32 (from 33:18-19) | explicit_reference | explicit | Names the plot "that Jacob had purchased from the sons of Hamor, Shechem's father, for a hundred pieces of silver," the same terms as 33:19 and a shared Hebrew run |
| John 4:5-6 (from 33:18-19) | explicit_reference | strong | Names "the plot of ground that Jacob had given to his son Joseph"; the purchase is 33:19, but the gift is 48:22 and Joshua 24:32 joins them |
| Luke 15:20 (from 33:4) | motif | plausible | The running welcome, embrace and kiss; Gill and the Cambridge Bible link the verses, but the Greek idiom recurs (29:13, 46:29, Acts 20:37) |
| Genesis 33:3-7 (from 27:29) | contrast_reversal | plausible | The same Hebrew verb for bowing, now done by Jacob to Esau; Calvin draws the contrast, but Genesis 33 does not refer back |

Choose one row and defend it, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` for a candidate. Candidates this lesson chose not to author:

- **Genesis 32:30 to 33:10 ("face of God").** Already authored as conn-gen32-face-gen33-10 in the Genesis 32 lesson; not duplicated.
- **Acts 7:16.** Stephen names the tomb "bought from the sons of Hamor at Shechem," but under Abraham's name. The row conn-gen23-tomb-acts7-16 already records that difficulty.
- **Genesis 12:6-7 (Abram's altar at Shechem).** Barnes, Keil and Ellicott compare Jacob's altar with Abram's at Shechem. A real echo of place and act, but it belongs with the Genesis 12 lesson's rows and was left unauthored here.
- **Obadiah 1:21.** Genesis Rabbah 78:14 reads Jacob's "until I come to my lord at Seir" as fulfilled in Obadiah's verse. That is a midrashic reading with no shared wording beyond Esau's name and mountain: `no_warrant_yet`.
