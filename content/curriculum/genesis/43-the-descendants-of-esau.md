Genesis 36 is the chapter most readers skip. It has two headings, four lists of names, eight kings and a closing sentence: "Esau was the father of the Edomites" (36:43). Yet it has drawn sharp arguments. A Talmudic story has a king of Judah mocking two of its verses as pointless. Maimonides names its lists among passages "misunderstood by many persons." Spinoza used its heading "These are the kings who reigned in the land of Edom before any king reigned over the Israelites" (36:31) in his case that Moses did not write the Pentateuch. This lesson counts what the chapter says, reports what named Jewish and Christian readers conclude about why it is here and when its king list was written, and lays out one word nobody is sure how to translate. It follows the chapter's names forward to Chronicles, Deuteronomy, Exodus and Obadiah, and grades each link. Every quotation attributed to the BSB, here and in the four connection files, was printed from this app's shipped corpus by a script and checked again after drafting (see Sources and Limits).

## Context

**What the chapter contains, in order.** A heading, "This is the account of Esau (that is, Edom)" (36:1), introduces Esau's three wives and five sons, "who were born to him in the land of Canaan" (36:5). Esau then "moved to a land far away from his brother Jacob. For their possessions were too great for them to dwell together" (36:6-7), and "So Esau (that is, Edom) settled in the area of Mount Seir" (36:8). A second heading, "This is the account of Esau, the father of the Edomites, in the area of Mount Seir" (36:9), introduces his sons and grandsons (36:10-14) and fourteen chiefs descended from him (36:15-19). Next come "the sons of Seir the Horite, who were living in the land," with their seven chiefs (36:20-30); eight kings, each after the first reigning "in his place" when the one before dies (36:31-39); and eleven chiefs listed "according to their families and regions" (36:40-43). Then the story turns: "Now Jacob lived in the land where his father had resided, the land of Canaan. This is the account of Jacob" (37:1-2).

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens files Genesis 12 through Malachi under stage 5 (`chapters: ["1.12"]`). Esau was last seen at Isaac's burial (35:29). The Genesis 25:19-34 lesson treats the twins' birth, the oracle "the older will serve the younger," Malachi 1:2-3 and Romans 9, and the later use of Esau and Jacob as figures for peoples. That material is not repeated here.

**Key words and counts, read as observations.** Each count comes from a script over the app's BSB, case-insensitive and on word boundaries.

- **"Esau."** The name occurs 24 times in 17 verses of chapter 36, and in 67 verses of Genesis. No verse of Genesis after 36:43 names him.
- **"Edom" and "Edomites."** "Edom" occurs in 9 verses of the chapter (11 in Genesis, the first at 25:30), and "Edomites" in two (36:9, 36:43). A clause equates the man and the nation three times: "Esau (that is, Edom)" (36:1, 36:8, 36:19). The Cambridge Bible calls the words "A gloss"; Barnes reads such clauses as "marks of an early or artless simplicity of style."
- **"Chief" and "chiefs."** These occur 15 times in 10 verses of the chapter; the Hebrew in 36:15 is *alluf* (Sefaria). Every other use of the English word in Genesis is a different office, such as Abraham's "chief servant" (24:2). "chiefs of Edom" occurs in four BSB verses: Genesis 36:43, Exodus 15:15, and 1 Chronicles 1:51 and 1:54. The Hebrew of Exodus 15:15, *alufei Edom*, is the phrase of Genesis 36:43.
- **"This is the account of."** The heading occurs in ten verses of Genesis. Only Esau has two headings in his own name, "the account of Esau" (36:1, 36:9); Noah's second (10:1) is the account of "Noah's sons." Barnes says the repetition "does not necessarily indicate diversity of authorship."
- **Twelve again.** The Cambridge Bible notes that Esau's descendants by his three wives in 36:9-14, "like the sons of Ishmael and Israel, are 12 in number," and that Amalek, "the son of Esau's concubine, Timna, is excluded from this list of twelve."

**The wives' names do not match the earlier chapters.** Genesis 26:34 names "Judith daughter of Beeri the Hittite and Basemath daughter of Elon the Hittite," and 28:9 names "Mahalath... daughter of Abraham's son Ishmael." Genesis 36:2-3 names "Adah daughter of Elon the Hittite, Oholibamah daughter of Anah," and "Basemath daughter of Ishmael." Ibn Ezra says the daughter of Elon "had two names." Rashi, following the midrash, says Esau renamed Oholibamah "Judith" to suggest "that she had abandoned idol-worship, so that he might deceive his father." Ramban objects that Rashi "has not explained" why her father's name differs too. Keil holds that "Moses availed himself of genealogical documents for Esau's family and tribe, and inserted them without alteration," and explains the names by surnames and new names at marriage.

**An echo of Abram and Lot.** The clause "their possessions were too great for them to dwell together" (36:7) closely resembles 13:6, where Abram and Lot "had so many possessions that they were unable to coexist." That is an observation about the English; no source read for this lesson draws the comparison.

**The Horites.** These are the people Esau's line displaced: "The Horites used to live in Seir, but the descendants of Esau drove them out" (Deuteronomy 2:12; again at 2:22). Ibn Ezra explains why Genesis lists them: "Scripture lists the children of Seir to inform us that they were of a different lineage from the children of Esau. The Bible notes this because Israel was prohibited from contending with the children of Esau."

**Timna and Amalek (36:12, 36:22).** "Timna, a concubine of Esau's son Eliphaz, gave birth to Amalek" (36:12), and "Timna was Lotan's sister" (36:22), so she is a Horite. In the Talmud (Sanhedrin 99b), King Manasseh mocks these verses as teaching nothing. The Talmud answers with a story: Timna "sought to convert. She came before Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and they did not accept her." She became a concubine in Abraham's family instead, Amalek was born of her, and the Talmud gives that as the reason Israel later suffered at Amalek's hand: "that they should not have rejected her." (Quoted words are those the Koren translation marks as the Talmud's own.) Rashi and Ramban read 36:12 as showing "how eager people were to attach themselves to his descendants," that is, Abraham's.

**Amalek after Genesis.** The name returns when "the Amalekites came and attacked the Israelites at Rephidim" (Exodus 17:8), and the LORD says, "I will utterly blot out the memory of Amalek from under heaven" (17:14). Deuteronomy says, "Remember what the Amalekites did to you," for they "attacked all your stragglers; they had no fear of God" (25:17-18). In 1 Samuel 15:3, Samuel orders Saul to "devote to destruction" the Amalekites, naming "men and women, children and infants." That is a command to kill a whole people. This lesson reports it only as part of the name's history and does not take up the moral questions it raises, which belong to a lesson on 1 Samuel 15. Readers differ on whether Timna's son is the ancestor of the Exodus Amalekites, since Genesis 14:7 already mentions "the whole territory of the Amalekites" in Abraham's day. Ellicott doubts so wide a people "sprung from a concubine of Eliphaz." Keil holds that 14:7 "does not imply that the tribe was in existence in Abraham's time."

Two medieval readers use the genealogy to limit that command. Maimonides (Guide 3.50) says the Seirite clans are listed in full "because of one particular commandment," the one against Amalek. The Seirites, he says, were joined to Esau's line and came to be called Amalekites, and if their genealogy "had not been described in full they would all have been killed, contrary to the plain words of the commandment." Ramban writes that toward Esau's descendants "we have been commanded not to abhor them" or "take their land," but "the son of the concubine is not part of the descendants of Esau." Both read the list as a boundary around a severe law.

**"Do not despise an Edomite, for he is your brother" (Deuteronomy 23:7).** The book that says to blot out Amalek also says this, and adds, "The third generation of children born to them may enter the assembly of the LORD" (23:8). Edom's record with Israel was not friendly. When Moses asked passage in the name of "your brother Israel" (Numbers 20:14), "Edom refused to allow Israel to pass through their territory" (20:21). The Pulpit Commentary notes that "though the former had refused permission to the Israelites to pass through their land," they were "connected with Israel by a bond of kindred - for he is thy brother."

**Edom later in Scripture.** Under David "all the Edomites were subject to David" (2 Samuel 8:14); in Jehoshaphat's day "there was no king in Edom; a deputy served as king" (1 Kings 22:47); under Jehoram "Edom rebelled against the hand of Judah and appointed their own king" (2 Kings 8:20). Obadiah's oracle "about Edom" (1:1) names "the mountains of Esau" and "Teman" (1:8-9), a name from 36:11, and charges "violence against your brother Jacob" (1:10).

**1 Chronicles 1:35-54.** Chronicles repeats the lists in the same order. A script comparison of the BSB finds every personal and place name of Genesis 36:10-43 in 1 Chronicles 1:35-54 except the wives Adah and Basemath and the spelling Hemam (Homam in 1:39). The king-list heading is the same sentence in both (Genesis 36:31; 1 Chronicles 1:43), and its Hebrew consonants and vowels are identical on Sefaria (only the cantillation marks differ). Keil calls 1:43-50 "a verbally exact repetition of Genesis 36:31-39," apart from small changes he lists. Chronicles adds one clause, "Then Hadad died" (1:51), which matters to Positions (2).

**Anah's "hot springs" (36:24): one word, four readings.** The BSB reads: "This is the Anah who found the hot springs in the wilderness as he was pasturing the donkeys of his father Zibeon." The BSB has no footnote on the verse. The Hebrew word is *ha-yemim* (Sefaria); Benson says it "occurs nowhere else in Scripture."

- *Mules.* The KJV has "found the mules." The Talmud (Pesachim 54a) reports Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's view that the first "mule was in the days of Anah," and Rashi, Saadiah (per Ibn Ezra) and Calvin follow it. Targum Pseudo-Jonathan writes it into the verse: Anah "coupled the onagers" with she-asses, "and after a time found mules" (Etheridge). Calvin reads it as blame: "Moses marks this unnatural pursuit in Anah." Keil objects that "mules are פּרדים," a different word, and that the verb "does not mean to invent."
- *Mighty men, or giants.* Onkelos renders the word, in Ibn Ezra's report, as "mighty men," and Ibn Ezra links it to the Emim of Deuteronomy 2:10. Ramban sets out both readings, then explains the Targum: Anah fought off raiders who "wished to rob him of the asses," and Ramban ends, "This is correct." Benson and Poole say "the Chaldee renders it giants." The Pulpit Commentary objects that this reading "would have required אימים."
- *Hot springs, or waters.* The Vulgate's *aquas calidas* gives the Douay "hot waters." Ellicott reports Jerome's claim that the word in Punic "means hot springs" and says the reading "is now generally adopted." Keil, Barnes, the Pulpit Commentary and the Cambridge Bible take it, as do most English versions on Bible Hub's parallel page. Gill objects that it needs the word "altered either in its points or letters, for which there is no authority."
- *A proper name.* The Septuagint keeps it as a name: "who found Jamin" (Brenton).

The verse's own detail, "pasturing the donkeys," is used by both sides: by Ibn Ezra for mules, and by Keil, who suggests "the asses may have contributed to the discovery" of springs.

## Literary Design

**The side line first, then the main line.** Genesis twice gives the line of the brother who does not carry the story before the line of the one who does: Ishmael's account (25:12) before Isaac's (25:19), and Esau's (36:1, 36:9) before Jacob's (37:2). The Pulpit Commentary reads 37:1 as closing the Esau section, "introducing a contrast between Esau, who dwelt in Mount Seir, and Jacob, who dwelt in the land of Canaan." The Cambridge Bible calls the heading at 37:2 "The formula of a new section in P," the priestly source in its analysis. Whether the order ranks the lines, or only clears the side line first, is Positions (1).

**Kings without dynasties.** The eight kings come from different places, and no king follows his father. Keil infers that the sovereignty "was elective." The last king, Hadad, is the only one whose wife is named and whose death is not recorded (36:39). (The BSB footnote says "other MT manuscripts Hadar," and the Masoretic text on Sefaria reads Hadar.)

## Positions

Three questions follow, each reported through named readers in their own words. None is adjudicated, and no position's wording should be read as this lesson's own conclusion.

**1. Why does Genesis give Esau's line a whole chapter?** The text gives no reason. Named readers supply four.

- **To clear the side line before the main story.** Rashi, on 37:1, following Midrash Tanchuma, says Scripture described Esau's settlements "in a brief manner," since "they were not distinguished and important enough that it should be related in detail," and then "explains clearly and at length the settlements made by Jacob." He compares a man who sifts sand for a jewel and "throws away the pebbles and keeps the jewel." Poole calls the genealogy "brought in by way of parenthesis."
- **To show a promise kept to Esau as well.** Calvin: "Though Esau was an alien from the Church in the sight of God; yet since he also, as a son of Isaac, was favored with a temporal blessing, Moses celebrates his race." He calls the record "an honorable sepulture," which shows "that the prophecy which Isaac uttered concerning Esau was not vain," yet "he has no sooner shown its effect, than he turns away our eyes, as if he had cast a veil over it." Ramban says the king list "was written in order to relate that the blessing of Isaac was fulfilled in Esau."
- **To serve a later law.** Maimonides names "the sons of Seir the Horite" and "the kings that reigned in Edom" among passages "which many consider as being of no use whatever," and gives each a legal purpose. The Seirite list protects non-Amalekites (see Context). The king list illustrates the law against a foreign king (Deuteronomy 17:15), "For of these kings none was an Edomite," and shows "that no nation ever chose a foreigner as king without inflicting thereby some great or small injury upon the country."
- **As a priestly genealogy among other sources.** The Cambridge Bible reads the headings as formulas of the priestly source and treats Esau's sons as "the names of clans." On this reading the chapter's purpose is a question about how Genesis was compiled.

These readings are not exclusive, and none of these readers argues directly against another's purpose. They pull in different directions: Rashi's "pebbles" against Maimonides' "deep wisdom," and the Cambridge Bible's sources against Barnes's view that the repeated heading "does not necessarily indicate diversity of authorship." The text itself states no purpose.

**2. "before any king reigned over the Israelites" (36:31): when, and by whom?** Israel had no king until Saul, so a heading that measures Edom's kings against Israel's has been read as evidence about when the list was written. The BSB footnote offers another rendering: "Or before an Israelite king reigned over them," which could refer to Israel ruling Edom, as under David (2 Samuel 8:14). The Cambridge Bible reports the same option from Dillmann. Five readings follow, in no ranked order.

- **Moses wrote it, and the "king" is Moses.** Ibn Ezra notes that "Some say that this chapter records a prophecy," and reports a writer, Yitzchaki, who dated the chapter to King Jehoshaphat. He rejects that dating: "Far be it for one to believe that our chapter was written in the days of Jehoshaphat as Yitzchaki maintains. Indeed his book is fit to be burned." He answers Yitzchaki's chronology by counting (Israel had about twice as many kings in a similar span) and gives his own view: "The truth is that king... refers to Moses who was king over Israel," citing "And there was a king in Jeshurun (Deut. 33:5)." The BSB reads that verse differently: "the LORD became King in Jeshurun." Benson holds the same view, "before Moses's time, for he was king in Jeshurun."
- **Moses wrote it, looking ahead to Israel's promised kings.** Keil calls the list "a record relating to the Edomitish kings of a pre-Mosaic age." He argues that Hadad, whose death Genesis does not record, "was still alive and seated upon the throne" when it was drawn up, probably the king Moses asked for passage (Numbers 20:14). The heading was "written with the promise in mind, that kings should come out of the loins of Jacob" (citing Genesis 35:11), and "merely expresses the thought, that Edom became a kingdom at an earlier period than Israel." Barnes argues the words do not "imply that monarchy in Israel had begun in the time of the writer," comparing Isaac's "before I die" (27:4). Gill says "Moses knew, from foregoing prophecies and promises, that kings would arise," and Poole says Moses foresaw it "by the Spirit of prophecy."
- **Moses wrote it about kings already past.** Ramban reports that "In the opinion of many commentators" the section "was written as a prophecy," and answers, "But this is not correct." He asks, "Why should prophecy mention these kings, and until what point in time was Scripture to enumerate them and stop?" His view: "all these ruled before the Torah was given in the days of Moses." He glosses "before there reigned any king" as "many years before."
- **A later, inspired note added to a Mosaic book.** Poole's third answer is that the line "might be inserted afterwards by some holy and inspired man of God, as it is confessed that part of the last chapter of Deuteronomy was." Ellicott thinks verses 31-43 "were probably added at the time when the Books of Samuel were composed," and holds that "there is nothing derogatory to the authority or inspiration of Holy Scripture in believing that the prophets were from time to time moved by the Spirit to add to what had been written."
- **Written after Israel had kings, by someone other than Moses.** Spinoza, in chapter 8 of the *Theologico-Political Treatise* (1670), says of 36:31, "The historian, doubtless, here relates the kings of Idumaea before that territory was conquered by David," and concludes, "it is thus clearer than the sun at noonday that the Pentateuch was not written by Moses, but by someone who lived long after Moses." His endnote allows a doubt: "It may be doubted whether the last of the Idumaean kings had begun to reign before the accession of Saul." The Cambridge Bible: "From this verse we infer that the writer lived at a time subsequent to the foundation of the Israelite monarchy." The Pulpit Commentary lists Le Clerc, Bleek, Ewald and Bohlen on this side.

*What Ibn Ezra's "secret" covers.* Spinoza says Ibn Ezra was the first he knew of to question Mosaic authorship, but "confined himself to dark hints." The hints are in Ibn Ezra on Deuteronomy 1:2: "If you understand the secret of the twelve... then you will recognize the truth." That list names Deuteronomy 31:22, Genesis 12:6, Genesis 22:14 and Deuteronomy 3:11, not Genesis 36:31, and on 36:31 Ibn Ezra defends a Mosaic reading. Spinoza adds 36:31 himself.

*Rashi* makes no authorship claim. He counts: Edom had eight kings, "and Jacob raised an equal number in whose days the kingdom of Esau temporarily ceased to exist," from Saul to Jehoshaphat (citing 1 Kings 22:48, "a deputy was king," in his translator's wording). Genesis Rabbah 83:2 has the same count: "This one crowned eight and that one crowned eight."

*Objections each reading faces.* Against "king" meaning Moses, the Cambridge Bible: "It is simplest to render, 'before there reigned a king for Israel,' i.e. before the time of Saul." Against the forward-looking readings, Ramban's question above, and Ellicott: "This is not a prophetical portion of the Bible, but a dry genealogical table." Against Ramban's reading, Keil has Hadad still reigning in Moses' day. Against a later insertion, Gill: "there is no need to suppose that." Against post-Mosaic authorship, Barnes's argument from 27:4, Keil's dating of the list, and Ibn Ezra's counting argument against Yitzchaki.

This question touches how a reader holds Scripture's authority. A reader for whom it has become personal is better served by a pastor, rabbi or teacher in their own tradition than by a study note.

**3. Esau's line in later reading: Rome, and a story of disgrace.** The Genesis 25:19-34 lesson documents how Christian writers from Tertullian on read Esau as the Jews, and what answered that use; it is not repeated here. This section reports what later readers did with chapter 36 itself.

- **Magdiel as Rome.** Rashi on 36:43: "MAGDIEL — This is Rome," citing Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer. Genesis Rabbah 83:4 has Rabbi Ami dream, on the day Diocletian took the throne, "Today, Magdiel assumed the throne." Ramban dissents in part, "I have not understood this," since Rome "is not a chieftaincy, but rather it is a great empire," though he keeps an allusion to Edom's future kings. Walfish (TheTorah.com) traces the Edom-Rome equation partly to Herod, "who was appointed by Rome as the king of Judea, and who was from a family of Edomite/Idumean converts to Judaism"; Simkovich names "Christianity and Herod" as the two factors.
- **Herod, from Josephus directly.** John Hyrcanus "subdued all the Idumeans; and permitted them to stay in that country, if they would circumcise their genitals, and make use of the laws of the Jews" (*Antiquities* 13.9.1). Herod's father was "a certain friend of Hyrcanus, an Idumean, called Antipater" (14.1.3); Josephus says Nicolaus of Damascus gave Antipater a Jewish pedigree "to gratify Herod." Herod's rival Antigonus called him "no more than a private man, and an Idumean, i.e. a half Jew" (14.15.2), an insult Josephus reports, not his own verdict. The New Testament names Idumea once in the BSB, as a region whose crowds came to Jesus (Mark 3:8), and never links Herod to Esau.
- **A line of illegitimate births.** The Talmud (Pesachim 54a) reads the Anah of 36:20 and of 36:24 as one man, born of Zibeon and his own mother, and those the Koren translation calls "interpreters of Torah symbolism" tie this to the mules. Rashi on 36:2 concludes from Midrash Tanchuma that "these were all of illegitimate birth," and on 36:5 calls Korah "an illegitimate child." Ibn Ezra reads more plainly: Oholibamah may be "the daughter of Anah the son of Zibeon," named with both "to distinguish between" two men called Anah. Barnes also takes the two Anahs as uncle and nephew.

*What answered these readings, and what the text says.* Walfish writes that Esau "in the Bible... is not described as a bad person. In fact, he is generally painted as the wronged party in the rivalry between the two." He traces "Esau the wicked" to the equation of Edom with Rome and the Church, which required that Rashi "apply it consistently throughout, to every occurrence of these names." Simkovich notes that "when we last see Esau in the Hebrew Bible, he has reconciled with his brother Jacob." Genesis 36 itself names no later people and charges no one with an unlawful birth. It records a peaceful move because "the land... could not support them" (36:7), and Deuteronomy commands, "Do not despise an Edomite, for he is your brother" (23:7).

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, a model of the shape and not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step, and a different element, situation or response would be just as legitimate. It deliberately builds nothing from Amalek or 1 Samuel 15, since a worked "application" of a command against a people could be heard as a license against someone.

**Passage element.** Genesis 36:6-7: Esau "moved to a land far away from his brother Jacob. For their possessions were too great for them to dwell together; the land where they stayed could not support them because of their livestock."

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text gives a practical reason for the separation and states no quarrel, though Esau once planned to kill Jacob (27:41) before the brothers embraced (33:4). Inference: a first audience who knew that history would likely hear a parting that kept a fragile peace by distance.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When two households' needs outgrow what they share, separating on practical terms can protect a relationship that crowding would strain.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: two related households, a shared resource that can no longer hold both, a peaceful parting. Not carried forward: the land promise to Jacob, the oracle about two nations, Edom's later history, and any claim that one sibling is "Jacob" and the other "Esau." Calvin reads the move as "the secret providence of God, by which he was driven into exile, that the possession of the land might remain free for Jacob alone"; that theological reading is not part of this analogy.

**Application class and promise scope.** Principle carried by analogy, not a command. Promise scope: none claimed; the passage promises no one that separating will keep the peace.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family and work. Two adult brothers have run their late father's landscaping business for six years. Both now have families, the business cannot support both households, and disagreements over hiring grow sharper each season.

**Response type and faithful response.** A peaceful, practical parting. They divide the business, one taking the commercial clients and the other the residential ones, put the division in writing with an attorney and an accountant, and tell their mother together before it takes effect.

**Cautions.** Dividing a business or property has legal and tax consequences that differ by place, so a qualified professional is the right next step, not a study note. The analogy is about practical crowding. It does not speak to a family where one person is controlling, threatening or harming another; that calls for safety and outside help, not a negotiated division. The bridge is an analogy the learner is testing, and it may not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. Rashi reads the brevity of Esau's account as a sign of lesser importance; Maimonides finds "deep wisdom" in the same lists; Calvin calls the record "an honorable sepulture." What in the chapter's own form would you weigh in choosing among them?
2. The heading of 36:31 has been read as Moses writing of himself, as foresight, as a record of past kings, as a later inspired note, and as evidence of a much later author. Which features of the verse, and of 1 Chronicles 1:43-51, does each reading need to explain?
3. Deuteronomy says both "blot out the memory of Amalek" (25:19) and "Do not despise an Edomite, for he is your brother" (23:7), and Genesis 36 makes Amalek Esau's grandson. Maimonides and Ramban use the genealogy to draw a line between the two. What does a reader gain, and what is at risk, when a family tree is used to limit a law?
