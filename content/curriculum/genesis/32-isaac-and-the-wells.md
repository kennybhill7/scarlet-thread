In Genesis 26 Isaac is the main actor, and almost everything that happens to him has happened before. A famine drives him toward Egypt, as one drove Abraham (12:10). He tells the men of Gerar that his wife is his sister, as Abraham did twice. He reopens his father's wells and ends at Beersheba under an oath with Abimelech and Phicol, as Abraham did (21:22-34). Twice the LORD renews to him the promise made to Abraham, "because Abraham listened to My voice and kept My charge, My commandments, My statutes, and My laws" (26:5). Readers have long asked what Abraham could have kept centuries before Sinai. This lesson sets each repetition beside its source and checks the wording. It reports, without a verdict, the main answers to the question 26:5 raises and the main readings of the wells Esek, Sitnah and Rehoboth. The wife-sister story belongs to the Genesis 20 lesson and is treated briefly here, as are Esau's Hittite wives (26:34-35), who lead into Genesis 27. Every BSB quotation was printed from this app's shipped corpus and checked by script; see Sources and Limits.

## Context

**What happens, in order (26:1-35).** "Now there was another famine in the land, subsequent to the one that had occurred in Abraham's time," and Isaac goes to "Abimelech king of the Philistines at Gerar" (26:1). The LORD tells him, "Do not go down to Egypt" (26:2), and "Stay in this land as a foreigner," and renews the promise (26:3-5). In Gerar Isaac says of Rebekah, "She is my sister" (26:7), until Abimelech sees him "caressing his wife Rebekah" (26:8) and protects them both (26:11). Isaac sows and reaps "a hundredfold" (26:12) and grows so rich "that the Philistines envied him" (26:14). They have stopped up Abraham's wells (26:15), and Abimelech says, "Depart from us, for you are much too powerful for us" (26:16). Isaac reopens his father's wells and gives them "the same names his father had given them" (26:18). The herdsmen of Gerar quarrel over two new wells ("The water is ours!"), which Isaac names Esek and Sitnah (26:20-21). Over a third "they did not quarrel," and he names it Rehoboth: "At last the LORD has made room for us, and we will be fruitful in the land" (26:22). At Beersheba the LORD appears again: "Do not be afraid, for I am with you" (26:24). Abimelech comes with Ahuzzath and Phicol (26:26). Isaac asks, "Why have you come to me? ... You hated me and sent me away" (26:27). They answer, "We can plainly see that the LORD has been with you" (26:28). They feast, swear an oath, and that day his servants report, "We have found water!" (26:30-32). He calls the well Shibah, "and to this day the name of the city is Beersheba" (26:33). The chapter closes with Esau, at forty, marrying two Hittite women, who "brought grief to Isaac and Rebekah" (26:34-35).

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens files Genesis 12 through Malachi under stage 5 (`chapters: ["1.12"]`). The chapter comes between the birth of the twins and the sold birthright (25:19-34, the previous lesson) and the stolen blessing (Genesis 27). Earlier lessons treated the sources of its repetitions: the famine of 12:10 (the Genesis 12 lesson), the wife-sister stories (the Genesis 20 lesson and its row conn-gen20-wifesister-gen26), the oath at Beersheba (the Genesis 21 lesson and its row conn-gen21-beersheba-gen26), and the oath of 22:16-18 (the Genesis 22 lesson). Those rows are cross-referenced here, not repeated.

**Which Abimelech?** The Cambridge Bible says this "can hardly be the Abimelech mentioned in Genesis 20:2" and suggests "the dynastic name of the Philistine rulers." Ramban says "It is not known whether this is the same Abimelech who lived in the days of Abraham" or whether "every Philistine king was so called," and reports that Onkelos takes him as the earlier king's son.

**Key words and claims, read as observations.**

- **"the oath that I swore to your father Abraham" (26:3).** By a script scan of this app's BSB, the only earlier verse in Genesis where God says He has sworn to Abraham is 22:16, "By Myself I have sworn, declares the LORD." Keil and Delitzsch read 26:3 "with express reference to His oath (Genesis 22:16)." The wording of 26:4-5 also follows 22:17-18 (see conn-gen26-oath-gen22).
- **"through your offspring all nations of the earth will be blessed" (26:4).** The BSB clause is word for word that of 22:18. On Bible Hub's interlinear, 22:18 and 26:4 share the Hebrew verb form *hitbarakhu*, while 12:3, 18:18 and 28:14 have *nivrekhu*. The Cambridge Bible on 26:4 calls the marginal rendering "bless themselves" right. Rashi reads the clause the same way: "A man will say to his son, 'May your seed be as the seed of Isaac'." The New Testament quotes this promise twice, both times as spoken to Abraham (Galatians 3:8; Acts 3:25). Those quotations are already reviewed rows from Genesis 12:3 and 22:18, and Teach-Back 5 explains why no row is authored from 26:4.
- **"commandments," "statutes," "laws" (26:5).** By script, 26:5 is the first verse in this app's BSB with "commandments" and the first with "statutes"; "My laws" occurs only four times (26:5; Ezekiel 44:24; Hebrews 8:10; 10:16). In Hebrew, Bible Hub's Englishman's Concordance lists Genesis 26:5 first among the occurrences of *mitsvah* (commandment), *chuqqah* (statute) and *torah* (law). The Brown-Driver-Briggs entries on the same pages mark the verse with the source labels "(R)" and "(RD)," meaning a redactor or a Deuteronomic redactor. Positions 1 reports what readers make of this.
- **"for the sake of My servant Abraham" (26:24).** "My servant Abraham" occurs once in this app's whole BSB, here. The Cambridge Bible notes the title "is only found in this place in Genesis" and that the Septuagint reads "thy father" instead.
- **"I am with you" (26:24).** By script, 26:24 is the first BSB verse with "I am with you," and 28:15, to Jacob, is the second. The theme runs through the chapter: "I will be with you" (26:3), "I am with you" (26:24), and Abimelech's "the LORD has been with you" (26:28). Abraham heard the same from Abimelech: "God is with you in all that you do" (21:22).
- **The well names.** Esek, Sitnah and Shibah each occur once in this app's BSB. Rehoboth occurs four times, but the other three are different places (10:11's "Rehoboth-Ir"; 36:37 and 1 Chronicles 1:48, "Rehoboth on the Euphrates"). The Cambridge Bible glosses Esek "Contention," Sitnah "Enmity" ("connected with the same root as the word satan, 'adversary'"), and Rehoboth "Broad places, or, Room." On Bible Hub's interlinear, Rehoboth (*Rechovot*) and the verb of "has made room" (*hirchiv*) share a root. "Made room" occurs nowhere else in the BSB. For Shibah, the Cambridge Bible notes that 21:31 gives "another tradition as to the origin of the name" Beersheba.
- **"a hundredfold" (26:12)** is the only Old Testament verse in this app's BSB with that word (see Teach-Back 5).

**The wife-sister episode (26:6-11), briefly.** The Genesis 20 lesson sets the three tellings in a table (12:10-20; 20:1-18; 26:6-11) and reports how readers account for the repetition. Three details belong to this telling. Rebekah is never taken. The truth comes out when the king sees Isaac "caressing" her (26:8); the Cambridge Bible notes the verb is "the same as that from which the name 'Isaac' was popularly derived." And there is no half-sister plea: the Cambridge Bible observes that "The plea of the relationship of a half-sister could be made for Sarah, but not for Rebekah." Readers judge Isaac differently. Calvin says "the negligence of Isaac is inexcusable." He grants that Isaac "does not in express terms deny his wife," but blames him because "he exposes her to prostitution," and most of all "in not taking warning from domestic examples." On 26:8 Calvin turns to "the kind forbearance of God" in pardoning "the twofold fault of his servant." Ramban explains why Rebekah came to no harm: the king's men "remembered the affair of Abraham." Rashi notes that after a long stay Isaac "was not sufficiently strict in guarding his actions."

As in the Genesis 20 lesson: if a partner's choices are putting you at risk, or you are pressured to say or do things that endanger you, that is not a question for a study note. The National Domestic Violence Hotline is free and confidential: call 1-800-799-7233, text START to 88788, or visit thehotline.org.

**Esau's Hittite wives (26:34-35), briefly.** Esau is "forty years old," the age at which Isaac married (25:20), as Gill and the Cambridge Bible note. The text says only that the two wives "brought grief to Isaac and Rebekah"; it gives no reason. Readers supply one. Rashi, citing Genesis Rabbah, says it was "because they worshipped idols." The Cambridge Bible says Isaac and Rebekah "regarded a mixed marriage with the people of the land as a source of dishonour." It adds that the Septuagint "took the meaning to be that Judith and Basemath were quarrelsome." Ellicott says "the place of these two verses would rightly be at the beginning of Genesis 27," and Rebekah takes them up at 27:46 (see conn-gen26-hittite-wives-gen27). Genesis 36:2-3 names Esau's wives differently: "Adah daughter of Elon the Hittite" and "Basemath daughter of Ishmael." Poole suggests the women had more than one name; Gill (following Ibn Ezra and Josephus) identifies Basemath with Adah. Several older commentators (Henry, Benson, Keil and Delitzsch) go further and call the Hittites a people under a curse or cast off. Those are their judgments, not words of Genesis 26. The chapter's verdict is only "grief," and the lesson does not treat these verses as a rule for anyone's marriage today.

## Literary Design

**A life told again.** The chapter's episodes line up with Abraham's, and the parallels can be read off the BSB.

| | Abraham | Isaac (Genesis 26) |
|---|---|---|
| Famine | "there was a famine in the land" (12:10) | "another famine in the land, subsequent to the one that had occurred in Abraham's time" (26:1) |
| Egypt | "Abram went down to Egypt" (12:10) | "Do not go down to Egypt" (26:2) |
| Wife called sister | 12:13; 20:2 | 26:7 |
| Wells disputed | "a well that Abimelech's servants had seized" (21:25) | "The water is ours!" (26:20) |
| "God with" him, said by the king | "God is with you in all that you do" (21:22) | "the LORD has been with you" (26:28) |
| Covenant and oath with Abimelech and Phicol | 21:22-32 | 26:26-31 |
| Beersheba named | "So that place was called Beersheba" (21:31) | "to this day the name of the city is Beersheba" (26:33) |

Calvin, on 26:26, writes, "We have had an exactly similar narrative in Genesis 21:22." Ramban, on 26:20, notes that "in the literal interpretation of the story there would seem to be no benefit nor any great honor to Isaac in that he and his father did the identical thing," and finds the purpose in what the wells foreshadow (Positions 2). The Cambridge Bible assigns 26:33 to its J source and 21:31 to E, two traditions about one name. This lesson does not choose among these answers.

**The frame.** Two appearances of the LORD, at Gerar (26:2-5) and at Beersheba (26:24), frame the chapter. Between them the human story runs from "Depart from us" (26:16) to "sending you on your way in peace" (26:29). The Cambridge Bible calls the second statement "scarcely veracious in view of Genesis 26:15; Genesis 26:20-21." Calvin says the visitors "dissemble on this point, and even make a wonderful boast of their own acts of kindness."

**Three wells, in order.** Quarrel, quarrel, no quarrel. The first two names record the dispute, and the third records relief. Calvin reads Isaac's naming as a protest: by calling the wells "strife" and "hostility," "he denies that the inhabitants possessed that by right." Isaac's speech at 26:27 is the only time in the chapter he names the wrong to the wrongdoers' faces.

## Positions

Two questions follow. Each is reported through named readers in their own words; none is decided here. Positions report what readers conclude; Context and Literary Design report what the text says.

**1. "kept My charge, My commandments, My statutes, and My laws" (26:5): what did Abraham keep before Sinai?** The text lists four terms and does not say what they contained. The Torah given at Sinai comes later in the canon's own story. Readers divide on what the four terms refer to and on how a reader should take them.

- **The whole Torah, written and oral.** The Mishnah (Kiddushin 4:14) says: "We found that Abraham our forefather fulfilled the entire Torah before it was given," citing 26:5. In the Talmud (Yoma 28b), Rav says the same: "Abraham our Patriarch fulfilled the entire Torah before it was given." Rav Shimi bar Ḥiyya challenges him: "say that the verse means that he fulfilled only the seven Noahide mitzvot and not the entire Torah." The Talmud notes that circumcision already goes beyond the seven. Rav answers from the list itself: "If so, why do I need the continuation of the verse, that Abraham kept: My mitzvot and My Torah?" The Talmud then adds that Abraham kept even a later rabbinic ordinance, because "My Torahs" is plural: "one, the Written Torah, and one, the Oral Torah." Genesis Rabbah 64:4 makes the same point ("'My Torahs' – two Torahs") and says Abraham "knew even the laws of Eiruv Hatzerot." Rashi gives each term a category. "My charge" means rabbinic "precautionary measures." "My commandments" are laws reason would require, "such as robbery and murder." "My ordinances" are decrees "for which there are no apparent reasons," and "My laws" includes "the Oral Law."
- **The Noahide laws and Abraham's own commands, kept in full.** Ramban quotes Rashi and then raises a difficulty against reading the verse as the whole Torah: "how did Jacob erect a pillar," marry two sisters, and Amram marry his aunt, if Abraham had kept these laws? He offers three answers. The first maps the four terms onto laws "the Sons of Noah were commanded," with Abraham "observing even the details and the strictures of their commandments." The second, which he says "appears to me from a study of the opinions of our Rabbis," is that Abraham "learned the entire Torah by Ruach Hakodesh" and kept it "as 'one who is not commanded but nevertheless observes it'," and only in the Land of Israel. The third is "the literal meaning of Scripture": "My charge" is faith in the one God, "My commandments" is "all that G-d commanded Abraham" (leaving his land, the binding, the expulsion of Hagar), "My statutes" is "walking in the paths of G-d," and "My Laws" is circumcision together with the Noahide commandments. Ibn Ezra takes the commandments to be specific divine orders, such as "Get thee out of thy country" and "Take now thy son," and the laws to be "Abraham's circumcision of himself, his children and his servants." He calls the statutes things "based on logic." Sforno says Abraham kept "the seven laws I laid down for all of mankind" and did "the very things which I Myself am in the habit of doing, such as to perform deeds of loving kindness."
- **Obedience described in the vocabulary of the law.** Calvin: "although laws, statutes, rites, precepts, and ceremonies, had not yet been written, Moses used these terms, that he might the more clearly show how sedulously Abraham regulated his life according to the will of God alone." Haydock's Catholic commentary, citing Menochius, glosses the terms as "Ceremonies of religion, observed under the law of nature." Benson says Abraham "obeyed the original laws of nature, the revealed laws of divine worship, particularly that of circumcision, and all the extraordinary precepts God gave him." Keil and Delitzsch read "constant obedience to all the revelations and instructions of God."
- **The vocabulary of a later writer.** The Cambridge Bible calls the list "A strange redundancy of expression, reminding us of the style of Deut.," notes that the four words "correspond to the more simple phrase 'the way of the Lord' in Genesis 18:19," and concludes: "The observance of legal enactments, ascribed to Abraham, is, strictly speaking, an anachronism." It cites Deuteronomy 11:1 (see conn-gen26-charge-deut11). The BDB labels "(R)" and "(RD)" on the verse (Key Words) belong to the same source-critical approach. Keil and Delitzsch, who read the terms as Abraham's obedience, also say they "frequently recur among the legal expressions of a later date."

*A linked question: does "because" make the blessing a reward?* The text says "because Abraham listened to My voice" (26:5) and "for the sake of My servant Abraham" (26:24). The Cambridge Bible: "The blessing of Isaac is here treated as the reward of the obedience of Abraham; the emphasis is laid on the unity and continuity of the Chosen Family." Sforno reads 26:24 as God doing Isaac favours "which were the result of the merits of others, i.e. the merit of his father." Rashi ties the hearing to the binding: "when I put him to the test." Calvin answers the other way: "Moses does not mean that Abraham's obedience was the reason why the promise of God was confirmed and ratified to him," but "what God freely bestows upon the faithful is sometimes, beyond their desert, ascribed to themselves." Matthew Poole holds both: "because Abraham performed his condition, God engageth himself to perform his promise," yet "it is an idle thing to think they could be merited by so mean a compensation as Abraham's obedience." The Geneva notes say the confirmation, like the promise, comes "of his free mercy." Gill reads the clause as "an example to Isaac" rather than a cause.

The challenges quoted (Rav Shimi bar Ḥiyya's, Ramban's) are each tradition's own internal questions, answered within it; no reading is given a rebuttal from another tradition here. The readings also answer different questions: the Talmud and Ramban ask what Abraham did, Calvin what Moses' words convey, and the Cambridge Bible when the words were written.

**2. Esek, Sitnah, Rehoboth (26:19-22): what do the wells mean?** The text gives the names and Isaac's one sentence of explanation (26:22). It states no further meaning. Readers take the wells in four ways, which mostly answer different questions.

- **Place-name tradition and history.** The Cambridge Bible: "In this and the two following verses we have popular tradition as to the origin of the names of wells in the region associated with the sojournings of the patriarch." Keil and Delitzsch, Barnes and the Pulpit Commentary report that nineteenth-century travellers placed Rehoboth at Wady Ruhaibeh, south of Beersheba, and Sitnah at Wady Shutein. Jamieson-Fausset-Brown say such contests over wells "occur among the natives in those regions as frequently in the present day as in the time of Isaac."
- **A moral example of patience.** Calvin sees in the third well "an example of invincible patience." On the wells generally he writes, "that this last necessity did not instigate him to seek revenge, is a proof of singular forbearance." Matthew Henry: "those who avoid striving, yet cannot avoid being striven with," and "Those that study to be quiet, seldom fail of being so." Benson: "Those that follow peace, sooner or later shall find peace." Calvin also says yielding is not silence. On 26:27 he writes that "it is lawful for the faithful to complain of their enemies, in order, if possible, to recall them from their purpose of doing injury." In his words, "nor does God require of his own people, that they should silently digest every injury which may be inflicted upon them, but only that they should restrain their minds and hands from revenge." Gill reads Sitnah more harshly: "from this word Satan has his name, and these men were of a diabolical spirit."
- **Figures of Israel's history and Torah.** Ramban: "there is a hidden matter involved here since Scripture's purpose is to make known a future matter. A well of living water alludes to the House of G-d which the children of Isaac will build." Esek is "an allusion to the First House," over which, he says, the nations contended with Israel until they destroyed it. Sitnah, "a name harsher than the first," "alludes to the Second House," and Rehoboth to "the Future House, which will be speedily built in our days, and it will be done without quarrel and feud." He reads "we will be fruitful in the land" as "all peoples will come to worship G-d with one consent." Genesis Rabbah 64:8 instead lines the wells up with the books of the Torah. Esek is "corresponding to the book of Genesis," Sitnah "to the book of Exodus," and Rehoboth "to the book of Deuteronomy, due to, 'when the Lord your God will expand [yarḥiv]'" (Deuteronomy 12:20).
- **A figure of interpretation itself.** Origen is quoted, on a Coptic Orthodox study page, applying the scene to his own preaching: "If I shall wish to dig deeply and open the hidden veins of living water, immediately the Philistines will be present and will strive with me. They will stir up disputes and malicious charges against me and will begin to refill my wells with their earth and mud." The page presents this as Origen's complaint against hearers who objected to "his discovery of a spiritual sense" in Scripture. Origen's Homilies on Genesis were not read for this lesson, so nothing further is reported of his reading.

These readings rarely contradict one another. The Cambridge Bible asks where the names came from, and Calvin and Henry what Isaac's conduct models. Ramban and Genesis Rabbah ask what the wells foreshadow, and Origen what the act of digging pictures. Genesis 26 itself records three names, two quarrels and one sentence of thanks.

A reader weighing a real dispute of their own (over property, work, a church or family) should take it to someone qualified to help. A study note can report how readers took Isaac's wells but cannot settle anyone's claim.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge. It models the shape and is not an answer key; the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate step.

**Passage element.** Genesis 26:19-22, 26:27. Isaac's servants dig two wells that the herdsmen of Gerar claim. He names the disputes ("Esek," "Sitnah"), moves on, and digs a third, saying, "At last the LORD has made room for us." Later, when his opponents come to him, he names the wrong: "You hated me and sent me away" (26:27).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text records Isaac giving way twice, naming each loss, finding an uncontested well, and later stating his grievance before agreeing to a covenant (26:27-31). Inference: a first audience of herders might have heard a pattern of moving on without pretending nothing happened. The text states no such moral.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When a shared resource becomes a repeated quarrel, it can be wise to look for room elsewhere while still naming honestly what happened. Whether to give way at all is a judgment the passage does not make for anyone.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a contested shared resource, repeated disputes, a choice to move rather than fight, an honest naming of the loss, and a later conversation. Not carried forward: the promise of the land, the theophanies, Isaac's wealth, and any claim about who was legally right about the water.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed. "We will be fruitful in the land" is Isaac's own statement about his own situation. It does not promise that moving on will bring room to anyone else.

**Modern domain and situation.** Community. Two volunteer groups share a church fellowship hall on the same evening. Twice the other group has arrived early and taken the room. The coordinator of the smaller group is deciding what to do.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: peacemaking with honesty. One faithful response is to ask the building manager about another room or night, and also tell the other group's leader plainly that the double booking cost the smaller group its meeting.

**Cautions.** This example concerns a low-stakes scheduling clash between equals. It is not advice to give up a legal, financial or employment claim. It does not apply where someone is being harassed, threatened or pushed out by a person with power over them. There, a mediator, attorney, union representative or other qualified person is the right next step, not a study note. Readers in Positions 2 draw different lessons from the wells; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. Using the table in Literary Design, which repetitions in Genesis 26 seem to you to change something, and which only repeat?
2. Of the four answers to 26:5 in Positions 1, which is a claim about Abraham and which about the writer? Can a reader hold two at once?
3. The text says both "because Abraham listened to My voice" (26:5) and "for the sake of My servant Abraham" (26:24). Is anything in Genesis 22 or 26 decisive for Calvin's "beyond their desert," or for the Cambridge Bible's "reward"?
4. Isaac yields twice over the wells, but at 26:27 he confronts Abimelech. What in the text, rather than in a later reading, would you point to if asked whether Isaac was passive?
5. Genesis 26:35 gives no reason for the "grief." Using only Genesis 24:3, 26:34-35, 27:46 and 28:1-9, what can a reader say about the reason, and what has to come from outside the text?

Choose one row and defend it, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`. Three candidates were not authored. (a) **Galatians 3:8 and Acts 3:25 from 26:4.** The blessing of the nations is word for word in the BSB at 22:18 and 26:4, and the Septuagint of 26:4 has "ἐν τῷ σπέρματί σου" as at 22:18. But Paul and Peter both introduce the words as spoken to Abraham ("foretold the gospel to Abraham," Galatians 3:8; "when He said to Abraham," Acts 3:25). 26:4 is spoken to Isaac. Those quotations are already reviewed rows (conn-gen12-blessing-galatians3-8, conn-gen22-offspring-acts3), and the second already names 26:4 as set aside for that reason. A third row from 26:4 would duplicate them, so the honest grade is `no_warrant_yet`. (b) **"a well of fresh water" (26:19) and "living water" (John 4:10).** The Cambridge Bible notes the Hebrew of 26:19 is literally "living" and cross-references John 4:10. Ramban and Origen use the image figuratively. John 4 names Jacob's well (4:6, 4:12), not Isaac's, and no source read here argues that John points to Genesis 26, so this is a shared image, `no_warrant_yet`. (c) **"hundredfold" (26:12) and the parable of the sower.** The Matthew 13 lesson weighed and declined this one; this lesson agrees and does not reopen it.
