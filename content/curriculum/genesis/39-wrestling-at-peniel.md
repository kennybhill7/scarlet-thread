Twenty years after he fled a brother who meant to kill him (27:41), Jacob is coming home, and Esau is "coming to meet you—he and four hundred men with him" (32:6). Jacob splits his household into two camps, prays (116 words in the BSB, the longest single prayer among the Genesis prayers this lesson counted), and sends a herd of gifts ahead. Then, alone at the Jabbok at night, "a man wrestled with him until daybreak" (32:24). By morning Jacob is limping, has a new name, Israel, and says, "I have seen God face to face, and yet my life was spared" (32:30). Who the man was has been debated for as long as the chapter has been read. This lesson reads the chapter closely, checks its word-play in the Hebrew, and reports four answers to that question, each in its own sources' words, without choosing among them. It does the same for the meaning of "Israel", for the tension with "no one can see Me and live" (Exodus 33:20), and for how Jacob's prayer and gift should be judged.

## Context

**What happens, in order (32:1-32).** "The angels of God met him," and Jacob names the place Mahanaim (32:1-2). He sends messengers to "my master Esau" (32:3-5). They report Esau coming with four hundred men; "In great fear and distress," Jacob divides his people into "two camps" (32:6-8). He prays (32:9-12). He selects a gift of goats, sheep, camels, cattle and donkeys, sends it in separate droves, and tells each driver what to say (32:13-21). At night he takes his family across the ford of the Jabbok (32:22-23). "So Jacob was left all alone," and the wrestling follows (32:24-29). He names the place Peniel and limps away at sunrise (32:30-31). The narrator adds a food custom: "to this day the Israelites do not eat the tendon which is at the socket of the hip" (32:32).

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens puts Genesis 12 through Malachi in stage 5 (machine-readable `chapters: ["1.12"]`). The chapter follows Jacob's parting from Laban (31:55) and stops the night before the brothers meet (33:1-4), which belongs to a later unit.

**Verse numbers.** Hebrew Bibles number Genesis 32 one verse ahead of English ones: BSB 31:55 is Hebrew 32:1, and BSB 32:1 is Hebrew 32:2. Hosea 12 runs the same way (BSB 12:3-4 is Hebrew 12:4-5). Sefaria, Skinner, Gunkel and some Bible Hub commentators use the Hebrew numbers. Every reference in this lesson is given in BSB numbering.

**Key words, read as observations.** Counts in the BSB were made by this lesson's own script over the app's text, matching case-insensitively on word boundaries. Hebrew counts were tallied by script from Bible Hub's Genesis 32 interlinear.

- **Camps (32:1-21).** "Camp" or "camps" occurs six times in the chapter (32:2, 32:7, twice in 32:8, 32:10, 32:21), and the interlinear shows the same Hebrew noun, *maḥănê*, in each; Mahanaim (32:2) is its dual form. Jacob calls the angels "the camp of God" (32:2), splits his own people into "two camps" for fear (32:7), and in prayer says, "now I have become two camps" (32:10). The Cambridge Bible calls 32:10 "Another evident allusion to the name Mahanaim." Readers differ on what the two camps of 32:2 were. Rashi: two camps of angels, those "ministering outside the Holy Land" and those "ministering in the Land of Israel." Keil: "the host of God joined his host as a safeguard." Calvin thinks it "much more probable, that angels were distributed in two camps on different sides of Jacob."
- **Face (32:20-30).** The BSB has "face" three times in the chapter (32:20, twice in 32:30). The Hebrew has more. The interlinear shows the noun *pānîm*, "face," ten times in the chapter (32:3, 32:16, 32:17, four times in 32:20, 32:21, twice in 32:30), seven of them between 32:20 and 32:30, plus the place name Peniel, "face of God" (32:30), and its variant Penuel (32:31). Ellicott gives 32:20 literally: "he said I will cover his face with the offering that goeth before my face, and afterwards I will see his face; peradventure he will lift up my face." He adds that covering the offended person's face "became the usual legal word for making an atonement." Where the BSB has "Jacob’s gifts went on before him" (32:21), the Hebrew again has "before his face." The run continues into the next chapter: "I have seen your face, and it is like seeing the face of God" (33:10), where the interlinear shows *pānîm* twice again. The Cambridge Bible on 33:10: "We can hardly doubt that this turn of compliment contains a side allusion to the name of the locality, Peniel."
- **Wrestled (32:24-25).** Bible Hub's concordance lists the Hebrew verb *ʾābaq* only in these two verses. Ellicott says it was "without doubt" chosen "because of its resemblance to the name Jabbok"; Keil and the Cambridge Bible also hear the river's name. Its root is disputed. Rashi reports Menachem's "a man covered himself with dust" and prefers "he fastened himself on." The Pulpit Commentary cites Ibn Ezra for a derivation from "dust" and Fürst for one meaning "to embrace." In the BSB, "wrestle" in any form occurs in six verses: 30:8 (Rachel's "I have wrestled with my sister"), 32:24, 32:25, Psalm 13:2, Hosea 12:3 and Colossians 4:12. Since *ʾābaq* is listed only at 32:24-25, the English repetition in 30:8 and Hosea 12:3 is not a Hebrew one.
- **Israel (32:28).** In this app's BSB, the first verse in the Bible with "Israel" is 32:28. The next, 32:32, already uses it for the people: "the Israelites." The verb behind "you have struggled," *śārâ* (Strong's 8280), is tagged in Bible Hub's interlinear only at 32:28 and Hosea 12:3. The same page counts three NASB renderings, and its Brown-Driver-Briggs entry, like Skinner, treats Hosea's next verb, "he struggled with the angel" (12:4, tagged 7786 in the interlinear), as the same verb in another form. Either way, Hosea uses the verb of 32:28 for this event.
- **The gift (32:13-15).** The animals listed total 550, not counting the camels' young; Keil gives the same figure ("550 head of cattle").
- **The sinew (32:32).** "Tendon" occurs in the BSB in Genesis only at 32:32. The Cambridge Bible notes that "No mention of this practice of ritual abstinence occurs in the Levitical law; but it is referred to in the Talmud Tract Chullin, cap. vii." The seventh chapter of Mishnah Chullin, read in Sefaria's English (the William Davidson translation, which weaves short explanations into the text), sets out the practice as Jewish law. It states that "The prohibition of eating the sciatic nerve applies both in Eretz Yisrael and outside of Eretz Yisrael," to domesticated and undomesticated animals and to both thighs, "But it does not apply to a bird," since a bird "has no spoon of the thigh" (7:1). The chapter's last ruling asks whether the prohibition covers non-kosher animals. Rabbi Yehuda says it does, arguing that it was forbidden "for the children of Jacob" before Sinai. The Rabbis reply that it "was stated in Sinai, but" it "was written in its place" (7:6; the Sefaria English adds the explanation that it "did not take effect then"). Radak says Jacob's sons "adopted this prohibition for themselves in commemoration of their father" and that it "continued until the Torah was given and it became law." Rashbam calls it "a reminder of the heroism displayed by Yaakov, as well as a reminder of the miracle." These readers explain the practice; this lesson reports them and does not rule between them.

## Literary Design

**The prayer (32:9-12).** The Cambridge Bible outlines it as "(1) an invocation; (2) a reminder of the promise; (3) a humble acknowledgment of mercies; (4) an entreaty." It opens and closes with God's own words. It begins with "Go back to your country and to your kindred, and I will make you prosper" (32:9) and ends with "I will surely make you prosper, and I will make your offspring like the sand of the sea" (32:12). Rashi asks where God had promised "sand of the sea" to Jacob, since 28:14 says "dust of the earth," and answers that the promise to Abraham (22:17, "the sand on the seashore") was included in "I will not leave you until I have done what I have promised you" (28:15).

*Is it the longest prayer in Genesis?* By this lesson's word count of the shipped BSB, Jacob's words in 32:9-12 run to 116. The servant's prayer at the well (24:12-14) runs to 94. Abraham's longest unbroken turn in his plea for Sodom (18:23-25) runs to 85, but his six turns in 18:23-32 total 172. So 32:9-12 is the longest single, uninterrupted address to God in Genesis among the passages checked, though not the most words a person speaks to God in one scene. Ellicott calls it "Jacob's prayer, the first recorded in the Bible." The text does not bear out "first": the servant "prayed" at 24:12, and Abraham's plea comes earlier still.

**Questions about names.** Two names are asked for. The man asks, "What is your name?" and Jacob answers with his own (32:27). Jacob asks the man's and is refused: "Why do you ask my name?" (32:29). The BSB has those words in only one other place, the angel of the LORD's reply to Manoah (Judges 13:18). Readers have heard the first exchange against Genesis 27, where Jacob answered his blind father, "I am Esau, your firstborn" (27:19). Rashi on 32:26 has the blessing Jacob demands mean "admit my right to the blessings which my father gave me," and the Pulpit Commentary says Jacob now sought "the blessing which he had previously stolen from his aged father by craft." The link to 27:19 is this lesson's observation; neither reader cites that verse.

## Positions

Four questions follow. Each is reported through named readers in their own words, and none is adjudicated. Nothing in a position's statement should be read as this lesson's own conclusion.

**1. Who wrestled with Jacob?** The text calls him "a man" (32:24). He cannot overpower Jacob, injures his hip, asks to go at daybreak, gives a blessing and a name, and refuses his own name (32:25-29). The name he gives is explained "because you have struggled with God" (32:28), and Jacob says, "I have seen God face to face" (32:30). Hosea later says Jacob "wrestled with God" and "struggled with the angel and prevailed" (Hosea 12:3-4). Four kinds of answer follow, in no order of merit.

- **An angel sent by God.** Targum Pseudo-Jonathan (in Etheridge's translation) has "an Angel contended with him in the likeness of a man," who says at dawn, "I am one of the angels of praise." The Talmud (Chullin 91b) gives the angel nearly the same words: "I am an angel, and from the day I was created my time to recite a song [before God] has not arrived, until now." (The bracketed words are Steinsaltz's explanatory addition in Sefaria's English.) Radak names him: "This was the angel Gavriel." Rashbam gives the angel a purpose: "an angel engaged him in a physical fight, his purpose being to prevent Yaakov from fleeing." Ramban reads the angel as restrained by God: he "could not prevail against him to harm him for it was not permitted to him to do other than that which he did to him." On 32:29 Ramban has the angel explain his silence about his name: "no one possesses the power and the capability other than G-d alone." Among Christian commentators, Ellicott writes: "There is no warrant for regarding the angel as an incarnation of Deity, any more than in the case of Manoah (Judges 13:22); but it was a manifestation of God mediately by His messenger, and was one of the many signs indicative of a more complete manifestation by the coming of the Word in the flesh."
- **God himself, appearing as a man, or the pre-incarnate Son.** Calvin: "it is not said that Satan, or any mortal man, wrestled with Jacob, but God himself." He explains the word "man": "since God appeared under the form of a man, the name is thence assumed." Keil reads it as God "not in a created angel, but in the Angel of Jehovah, the visible manifestation of the invisible God." Justin Martyr (*Dialogue with Trypho* 125-126) reads it of Christ. He says Moses writes "'A man wrestled with Jacob,' and asserts it was God," and that "Christ would act so when He became man was foretold by the mystery of Jacob's wrestling with Him who appeared to him." Benson: "The eternal Word, or Son of God, who often appeared in a human shape, before he assumed the human nature." Poole: "an angel, yea, the Angel of the covenant, the Son of God." Matthew Henry: "the second Person in the sacred Trinity, who was afterwards God manifest in the flesh." Gill calls him "the Son of God in an human form."
- **Esau's guardian angel.** Genesis Rabbah 77:3: "Rabbi Ḥama ben Rabbi Ḥanina said: It was Esau's guardian angel." He takes his proof from Jacob's words to Esau the next day (33:10), which the Sefaria translation renders "as the sight of the face of angels." Rashi on 32:24 reports the same: "Our Rabbis of blessed memory explained that he was Esau's guardian angel." On 32:28 he reads the struggle as a dispute over Isaac's blessing, which the angel is forced to concede. On Hosea 12:4 he writes, "Now that angel was Esau's genius, and he was contesting the blessings." On this reading Hosea's "he wept and sought His favor" describes the angel, not Jacob: "i.e., the angel wept." The midrash also keeps other views. Rabbi Ḥunya says the man appeared "in the image of a herdsman," and the Rabbis say "as the leader of robbers" (77:2). Rabbi Berekhya says, "We do not know who was victorious, whether it was the angel or Jacob," and then answers from the verb *vaye'avek*, read as the man "who became covered with dust," "the man who was with him"; the translator's note takes this to mean that Jacob won (77:3).
- **An older legend behind the text.** Hermann Gunkel (*The Legends of Genesis*, 1901) calls the Penuel story a "legend" and writes, "It is a marvel indeed that the legend of Penuel ... is transmitted to us in such primitive form; in this the device has been to leave it undefined who the God really was that attacked Jacob." John Skinner (International Critical Commentary, 1910) takes "Let me go, for it is daybreak" as "a survival of the wide-spread belief in spirits of the night which must vanish at dawn." He proposes that "the god was probably not Yahwe originally, but a local deity, a night-spirit who fears the dawn and refuses to disclose his name." The river-spirit idea is Frazer's: Skinner reports that James Frazer "hazards the conjecture that the original deity of this passage was the spirit of the Jabbok." Skinner then describes how the story was retold: "By J and E the story was incorporated in the national epos as part of the history of Jacob. The God who wrestles with the patriarch is Yahwe." He reads Hosea's "angel" as "increasing sensitiveness to anthropomorphism." The Cambridge Bible, which reads the scene as Jacob's "personal meeting with the Divine Being," also calls the daybreak line "A survival of the old belief that unearthly visitants of the night must be gone before daybreak."

This lesson does not collect objections to these four readings; readers can weigh them directly. Two related rejections are on record. Ellicott and Skinner both reject reading the narrative as "an allegory" (Skinner: not "an allegory of the spiritual life"). Barnes, Gill and Keil reject reading it as a dream or vision (Barnes: "we do not feel at liberty to depart from the literal sense"), a view Radak holds (Positions 3).

One more use of the scene is not tied to any identity. The Catechism of the Catholic Church (2573) says only that Jacob "wrestles all night with a mysterious figure who refuses to reveal his name." It adds that "the spiritual tradition of the Church has retained the symbol of prayer as a battle of faith and as the triumph of perseverance." Calvin, Henry, Keil and Hosea's "he wept and sought His favor" read the struggle as prayer in a similar way.

**2. What does "Israel" mean?** The BSB explains the name: "Your name will no longer be Jacob, but Israel, because you have struggled with God and with men, and you have prevailed" (32:28). Readers derive the name in different ways.

- **"Prince with God."** The KJV renders the clause "for as a prince hast thou power with God and with men, and hast prevailed." Calvin derives the name from a verb "which signifies to rule, as if he were called a Prince of God." Matthew Henry: "a prince with God." Ellicott: "a prince of God, or, one powerful with God."
- **"Striver with God."** Keil: "God's fighter, from שׂרה to fight, and אל God." The Cambridge Bible's first sense is "the perseverer with God." Hosea's "he wrestled with God" (12:3) uses the same verb as 32:28.
- **"God strives."** The Brown-Driver-Briggs entry on Bible Hub defines the name as "'God strives,' another name of Jacob and his desc." The Cambridge Bible's second sense is "El persevereth," on the pattern of "Ishmael = 'God hears.'" Skinner gives "probably = 'God strives'" and says the name "is interpreted as 'Striver with God'"; he adds that the verb's "real meaning is uncertain." Kalisch, as the Pulpit Commentary reports him, rendered "God governs."
- **Other early readings.** Justin: "the name Israel signifies this, A man who overcomes power; for Isra is a man overcoming, and El is power." Rashi reads the new name against the old: "It shall no longer be said that the blessings came to you through supplanting and subtlety but through noble conduct (שררה) and in an open manner." Onkelos, in Sefaria's English, has "you have contended with God[ly beings] [are great before God]." Targum Pseudo-Jonathan has "thou art magnified with the angels of the Lord and with the mighty."

This lesson does not collect objections for this question (Gill, for example, says the "prince" derivation "confutes all other etymologies," and Skinner says Driver's "persist" "hardly yields a suitable idea"); readers can weigh the positions directly. Genesis gives the name a second time, at Bethel: "Though your name is Jacob, you will no longer be called Jacob. Instead, your name will be Israel" (35:10). Genesis Rabbah 78:3 and Rashi read 35:10 as God confirming what the angel had said. Keil says God "confirmed to him the name of Israel, which He had already given him." The Cambridge Bible treats the two as accounts from different sources ("This change of name has been mentioned by J in Genesis 32:28").

**A use of the name against Jews.** Justin (*Dialogue* 125) reads the name as belonging to "all who through Him have fled for refuge to the Father," whom he calls "the blessed Israel," and tells Trypho that, as children of Jacob "after the fleshly seed," his hope of salvation is mistaken. The Catholic Church's Second Vatican Council, which calls the Church "the new people of God," also wrote that "the Jews should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God, as if this followed from the Holy Scriptures" (*Nostra Aetate* 4); that declaration addresses Catholic teaching and preaching. Genesis 32 itself gives the name to Jacob and, in 32:32, to "the Israelites."

**3. "I have seen God face to face, and yet my life was spared" (32:30), and "no one can see Me and live" (Exodus 33:20).** In Exodus, God tells Moses, "You cannot see My face, for no one can see Me and live" (Exodus 33:20). Yet nine verses earlier, "the LORD would speak to Moses face to face" (33:11). Jacob says he saw God face to face and lived. Readers reconcile the texts in different ways.

- **What Jacob saw was an angel or a form.** The Targums turn the sentence. Targum Pseudo-Jonathan: "I have seen the Angels of the Lord face to face, and my soul is saved." Onkelos renders "angels" of God (Metsudah's English prints it in brackets after "God[ly beings]"). The Cambridge Bible: "The face of God was to be seen in the Angel: he that looked on the Angel saw the Presence of Jehovah." The Pulpit Commentary on Exodus 33:20 says Jacob "marvelled that he could see God, even in that intermediate way, and live."
- **He saw the Son, not the Father.** Justin argues that "neither Abraham, nor Isaac, nor Jacob, nor any other man, saw the Father and ineffable Lord of all, and also of Christ, but [saw] Him who was according to His will His Son, being God, and the Angel because He ministered to His will" (*Dialogue* 127).
- **A real but partial sight.** Calvin: "The simple meaning then is, that he saw God in an unwonted and extraordinary manner." Compared "with the splendor of the gospel, or even of the law, it will appear like sparks, or obscure rays." Gill on Exodus 33:20: "men have, in a sense, seen the face of God in this life, and have lived." The Pulpit Commentary there: "The inability proclaimed in these words is not an absolute inability to see God, but an inability to see and survive the sight."
- **A vision felt as real.** Radak, who reads the whole episode as "a prophetic vision," explains "face to face" as meaning that "the vision was so real to him that in his vision he reacted physically to the angel that appeared to him."
- **A shared belief that the text reports.** The Cambridge Bible lists the passages and says: "The belief that to see God was to die prevailed amongst the Israelites." Skinner says the name Peniel "is derived from an incidental feature of the experience" and cross-refers Exodus 33.

This lesson does not collect objections for this question (Poole, Gill and Keil, for example, reject a vision reading); readers can weigh the positions directly.

**4. Jacob's prayer and gift: trust, or self-reliance?** Jacob prays (32:9-12) between two pieces of planning: dividing the camps (32:7-8) and sending the gift (32:13-21). His stated aim for the gift is "I will appease Esau" (32:20).

- **Faith that also uses means.** Calvin on the gift: Jacob "does not act distrustfully"; "he did not omit the use of the means which were in his power, while leaving success in the hand of God." On the camps: "it proceeded from the spirit of faith." Keil: "Although hoping for aid and safety from the Lord alone, Jacob neglected no means of doing what might help to appease his brother." The Pulpit Commentary says the prayer's "humility and boldness" has been "universally recognized." Rashi, following Tanchuma, says Jacob "prepared himself for three things": a present, prayer, and war.
- **A humble prayer that leaves something out.** The Cambridge Bible: "The absence of confession of sin has been remarked upon. The self-sufficiency still lingers." On 32:11 it adds that Jacob "makes no reference to the possible cause of Esau's anger." Barnes on 32:24 says "the prominent feature of his character was a strong and firm reliance on himself," and that he "is now to learn the lesson of unreserved reliance on God." Rashi reads "I am unworthy" (32:10) as a fear rather than only modesty: "My merits are diminished in consequence of all the kindness and truth which You have already shown me. For this reason I am afraid." Genesis Rabbah 78:7 tells a fable of a fox who boasts of three hundred parables to calm a lion and forgets them all. Rabbi Yehuda bar Simon and Rabbi Levi apply it to Jacob, who first trusted his ability to pray or to make war and at the meeting could only let "the merit of each and every person stand on his behalf."

Against the faith reading, Kalisch (as the Pulpit Commentary reports him) held that the prayer "ought to have been offered before resorting to the preceding precautions." Against the self-reliance reading, the Pulpit Commentary holds that the prayer's "profound humility" is "a sure indication that the character of Jacob had either undergone a great inward transformation" or had "shaken off the moral and spiritual lethargy."

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, as a model of the shape, not an answer key. It does not build on the wrestling, whose meaning Positions (1) shows readers do not agree on.

**Passage element.** Genesis 32:9-12. Before a meeting he dreads, Jacob recalls what he was told (32:9), names what he has received ("with only my staff I came across the Jordan, but now I have become two camps," 32:10), and then names his fear in plain words ("I am afraid that he may come and attack me," 32:11).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Jacob has wronged his brother (27:35-36) and does not know how he will be received. The prayer joins thanks and fear rather than hiding one behind the other. Calvin reads its thanks as Jacob gathering "materials for confidence."

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Before a hard conversation, naming plainly both what one has been given and what one fears can be a more honest preparation than either rehearsing excuses or pretending to be calm.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a person preparing to face someone they once wronged, and the act of naming gratitude and fear plainly. Not carried forward: the angels, the camps, the gift as appeasement, the wrestling, the promises of offspring.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed. "I will surely make you prosper" (32:12) was spoken to Jacob. Whether and how it applies to anyone else is a question for the reader's own tradition, not for this bridge.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. Someone missed a commitment that cost a colleague a deadline months ago, never addressed it, and now must work closely with that colleague again.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: honest self-examination. One faithful response is to write two short lists before the first meeting, "what I have been given here" and "what I am afraid of," and then to add a third: what I owe this person.

**Cautions.** The bridge does not say that the other person must forgive or accept, or that a gift can settle a wrong. Esau's answer is his own (Genesis 33). It also does not fit a situation in which someone fears for their physical safety, as Jacob did. That calls for practical protection and qualified help, not a study exercise. The bridge is an analogy the learner is testing and may find does not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. Genesis 32:30 and Exodus 33:20 both use "face" and "live." Which details in each passage would you need before saying whether they conflict?
2. Hosea says Jacob "wept and sought His favor" (Hosea 12:4). Genesis 32 has no weeping. Does Hosea add to the story, interpret it, or draw on another telling?
3. Read 32:20 with the Hebrew "face" words restored, then 32:30 and 33:10. What does the repetition do to the order of the scenes?
4. Rabbi Berekhya first says "We do not know who was victorious," then settles it from the verb. Does 32:25-29 support his answer, or does it point both ways?
5. Jacob asks for a blessing (32:26) from someone who will not give his name (32:29). What does the text let you say about what Jacob knew, and when?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 32:1-32 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) Give the four parts of Jacob's prayer. (c) Explain why a Hebrew Bible's verse numbers for this chapter differ from the BSB's.
2. **Five-minute outline.** Sequence and key words, especially "camp" and "face" (Context); the prayer and the name questions (Literary Design); the four readings of the wrestler (Positions 1); the name Israel (Positions 2); Exodus 33:20 (Positions 3); trust or self-reliance (Positions 4); the three connections (prompt 5).
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own first. If you are stuck, here is one: the text calls the wrestler "a man" and Hosea calls him "the angel," so reading him as God himself says more than either text. Check first what Calvin and Keil do with "man" and "angel." A second candidate, running the other way: if he was only an angel, why does the name's explanation say "you have struggled with God," and why does Jacob name the place "face of God"?
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) It does not name the wrestler; he refuses his name (32:29). (b) It does not say Jacob limped for the rest of his life. Calvin holds that he was "lame even to the end of his life"; Skinner calls that idea "a gratuitous addition to the narrative." (c) It does not say when the food custom of 32:32 began; Mishnah Chullin 7:6 records a dispute over non-kosher animals that turns on exactly that.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** The reviewed connections attached to this lesson:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hosea 12:3-4 | explicit_reference | explicit | Names Jacob (12:2) and recounts the event: "he wrestled with God ... he struggled with the angel and prevailed"; Genesis records only one such wrestling |
| Genesis 33:10 | motif | strong | "I have seen your face, and it is like seeing the face of God" repeats 32:30's *pānîm* and *ʾĕlōhîm*; the Cambridge Bible, Skinner and Genesis Rabbah 77:3 all read the two together |

Choose one row and defend it, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` for a candidate. Candidates this lesson chose not to author:

- **The wrestler as Christ, linked to a New Testament text.** Justin, Benson, Poole, Henry and Gill read him so, but no New Testament text names Peniel or the wrestling: `no_warrant_yet` for any New Testament row.
- **2 Corinthians 12:7, Paul's "thorn in my flesh."** The Pulpit Commentary compares Jacob's limp to "a stake in his flesh" that Paul bore. A commentator's comparison, not a textual link: `no_warrant_yet`.
- **Genesis 48:16, "the angel who has redeemed me from all harm."** The Cambridge Bible on 32:30 says that Jacob "on his deathbed, refers to this event" there. The verse names no place or wrestling; at most a plausible allusion, left unauthored.
- **Genesis 30:8, Rachel's "I have wrestled with my sister."** It belongs to the lesson on Genesis 30, and the Hebrew verb differs from 32:24's (Context).
