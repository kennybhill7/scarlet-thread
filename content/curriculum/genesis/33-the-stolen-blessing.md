Genesis 27:1-28:9 is a family story in which almost everyone acts in secret. A blind father plans to bless his favorite son. A mother overhears, dresses the younger son in the older son's clothes, and sends him in with a goat-meat dish. Jacob says, "I am Esau, your firstborn" (27:19), and receives the blessing. When Esau comes back, Isaac trembles, refuses to take the blessing back, and gives Esau a much harder word. Esau plans murder, Rebekah sends Jacob away, and Isaac blesses Jacob a second time, this time knowingly, with "the blessing of Abraham" (28:4). The narrator never says whether Rebekah or Jacob did wrong. Readers have filled that silence in very different ways, and this lesson reports them in their own words without choosing. The New Testament refers to the scene twice: Hebrews 11:20 says Isaac blessed "by faith", and Hebrews 12:17 says Esau "was rejected" though he "sought the blessing with tears". That second verse, in older English, has frightened people about their own souls, so the lesson reports what its readers say it means and where to turn if the fear is personal.

## Context

**What happens, in order.** Isaac, old and blind, sends Esau to hunt and make him "a tasty dish" before he blesses him (27:1-4). Rebekah overhears and tells Jacob to bring two goats instead (27:5-10). Jacob fears being "revealed to him as a deceiver" and cursed; Rebekah answers, "Your curse be on me, my son" (27:11-13). She dresses him in Esau's clothes and goatskins (27:14-17). Jacob twice claims to be Esau (27:19, 27:24), credits "the LORD your God" for his speed (27:20), and is blessed (27:27-29). Esau returns, and Isaac "began to tremble violently" but says of the first son, "indeed, he will be blessed!" (27:33). Esau weeps and receives a different word (27:34-40). He plans to kill Jacob after Isaac's death (27:41). Rebekah sends Jacob to her brother Laban, giving Isaac a different reason, Esau's Hittite wives (27:42-46). Isaac blesses Jacob and sends him to find a wife (28:1-5). Esau, seeing this, marries a daughter of Ishmael (28:6-9).

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens puts Genesis 12 through Malachi in stage 5. The unit builds on this curriculum's lesson on Genesis 25:19-34 (the oracle and the sold birthright): Esau's charge, "He took my birthright, and now he has taken my blessing" (27:36), joins the two scenes. The sale of the birthright, the oracle "the older will serve the younger" (25:23), its use in Romans 9, and Hebrews 12:16 are handled in that lesson and are not repeated here. Esau's Hittite marriages (26:34-35) set up Rebekah's complaint in 27:46.

**Key words, read as observations.** Every count below was made by this lesson's own script over the app's BSB text, case-insensitive and on word boundaries.

- **"Tasty" (27:4 and five more).** In Genesis the BSB uses "tasty" six times, all in this chapter (27:4, 27:7, 27:9, 27:14, 27:17, 27:31). The meal is the occasion of the blessing each time it is mentioned.
- **"My voice" (27:8, 27:13, 27:43).** Rebekah says "my voice" three times, twice to send Jacob in and once to send him away.
- **Deceit (27:12, 27:35).** Jacob fears he will be "revealed to him as a deceiver" (27:12). Isaac tells Esau, "Your brother came deceitfully" (27:35). The Hebrew in 27:35 is *be-mirmah*. Bible Hub's Strong's entry derives *mirmah* from the verb *ramah*, the verb Jacob himself uses to Laban at 29:25: "Why have you deceived me?" Readers differ on how strong *mirmah* is here. Rashi glosses it "with cleverness (cf. Onkelos)." In Genesis Rabbah 67:4, Rabbi Yohanan reads, "He came with the wisdom of his Torah." The Septuagint has μετὰ δόλου, roughly "with guile."
- **"Cheated me twice" (27:36).** Esau puns on Jacob's name: "Is he not rightly named Jacob? For he has cheated me twice." The Hebrew verb, *aqab* (*vayya'qeveni*), shares its root with the heel Jacob grasped at birth (25:26), as this curriculum's Genesis 25 lesson notes. Strong's lists five occurrences of the verb in four verses: here, Job 37:4, twice in Jeremiah 9:4 ("every brother deals craftily"), and Hosea 12:3 ("In the womb he grasped his brother's heel"). One of the Jeremiah forms, *ya'qov*, has the same consonants as the name Jacob; whether Jeremiah means a play on the patriarch's name is a reader's question.
- **"I am Esau, your firstborn" (27:19) and "I am" (27:24).** The Hebrew of 27:19 is *anokhi Esav bekhorekha*, three words with no verb, and the Septuagint has Ἐγὼ Ἠσαὺ ὁ πρωτότοκός σου. At 27:24 Jacob answers one Hebrew word, *ani*, "I." Both details matter to the readings in Positions.
- **"He will be blessed" (27:33).** Isaac does not withdraw the blessing; he confirms it. The Septuagint reads εὐλογημένος ἔστω, roughly "let him be blessed."
- **"Only one blessing" (27:38).** Esau asks, "Do you have only one blessing, my father? Bless me too, O my father!" In the BSB "Bless me too" occurs in two verses, both Esau's (27:34, 27:38).

**Isaac's word to Esau (27:39-40), two translation questions.** First, the BSB reads, "your dwelling place shall be away from the richness of the land, away from the dew of heaven above" (27:39). The Hebrew preposition *min* is the same as in Jacob's blessing, "May God give to you the dew of heaven and the richness of the earth" (27:28). Keil and Delitzsch take it as "partitive there, and privative here," "from equals away from," and say the opposite reading is that of the Vulgate and Luther. Barnes agrees: "after a noun of place, it denotes distance or separation." Calvin reads it the other way, of a land "sufficiently fertile in itself to render the promise by no means nugatory," and Rashi reads it of a rich land, "the Greek portions of Italy," following Genesis Rabbah 67:6. The Septuagint keeps the ambiguity, with the same ἀπὸ τῆς πιότητος in 27:28 and 27:39. Second, "when you rebel" (27:40) renders *tarid*, from a rare verb (Strong's 7300, *rud*, "to wander restlessly"). The King James Version has "when thou shalt have the dominion," which Ellicott calls "scarcely more than a guess made by two or three ancient Jewish commentators."

**Edom's later history.** "You shall live by the sword and serve your brother. But when you rebel, you will tear his yoke from your neck" (27:40). Calvin observes that "events are here predicted which were never fulfilled in the person of Esau," since Jacob later bowed to his brother. Keil and Delitzsch trace a cycle of "servitude, revolt, and reconquest" from David through Jehoram, Amaziah and Ahaz to John Hyrcanus. Second Kings records one revolt: "In the days of Jehoram, Edom rebelled against the hand of Judah and appointed their own king" (8:20), and "So to this day Edom has been in rebellion against the hand of Judah" (8:22). Benson, Barnes, Gill and the Pulpit Commentary, on 2 Kings 8:20, each name Genesis 27:40 as the prophecy fulfilled there. Two cautions. The BSB's shared "rebel" is an English match only: Genesis 27:40 has *tarid* and 2 Kings 8:20 has *pasha'* (Sefaria's Hebrew). And Jewish readers have read the line conditionally: Rav Huna in Genesis Rabbah 67:7 says, "If he merits, you will serve; if not, you will eradicate," and Rashi reads the yoke as broken "when Israel will transgress the Torah."

**Hebrews 11:20 and 12:16-17.** Hebrews refers to this scene twice. "By faith Isaac blessed Jacob and Esau concerning the future" (11:20, BSB). The Greek editions on Bible Hub divide on one word: Nestle 1904, Westcott-Hort and the Greek Orthodox text read Πίστει καὶ περὶ μελλόντων ("even concerning things to come"), while the Byzantine and both Textus Receptus editions omit καὶ. Then, after naming Esau "who for a single meal sold his birthright" (12:16): "For you know that afterward, when he wanted to inherit the blessing, he was rejected. He could find no ground for repentance, though he sought the blessing with tears" (12:17, BSB). The key clause, μετανοίας γὰρ τόπον οὐχ εὗρεν, is the same in every edition listed. The King James Version renders it "he found no place of repentance"; the BSB has "no ground for repentance" (in the BSB that phrase occurs in this verse only). The final Greek word is a pronoun, αὐτήν, "it"; the BSB renders it "the blessing," which is one of the readings reported in Position 2.

**How the chapter was composed.** The Cambridge Bible, on 28:4, says the verse "would be almost unintelligible, if we were not on literary grounds sure that this section is from the P tradition," independent of chapter 27. David Frankel (TheTorah.com) argues the same: in the strand scholars assign to P (26:34-35, then 27:46 onward), "Jacob never deceived Isaac." He adds that "the Torah chose not to adopt one tradition at the expense of the other." Readers who take the chapter as one account read Rebekah's two speeches (27:42-45 and 27:46) as one strategy; Keil and Delitzsch say she spoke of the Hittite wives "without hurting his feelings by telling him of Esau's murderous intentions." This lesson reads the text as it stands and does not decide how it was put together.

## Literary Design

**Scenes of two.** The chapter moves in pairs: Isaac and Esau (27:1-4), Rebekah and Jacob (27:5-17), Isaac and Jacob (27:18-29), Isaac and Esau (27:30-40), Rebekah and Jacob (27:42-45), Rebekah and Isaac (27:46), Isaac and Jacob (28:1-5). Jacob and Esau never share a scene. At 27:30 one leaves as the other arrives; Rashi glosses the doubled verb, "as one went out the other came in." No scene has God speaking. God and the LORD are named only in the characters' mouths (27:7, 27:20, 27:27-28, 28:3-4).

**The senses.** The blind father tests with four senses. He hears ("The voice is the voice of Jacob, but the hands are the hands of Esau," 27:22), touches (27:21-23), tastes (27:25) and smells ("the smell of my son is like the smell of a field that the LORD has blessed," 27:27). Sight is the one sense he lacks (27:1). Readers can notice that the evidence he trusts last, the smell of Esau's clothes, is the one Rebekah prepared (27:15).

**Echoes inside Genesis.** Two later scenes repeat this one's terms. At 29:25-26 Jacob, given the older sister in place of the younger, asks Laban, "Why have you deceived me?" and Laban answers, "It is not our custom here to give the younger daughter in marriage before the older." Chaim Steinmetz (Jewish Journal) notes that commentaries "from the Midrashim onward" read Jacob as "punished, measure for measure, by the deceptions of others," pointing to Laban and to Joseph's brothers deceiving their father "also, while using a goat." Isaac's line "May those who curse you be cursed, and those who bless you be blessed" (27:29) echoes God's promise to Abram (12:3); Keil and Delitzsch call it "moulded according to Genesis 12:3." The second blessing, at 28:3-4, uses the vocabulary of Genesis 17 (see the connections table).

## Positions

Two questions follow, each reported through named readers in their own words and not adjudicated. A third section names one misuse of the passage.

**1. How should Rebekah's plan, Jacob's words and Isaac's part be judged?** The narrator's only evaluation is in Isaac's words, "Your brother came deceitfully" (27:35), and Esau's, "he has cheated me twice" (27:36). Five readings follow.

- **Not a lie but a mystery (Augustine).** In *Against Lying* (*Contra mendacium*, to Consentius, section 24), Augustine writes that what Jacob did at his mother's bidding, "if with diligence and in faith it be attended to, is no lie, but a mystery." (The Latin is *non est mendacium, sed mysterium*.) He explains that the goatskins signify "He who bare not His own, but others' sins," and that Jacob's answer to his father refers to Christ "in His body, which is His Church." In *The City of God* 16.37 he makes a parallel argument from Genesis 25:27, which his Bible rendered "Jacob was a simple man" (the BSB has "a quiet man"): "what the fiction of the man who does not lie, but a profound mystery of the truth?" He adds that Isaac "does not complain that he has been deceived," and asks, "Who would not rather have expected the curse of an angry man here, if these things had been done in an earthly manner, and not by inspiration from above?"
- **Not a lie in its wording (Rashi; Genesis Rabbah).** Rashi divides the words of 27:19: "I am he that brings food to you, and Esau is your first-born." At 27:24 he notes, "He did not say, 'I am Esau', but 'It is I'." Genesis Rabbah 65:18 records a different division from Rabbi Levi: "I am destined to receive the Ten Commandments, but Esau is your firstborn." The midrash also reads Jacob as unwilling ("compelled, coerced, and weeping," 65:15) and calls Rebekah a prophet who knew Esau's plan (67:9). On "he will be blessed," Rabbi Elazar says the verse is there "so that you will not say that had Jacob not deceived Isaac, he would not have received the blessings" (67:2).
- **Faith mixed with fault, in all three (Calvin).** Calvin holds that "the stratagem of Rebekah was not without fault," and that "as a lie is in itself culpable, she sinned more grievously still in this, that she desired to sport in a sacred matter with such wiles." He adds that "her faith was mixed with an unjust and immoderate zeal." Of Jacob's appeal to "the LORD your God" he writes that "his fault was not free from perjury," and that "the truth of God was not to be aided by such falsehoods." Of Isaac's plan to bless Esau he writes, "his obstinate attachment to his son was a kind of blindness." Yet he reads Isaac's trembling as faith: "I have blessed him, yea, and he shall be blessed, was the effect of a rare and precious faith." Of Rebekah's part he concludes that God "even executed his design through the deceit of a woman."
- **Wrong and repaid (Keil and Delitzsch; Genesis Rabbah 67:4).** Keil and Delitzsch hold that "the deceit by which his mother prompted him to secure the blessing was never approved. On the contrary, the sin was followed by immediate punishment." Rebekah "did not see him for twenty years"; Jacob "had to atone for his sin against both brother and father by a long and painful exile"; Isaac "was punished for retaining his preference for Esau." Genesis Rabbah, which elsewhere defends Jacob, also records this line: "Jacob caused Esau to cry out one cry," and God repaid it centuries later "in the Shushan citadel," when Mordecai "cried out an exceedingly loud and bitter cry" (67:4, citing Esther 4:1).
- **Isaac knew (Zucker).** David J. Zucker (TheTorah.com, 2024) builds on Genesis Rabbah 65:19, where Isaac reasons, "I know that Esau would not mention the name of the Holy One blessed be He; this one mentions." Zucker proposes, "we can imagine that Isaac knows about Rebecca's plan all along," citing the couple's closeness and noting that "Isaac is not angry when the ruse is revealed" and blesses Jacob again in 28:1. He sets his reading against commentators who see "a doddering old man," quoting Gerhard von Rad's description of Rebekah's "monstrous crime" (von Rad is quoted here only through Zucker).

**Objections each reading faces.** Each reading has been answered by a named reader.

- *Against the "mystery" reading,* Calvin, without naming Augustine, holds that Jacob "insinuates himself by many lies" and that "although it tended to a right end, it was not lawful to attain that end, through this oblique course," though he adds at once that "faith prevailed over these impediments."
- *Against reading the words as no lie,* Steinmetz calls Rashi's comment on 27:19 "an excellent example" of a tendency in which "Jacob's ethical failings are rationalized and defended," and sets beside it medieval commentators such as Joseph Bechor Shor who "don't romanticize the actions of Biblical heroes" (Bechor Shor is reported here only through Steinmetz).
- *Against crediting Rebekah's faith,* von Rad, as Zucker quotes him, calls her act a "monstrous crime."
- *Against the "repaid" reading,* Augustine notes that Isaac "does not complain that he has been deceived," and Genesis Rabbah has Rabbi Abahu say Isaac blessed Jacob again because "the blessings were uncertain in his hand" (67:12), a confirmation, not a punishment.
- *Against "Isaac knew,"* Keil and Delitzsch hold that "The shock was inconceivable which Isaac received, when he found that he had blessed another," and Calvin, that Isaac "was suspicious of fraud" but "with respect to the person, he was plainly in error."

These readings do not agree on whether a lie was told, who bears the fault, or whether the outcome excuses the means. This lesson does not decide between them.

**2. What does Hebrews 12:17 mean by "no ground for repentance," and whose repentance?** The King James wording, "he found no place of repentance," has long been read as saying a person can seek repentance with tears and be refused. Alexander MacLaren, preaching on the verse, says it has often been "understood as teaching a very ghastly and terrible doctrine," and that "Such teaching has burdened many a heart." The Cambridge Bible records an older episode: "The abuse of this passage to support the merciless severity of the Novatians was one of the reasons why the Epistle was somewhat discredited in the Western Church." Three readings of the clause follow.

- **Isaac's change of mind.** Barnes takes it as "no place for repentance 'in the mind of isaac,' or no way to change his mind." He writes that "It does not mean that Esau earnestly sought to repent and could not." He adds that the passage "should not be alleged to show" that a sinner "cannot repent," but that it warns against "neglecting religion until death comes; because when God has once pronounced a sentence excluding us from his favor, no tears, or pleading, or effort of our own can change him," and he links it to Hebrews 6:1-6 on apostasy. Meyer holds the same reading, "Esau did not succeed in causing his father Isaac to change his mind," and lists many earlier holders, beginning with Beza. Gill calls it "the better interpretation."
- **Esau's own repentance, too late or not genuine.** Calvin reads it of Esau: "he profited nothing, he gained nothing by his late repentance, though he sought with tears the blessing which by his own fault he had lost." He then asks whether "the sinner, endued with repentance, gains nothing by it," and answers that "repentance here is not to be taken for sincere conversion to God," and that "whenever a sinner sighs on account of his sins, the Lord is ready to pardon him, nor is God's mercy ever sought in vain." He goes on: "but as the tears of Esau were those of a man past hope, they were not shed on account of having offended God." Bengel takes the "repentance" as "that distress (anxiety or labour) of mind in Esau demanding the blessing afterwards," and says of it, "The nature of the thing did not admit of it."
- **The tears were for the blessing.** Ellicott places "for he found no place of repentance" in parentheses, so that Esau "sought the blessing earnestly with tears" and was refused, because "that first act (Genesis 25:33) could not be recalled." The Cambridge Bible prefers the same: "his repentance, bitter as it was, could not avert the earthly consequence of his profanity." It adds, "The text gives no ground for pronouncing on Esau's future fate," and that his "repentance," "if it failed, could only have been a spurious repentance." MacLaren, who also reads the tears as sought for the blessing, says the verse means Esau "found no field on which such repentance as he had could operate - so as to undo that which was past." The BSB's "sought the blessing with tears" renders the Greek pronoun this way.

**Objections each reading faces.**

- *Against Isaac's change of mind,* Vincent calls it "unnatural, forced, and highly improbable," and the Cambridge Bible objects that "in the N. T. usage the word 'repentance' (μετάνοια) is always subjective." Ellicott calls such readings "much less probable."
- *Against Esau's too-late repentance,* Meyer writes that it is "false at least for the application of the statement, since in the Christian domain a repentance that is worthy of the name can never be too late."
- *Against reading the tears as for the blessing,* Meyer objects to "the harshness and unnaturalness of the grammatical construction." MacLaren himself grants that the parenthesis "may not, perhaps, be legitimate," because "found" and "sought" are joined in one sentence.

This lesson does not decide between these readings. *A pastoral note:* if this verse has stopped being a study question and become a fear that you have sinned past forgiveness, please talk with a pastor who knows you. A study note cannot carry that weight. For some people the fear returns no matter how often it is answered. The International OCD Foundation describes scrupulosity as "A form of obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) involving religious or moral obsessions," and lists "Going to hell" among its common fears. Its packet for faith leaders notes that "providing reassurance serves to maintain the anxiety disorder cycle." If that sounds familiar, a licensed counselor can help alongside your pastor; the Foundation lists therapists and support groups at iocdf.org/find-help. If the fear ever comes with thoughts of harming yourself, in the United States you can call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. Elsewhere, call your local emergency number.

**A misuse: Jacob "the deceiver" as a figure for Jews.** Jacob's dealings with his brother, which Esau sums up in 27:36 ("He took my birthright, and now he has taken my blessing"), have been turned against the Jewish people, read as a portrait of a people rather than of one man in one family quarrel. Chaim Steinmetz reports that Joseph Official, the thirteenth-century author of the polemical work *Sefer HaMekaneh*, met a friar on the road to Paris who told him, "Jacob your father was a thief, and no greater extractor of usury was there," over the price of the birthright. (Steinmetz calls the friar a Capuchin; that order was founded in 1525, per Wikipedia, so the label cannot be exact for the thirteenth century. Joseph Official's book was not read for this lesson.) Steinmetz places the friar's charge among the ways "antisemites have always found a way to reconcile their reverence for the Bible with their loathing of the Jews." He reports Joseph Official's answer, shared with the Rashbam, that "Jacob actually paid full price to Esau," and adds that Joseph Official, unlike a modern Jewish reader, did not have "that luxury" of reading Jacob critically. Several things can be checked against the text. The charge of deceit in 27:35-36 is spoken by Isaac and Esau about one son. Within the Hebrew Bible, the heel-grasping image is used by Israel's own prophet to rebuke his own people (Hosea 12:2-3), not by outsiders to describe them. Genesis itself goes on to show Jacob deceived (29:25) and the brothers reconciled: Esau "ran to him and embraced him" (33:4). Hebrews names Isaac's blessing of Jacob and Esau as an act of faith (11:20). A separate figural use, the twins as Church and Synagogue, is reported in this curriculum's Genesis 25 lesson, with the Second Vatican Council's rejection of that kind of reading; Augustine's reading of Isaac's blessing in *City of God* 16.37 belongs to that tradition and is not repeated here.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, as a model of the shape, not an answer key.

**Passage element.** Genesis 27:12-13: "'What if my father touches me? Then I would be revealed to him as a deceiver, and I would bring upon myself a curse rather than a blessing.' His mother replied, 'Your curse be on me, my son. Just obey my voice and go get them for me'" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: Jacob's objection is about being caught, not about whether the plan is right; Rebekah answers by taking the risk on herself, not by answering the question. Inference (this lesson's own): for a first audience, the scene shows a family member persuaded into deception by someone who believes the end is right and offers to carry the consequences. Whether the first audience judged the plan wrong is disputed (Position 1).

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When someone asks us to deceive a family member for a good end and offers to take the blame, the question of whether it is right is still ours to answer.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a family, a hidden plan, an end the planner believes is right, and a person asked to carry it out. Not carried forward: the oracle, the patriarchal blessing, God's purposes, and any verdict on who in a modern family is "Jacob," "Rebekah" or "Esau."

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a narrative pattern applied by analogy. It is not a command. Promise scope: none. The passage promises no outcome to anyone who refuses or agrees.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family and elder care. An adult daughter is asked by her sibling to tell their aging, half-blind father that a document is a routine form so that he will sign over a property the sibling believes he had always meant her to have. The sibling says, "If it goes wrong, it's on me."

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: honesty and restraint. One faithful response is for her to decline to misdescribe the document, to say plainly to the sibling why, and to suggest that the father's wishes be put to him openly, with his own licensed attorney present.

**Cautions.** The bridge does not say that every family secret is a lie, that either sibling is a villain, or that the father's wishes are known. Questions about wills, property and capacity need a licensed attorney, not a study note. If an older adult is being pressured or deceived about money or property, an elder-law attorney or your local adult protective services agency is the right next step.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. The narrator states no verdict on Rebekah or Jacob, but has Isaac say "deceitfully" (27:35) and later lets Laban deceive Jacob (29:25). Is that a verdict by arrangement, or a reader's inference?
2. Isaac trembles and then says, "indeed, he will be blessed!" (27:33). Augustine, Calvin, Keil and Delitzsch and Zucker each explain that line differently. Which explanation needs the fewest things the text does not say?
3. Hebrews 11:20 says Isaac blessed "by faith" in a scene Isaac himself calls deceit (27:35). What does each reading in Position 1 need "by faith" to mean?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 27:1-28:9 in order, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) Recite Jacob's words in 27:19 and his answer in 27:24 in the BSB's words. (c) Name the two New Testament verses that refer to this scene.
2. **Five-minute outline.** (0:00-1:00) the plot and its scenes of two; (1:00-2:00) the key words, *mirmah* and *aqab*, and the two translation questions in 27:39-40; (2:00-3:00) the five readings of the deception and their objections; (3:00-4:15) Hebrews 12:17, the three readings, their objections, and the pastoral note as written; (4:15-5:00) the misuse and what the text shows.
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: reading 27:19 as "I am he that brings food to you, and Esau is your first-born" ignores that Jacob says "I am" again at 27:24 when asked, "Are you really my son Esau?" Weigh it against Rashi's own note on 27:24. A second candidate, running the other way: calling the scene simply a sin ignores that Isaac confirms the blessing and Hebrews calls it faith. Weigh it against Keil and Delitzsch, who hold both that the deceit "was never approved" and that "a higher hand prevailed."
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Genesis 27 does not state God's approval or disapproval of Rebekah's plan; God does not speak in the chapter. (b) Genesis 27 does not mention repentance; the word comes from Hebrews 12:17, and Genesis records Esau's tears and his plan to kill (27:38, 27:41). (c) Genesis 27 does not describe any people as deceivers; Isaac's and Esau's words concern one son.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** The connections attached to this lesson:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hebrews 11:20 | explicit_reference | explicit | Names Isaac blessing "Jacob and Esau," which Genesis narrates only at 27:27-40; no formula |
| Hebrews 12:17 | explicit_reference | explicit | Continues 12:16's named Esau with the blessing, the rejection and the tears of 27:30-40; completes conn-gen25-birthright-hebrews12, which stops at 12:16 |
| Genesis 12:3 (from 27:29) | allusion | strong | Shares the bless-and-curse formula; Keil and Delitzsch call 27:29 "moulded according to Genesis 12:3" |
| Genesis 17:1-8 (from 28:3-4) | explicit_reference | strong | Names "the blessing of Abraham"; "God Almighty" and the Hebrew "land of your sojournings" match Genesis 17, but commentators differ on which promise is meant |

Choose one row and defend it, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` for a candidate. Three candidates were considered and not authored:

- **2 Kings 8:20-22** (Edom's revolt). Benson, Barnes, Gill and the Pulpit Commentary call it the fulfillment of 27:40. But 2 Kings does not refer back to Genesis, the shared "rebel" is English only (*tarid* against *pasha'*), and this curriculum's Genesis 25 lesson declined 2 Samuel 8:14 on the same grounds.
- **Galatians 3:14** ("the blessing promised to Abraham"). The Greek, ἡ εὐλογία τοῦ Ἀβραὰμ, matches the Septuagint of 28:4, τὴν εὐλογίαν Ἀβραὰμ, though the BSB's English differs. But the commentators on Galatians 3:14 read for this lesson explain the phrase from Paul's own argument in Galatians 3:8-9 (the Pulpit Commentary says the passage cited in 3:8 "suggested the notion of 'the blessing of Abraham'"), and none of them cites Genesis 28:4. That pairing already has a row, conn-gen12-blessing-galatians3-8.
- **Numbers 24:9** ("Blessed are those who bless you and cursed are those who curse you"). Its Hebrew is closer to 27:29 than 12:3 is, and Rashi compares the two. It belongs to a Numbers lesson.

- **A correction to this lesson's brief.** The phrase "non mendacium sed mysterium" is not in *City of God* 16.37. It is in *Against Lying* (*Contra mendacium*) 10.24, in the form *non est mendacium, sed mysterium*. *City of God* 16.37 makes the parallel point in different words (*quae fictio non mentientis, nisi profundum mysterium ueritatis?*). Both are quoted above, each with its own reference.
