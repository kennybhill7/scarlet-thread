Genesis 18:16-33 is a conversation held at a spot overlooking Sodom (18:16). Three visitors have just eaten at Abraham's tent and promised Sarah a son (18:1-15). As they leave, two of them walk on toward Sodom, and Abraham is left "standing before the LORD" (18:22). What follows is a sustained exchange between Abraham and God: the LORD first asks whether to hide His plan from Abraham (18:17), then tells him that "The outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah is great" (18:20), and Abraham answers with a question that has been quoted ever since: "Will not the Judge of all the earth do what is right?" (18:25). He then asks six times, the number of righteous people falling from fifty to ten, and each time the LORD agrees. This lesson does three things and settles none of them. It reads the dialogue word by word, including the vocabulary that ties Abraham's argument in 18:25 to the LORD's own words about him in 18:19. It reports how named Christian and Jewish readers disagree about what the bargaining is doing and why it stops at ten. And it follows the passage forward into Jeremiah, Ezekiel and James. What Sodom's sin actually was is not taken up here. Genesis 19, the next lesson's passage, is where that question, including its contested modern debate, belongs. Every quotation attributed to the BSB below, here and in this lesson's four connection files, was printed from this app's shipped corpus before drafting and checked afterward by a second script; see Sources and Limits.

## Context

**What happens, in order (18:16-33).** "When the men got up to leave, they looked out over Sodom, and Abraham walked along with them to see them off" (18:16). The LORD speaks, not yet to Abraham: "Shall I hide from Abraham what I am about to do?" (18:17), giving two reasons. Abraham "will surely become a great and powerful nation, and through him all the nations of the earth will be blessed" (18:18), and "I have chosen him, so that he will command his children and his household after him to keep the way of the LORD by doing what is right and just, in order that the LORD may bring upon Abraham what He has promised" (18:19). Then: "The outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah is great. Because their sin is so grievous, I will go down to see if their actions fully justify the outcry that has reached Me. If not, I will find out" (18:20-21). "And the two men turned away and went toward Sodom, but Abraham remained standing before the LORD" (18:22). Abraham "stepped forward" and asks, "Will You really sweep away the righteous with the wicked?" (18:23), proposes fifty righteous people (18:24), and objects twice, "Far be it from You!" (18:25). The LORD agrees to spare "the whole place" for fifty (18:26). Abraham, calling himself "but dust and ashes" (18:27), asks again for forty-five, forty, thirty, twenty and ten, and is answered each time (18:28-32). "When the LORD had finished speaking with Abraham, He departed, and Abraham returned home" (18:33).

**Where this unit sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens (`content/lens/eleven-stages.json`) places Genesis 12 through Malachi in stage 5, "Israel: The Twelve Tribes" (machine-readable `chapters` field `["1.12"]`), so this lesson sits in the same stage as this curriculum's other Abraham units. The passage continues the visit of 18:1-15 without a break: "the men" who ate at Abraham's tent (18:8) are the ones who "got up to leave" (18:16). Its sequel comes in the next chapter. "Early the next morning, Abraham got up and returned to the place where he had stood before the LORD" (19:27), saw "smoke rising from the land like smoke from a furnace" (19:28), and the narrator adds that God "remembered Abraham, and He brought Lot out of the catastrophe" (19:29). The events of Genesis 19 belong to the next lesson. They are named here only because 19:27 points back to this conversation.

**What 18:20-21 says about Sodom, and what this lesson leaves for later.** In this passage the LORD says three things about the cities: "The outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah is great," "their sin is so grievous," and "I will go down to see if their actions fully justify the outcry that has reached Me." The passage does not, in these verses, say what the sin was, who was crying out, or what the outcry said. Readers have answered those questions in very different ways, and the answers draw on Genesis 19, on Ezekiel 16:49-50, and on later Jewish and Christian tradition. That is the companion Genesis 19 lesson's work, including the contested modern debate over it. Here the lesson reports only what 18:20-21 itself says.

**Key words, read as observations.**

- **"I have chosen him" (18:19).** The Hebrew verb, per Bible Hub's interlinear, is *yada*, "I have known him," followed by *lemaʿan*, "in order that." Readers divide over how to take it. Ellicott notes that the older rendering "I know him, that he will" (the KJV's) "has most of the Versions in its favour," but that "the Hebrew is, For I have known him in order that he may command his sons." Rashi calls the verb "an expression denoting" affection and argues that if the verb is read as the Targum reads it, as knowing something "of" Abraham, then *lemaʿan* "does not fit into the sense." The Pulpit Commentary lists Gesenius, Delitzsch, Keil and others for the sense of election, and the Septuagint, Vulgate, Targums, Luther and Calvin for the older one. The BSB's "I have chosen him" follows the first group.
- **"what is right and just" (18:19) and "do what is right" (18:25).** These two phrases share Hebrew vocabulary. In 18:19 the interlinear has *laʿasot tsedaqah u-mishpat* (Strong's 6666 and 4941), which Bible Hub glosses "by doing what is right and just." In 18:25 it has *yaʿaseh mishpat* (Strong's 4941), glossed "do justice." Both use the verb "do" (*ʿasah*) and the noun *mishpat*. One caution about the English: the BSB's "what is right" translates *tsedaqah* in 18:19 but *mishpat* in 18:25, so an English reader sees a match that is not word for word. The Cambridge Bible on 18:25 gives the literal sense, "Lit. 'do judgement.' The Judge (shôphêt) will do judgement (mishpât)." Read side by side, the two verses run like this. In 18:19 the LORD says He chose Abraham so that his household would do *mishpat*. In 18:25 Abraham asks whether the Judge will do *mishpat*. That the vocabulary is shared can be checked. What the link signifies is part of what the Positions below dispute.
- **"the Judge of all the earth" (18:25).** A script scan of this app's whole BSB finds the phrase only here. The Cambridge Bible calls it "A very remarkable declaration that Jehovah is supreme throughout the world," and qualifies that at once: "This is not monotheism, but it is the stage next before it."
- **"spare the place" (18:24, 18:26).** The verb is *nasa*, "lift" or "carry." The Cambridge Bible notes that it "means literally 'and take away for the place,' i.e. its guilt, and so 'forgive,' as in Numbers 14:19." Keil and Delitzsch gloss it the same way: "to take away and bear the guilt, i.e., forgive."
- **"Lord" and "LORD."** Inside Abraham's own speech the BSB always writes "Lord" (18:27, 18:30, 18:31, 18:32), the convention for *Adonai*. "LORD," the convention for the divine name, appears only in the narrator's lines (for example 18:22, 18:26, 18:33; in 18:29 the BSB supplies "the LORD" where the Hebrew has "to Him"). A script check of 18:23-32 confirms the pattern in this translation. Ramban builds an argument on the same distinction (see Positions 2); his translator, Charles Chavel, explains in a note that for Ramban "The Tetragrammaton signifies Divine mercy, while the Name beginning with Aleph Dalet signifies Divine justice."
- **"dust and ashes" (18:27).** The Cambridge Bible notes "Two alliterative words in the Heb. (âphar va-êpher) which defy reproduction in English," and cross-references Job 30:19 and 42:6. A scan of this app's BSB finds "dust and ashes" in exactly those three verses.
- **"I will go down to see" (18:21).** The commentators read here treat the inquiry as spoken "after the manner of men" (Benson's phrase) and differ about what it is for. Ellicott: "God examines before He punishes (see Note on Genesis 11:5) with the same care and personal inspection as the most conscientious earthly judge." Rashi: "This teaches the judges that they should not give decisions in cases involving capital punishment, except after having carefully looked into the matter." Calvin: "Moses introduces Him as speaking according to the manner of men."
- **"Abraham remained standing before the LORD" (18:22).** Rashi records a tradition (Genesis Rabbah 49:7) that the wording was once "And the Lord stood yet before Abraham," changed by scribes "to avoid an apparently irreverent expression." The Pulpit Commentary reports the same tradition and rejects it. The difference is noted, not decided, here.

## Literary Design

**Six requests, six answers.** Abraham's numbers and the LORD's replies, as the BSB words them:

| Verse | Abraham | The LORD |
|---|---|---|
| 18:24-26 | "What if there are fifty righteous ones in the city?" | "If I find fifty righteous ones within the city of Sodom, on their account I will spare the whole place." |
| 18:28 | "suppose the fifty righteous ones lack five" | "If I find forty-five there, I will not destroy it." |
| 18:29 | "Suppose forty are found there?" | "On account of the forty, I will not do it." |
| 18:30 | "Suppose thirty are found there?" | "If I find thirty there, I will not do it." |
| 18:31 | "suppose twenty are found there?" | "On account of the twenty, I will not destroy it." |
| 18:32 | "Suppose ten are found there?" | "On account of the ten, I will not destroy it." |

Three things can be checked in the table. Each answer grants exactly what was asked, with no counter-offer. Abraham's deference grows as the numbers fall: "Now that I have ventured to speak to the Lord—though I am but dust and ashes" (18:27), "May the Lord not be angry, but let me speak further" (18:30), and "let me speak once more" (18:32). And the LORD never names a number of His own. Barnes counts the same pattern: "He ventures to repeat his intervention six times, every time diminishing the number," and "In every case he grants his petition." Keil and Delitzsch note that "let me speak once more" (18:32) renders a Hebrew phrase meaning "only this (one) time more," as in Exodus 10:17.

**A frame of looking.** The passage opens with the visitors looking "out over Sodom" (18:16), and its sequel has Abraham return to the same place and look "down toward Sodom and Gomorrah" (19:28). The parallel is checkable in the BSB of both verses, and Bible Hub's interlinear shows the same Hebrew verb in both (*shaqaf*, Strong's 8259, which Bible Hub glosses "looked," followed in both verses by *ʿal-pene*, "on the face of," Sodom). No source read for this lesson discusses the parallel, so it is offered as an observation, not a cited finding.

**A speech to Himself before a speech to Abraham.** In 18:17-19 the LORD talks about Abraham in the third person before addressing him. The Cambridge Bible reads "the LORD said" as "i.e. within Himself," comparing 20:11, where "said" means "thought." Ramban offers two options, that He spoke "to the host of heaven standing by Him" or that the word "refers to thought." On either reading, the reader overhears the reasons for the disclosure before Abraham hears the disclosure itself.

## Positions

Three questions, each reported through named readers in their own words. None is decided here, and no position below is this lesson's own conclusion. Positions report what commentators conclude; the Context section reports what the text itself states.

**1. What is the bargaining doing?** The text records six requests granted. Readers disagree about who is moving whom.

- **Prayer that God allows to move Him.** Keil and Delitzsch quote Franz Delitzsch: "This 'seemingly commercial kind of entreaty is,' as Delitzsch observes, 'the essence of true prayer.'" Delitzsch goes on to say that God "allows them to work upon Him by means of their faith." On this reading the exchange is a real petition that has a real effect. Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, on 18:23, read the scene the same way, as "showing in an unmistakable manner the efficacy of prayer and intercession."
- **God drawing out what He had already decided.** Calvin, on 18:17: "Seeing that God here takes counsel, as if concerning a doubtful matter, he does it for the sake of men; for he had already determined what he would do. But he designed, in this manner, to render Abraham more intent upon the consideration of the causes of Sodom's destruction."
- **Disclosure because the case was still open.** Ramban, on 18:18, reads the disclosure differently: if God hid His plan, future generations might ask how "the righteous one" could "be so callous about his close neighbors and have no mercy on them, not praying at all in their behalf." His paraphrase of God's reasoning continues: "if it is possible in keeping with righteousness and justice to free the cities from destruction, he will pray before Me to let them go, and it will be well and good. And if they are completely guilty, he too will want their judgment. Therefore, it is proper that he enter in the council of G-d." For Ramban the outcome was not yet settled when Abraham spoke. On 18:21: "He thus informed him that their judgment was not yet complete for now He will visit their sin and judge them." On 18:26, God did not say "Know that there is not such a number there as you said," "since their trial had not been completed."
- **A heart attuned to God's compassion.** The Catechism of the Catholic Church (2571), in its section on prayer, reads the scene through Abraham's own disposition rather than through either party's strategy: "After that, once God had confided his plan, Abraham's heart is attuned to his Lord's compassion for men and he dares to intercede for them with bold confidence."
- **How Abraham argues: strategy, growing confidence, or distress.** On the method itself, three Christian commentators read the descending numbers three ways. The Pulpit Commentary, on 18:23, holds that Abraham's aim was "the rescue of their entire populations," and that "with what might be characterized as holy adroitness he veils his ulterior aim, and commences his petition at a Point somewhat removed from that to which he hopes to come." Pulpit gives a reason: he held back at first, "perhaps regarding such an absolute reversal of the Divine purpose as exceeding the legitimate bounds of creature supplication." Ellicott, on the same verse, rejects that picture: "Neither must we suppose that Abraham adroitly began with a large number, with the intention of lessening it. It was the readiness with which each prayer was heard which made him in his earnestness continue his entreaties," which on his reading shows that "the faith of the believer grows strong as he feels that his prayers are accepted." Calvin, on 18:27, raises the objection that Abraham "does but sophistically trifle with God" and answers "that this was rather to be considered as the language of a perturbed mind." Rashi, on 18:23, drawing on Genesis Rabbah 49:8, reads the verb of 18:23 (BSB "stepped forward"; "drew near" in Rashi's translation) as covering three modes at once: Abraham approached "to fight, by speaking stern words, and to persuade by entreaty, and to pray."

**2. What does "do what is right" appeal to?** Abraham's argument in 18:25 is about justice: the righteous should not be "treated alike" with the wicked. Readers differ on whether he is appealing to strict justice, to mercy, or to both.

- **Justice, not covenant grace.** Keil and Delitzsch, disagreeing with Kurtz, write that Abraham neither interceded out of special concern for Lot "nor did he appeal to the covenant grace of Jehovah, but to His justice alone." The Pulpit Commentary reports the same disagreement: "The patriarch appeals not to Jehovah's covenant grace (Kurtz), but to his absolute judicial equity (Keil)."
- **Not strict justice, but equity or mercy.** Gill: "by 'right' Abraham seems to mean, not strict rigorous justice, but a mixture of mercy with justice, even moderation and clemency." Gill also distinguishes eternal from temporal judgment: "to slay the righteous with the wicked" is ruled out in a way "which is true of eternal punishment, but not of temporal calamities, in which the righteous are often involved with the wicked." Matthew Poole likewise says Abraham "speaks not here of strict and rigorous justice, but of that moderate and equitable way which God is pleased to use with the sons of men, and of that right to temporal deliverances which by virtue of God's gracious covenant and promise did accrue to pious and virtuous persons." Poole thus grounds the "equitable way" partly in covenant promise, closer to the view Keil and Pulpit attribute to Kurtz. Haydock's Catholic commentary, citing Calmet on 18:23, makes a point near Gill's about temporal suffering: "God frequently suffers the just to be here the most afflicted; designing to reward them abundantly hereafter. But this was not so common in the days of Abraham and Job."
- **Both justice and mercy, as two divine attributes.** Ramban reads Abraham's double "Far be it from You" as one appeal to "the attribute of justice, and one for mercy." On his reading, Abraham thought the judgment would be by strict justice, and "the Holy One, blessed be He, conceded that He would forgive the entire place for the sake of the fifty righteous, for He will conduct Himself towards them with the attribute of Mercy." He supports this from the divine names (see Key Words): God's reply in 18:26 "is written with the Tetragrammaton, and all references by Abraham to the Divine Name are written Ado-noy."
- **An appeal to God's own nature.** Calvin, on 18:25: "He does not here teach God His duty, as if any one should say to a judge, See what thy office requires," but "reasons from the nature of God, that it is impossible for Him to intend anything unjust." Calvin adds, on 18:23, that "God does not here bind himself by a perpetual rule, so that it shall not be lawful for him, as often as he sees good, to bring the wicked and the just together to punishment." The Cambridge Bible quotes A. B. Davidson's *Theology of the Old Testament* (p. 130) for a related point: "Righteousness is one, whether in God or in man. ... The fact that God is God does not withdraw Him and His actions from the sphere of moral judgement. Nothing would be right in God because He is God, which would not be right in Him were He man." (Davidson is reported here only as the Cambridge Bible quotes him.)

**3. Why fifty, and why stop at ten?** The text gives the numbers but no reason for either end of the sequence.

- **Ten for each of five cities, and Noah's eight.** Rashi, on 18:24, reads fifty as "ten righteous men for each city for there were five localities concerned." On 18:28 he reads Abraham's forty-five as "nine for each city," with God, "the All-Righteous One of the Universe," counted with them as the tenth. On 18:29 he has forty saving four cities, thirty three, twenty two and ten one. On why Abraham stopped at ten (18:32): "For a smaller number he did not plead because he knew already of two instances where less than ten had failed to save the wicked. ... In the generation of the Flood there were eight righteous people, viz., Noah, his sons and their wives, and they could not save their generation," "and for nine in association with God he had already pleaded but had found no acceptance."
- **The same opening number, a different reading of the rest.** Ramban quotes Rashi in full and then objects. If forty could save four cities, "what is this prayer and supplication which he pleads each and every time, saying, oh let not the Lord be angry"? His own reading: "First Abraham said fifty in order to give a perfect number of ten for each city, and then he decreased the number as much as possible, and each time he thought to save all five cities. And I do not know who brought the Rabbi [Rashi] to that which he said." Ramban adds, on the words he renders "within the city" (18:24; the BSB has "in the city"), that Abraham said them "on account of Lot." Calvin and Keil and Delitzsch both reject the idea that concern for Lot alone drove the plea. Keil and Delitzsch: "not by any special interest in Lot, for in that case he would have prayed for his deliverance." Ellicott, on 18:23, also cautions: "Nor must we suppose that he thought only of Lot." The Cambridge Bible holds the opposite: "His concern for Lot, doubtless, forms the motive of the intercession, though Lot's name and relationship are not put forward in extenuation of the plea." Haydock, on 18:32, agrees with Cambridge and gives it as the reason Abraham stopped: "Abraham's chief solicitude was for Lot; though, out of modesty, he does not mention him; trusting, however, in the divine goodness the he would be preserved, unless he had forfeited his justice, he proceeds no farther."
- **Ten as a congregation, as reported by a Christian commentator.** Gill, on 18:32, reports a reading he attributes to Bereshit Rabba 49: "he ended at ten, because he supposed there were ten righteous persons in Lot's family." He gives its count (Lot, his wife, four daughters and four husbands) and then objects in his own voice: "they forgot that two of Lot's daughters were unmarried." He also reports that "ten they say make a congregation, and wherever there are ten righteous persons, a place is saved for their sakes." Genesis Rabbah itself was not read for this lesson.
- **Modesty, charity, and the outcome.** Poole, on 18:32: "Abraham in modesty could proceed no further; and being a good man himself, he had a charitable opinion of others." Matthew Henry, on 18:23-33: "Abraham, indeed, failed in his request for the whole place, but Lot was miraculously delivered." Benson, on 18:33, puts the same outcome differently: "it proved that his prayer was heard; and yet Sodom was not spared, because there were not ten righteous persons in it."

All of these readers take the same six requests and the same six answers as their text. They disagree about who is persuading whom, what kind of justice Abraham invokes, and how the numbers work. This lesson reports them and does not rank them. Some readers ask whether Abraham stopped too soon; no source read here says so (see Sources and Limits). The passage raises a question many readers bring from their own lives: why some prayers seem to be granted and others not, and why the innocent sometimes suffer with the guilty. The Cambridge Bible notes on 18:25 that this "was one of the great problems of religious thought in ancient Israel," and points to the Book of Job. A study note can report what Genesis 18 says and what readers have concluded from it. It cannot answer that question for anyone's own loss. A reader carrying it, especially in grief, should take it to a pastor, priest, rabbi or trusted counselor.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from the passage in its own setting to a modern situation. It models the shape; it is not an answer key, and the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate step.

**Passage element.** Genesis 18:23-25. Abraham speaks up for people who are not his household, to the one with authority to act. He names the specific harm he fears ("sweep away the righteous with the wicked"), appeals to the decision-maker's own standard ("Will not the Judge of all the earth do what is right?"), and speaks with stated deference ("though I am but dust and ashes," 18:27).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text records that Abraham's stated grounds are "the righteous" (18:23-25) and whether the Judge will "do what is right," that he never names Lot, and that he is answered rather than rebuked. Inference: a hearer who already knew 18:19 ("by doing what is right and just") might hear Abraham asking the LORD to act on the same standard Abraham's household is meant to keep. Whether the text intends that link is exactly what the Positions above leave open.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When a decision will fall on many people at once, someone without the authority to decide can still respectfully ask the decision-maker to weigh fairness to those who did nothing wrong, appealing to the decision-maker's own stated standard rather than to personal favor.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a pending collective decision, a concern that blameless people will be treated the same as those at fault, and a respectful appeal to a standard both parties already accept. Not carried forward: the divine Judge, the cities, the numbers, the destruction, and any expectation that a request will be granted the way Abraham's were.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed. Nothing here promises that an appeal, to a person or to God, will be granted, or granted in the form asked.

**Modern domain and situation.** Neighborhood and housing. After repeated damage to an apartment building's shared laundry room by a few residents, the property manager announces that the room will be closed to every tenant for three months. One tenant, who has done nothing wrong and knows several neighbors who rely on the room, considers saying nothing.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: advocacy on behalf of others. One faithful response is to ask the manager, courteously and in writing, whether the building's own posted policy, which promises to address violations with the residents responsible, could be applied instead of a blanket closure, and to offer something practical (a sign-in sheet, a volunteer check) rather than only a complaint.

**Cautions.** This example does not suggest that collective consequences are always unjust; sometimes they are the only workable option, and a manager may reasonably decline. If the situation involves housing law, a disability accommodation, retaliation, or the threat of eviction, a tenant-rights organization or attorney is the right next step, not a study note. The passage is not a text about property management; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing and may find does not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. In 18:19 the LORD says He chose Abraham so that his household would keep His way "by doing what is right and just." In 18:25 Abraham asks whether the Judge will "do what is right." Knowing that the Hebrew shares the verb and the noun *mishpat*, but that the BSB's matching English "what is right" renders different Hebrew words, how much weight would you put on the link? What else in 18:16-33 would you look at before deciding?
2. Calvin reads 18:17 as God taking counsel "for the sake of men"; Delitzsch, as Keil and Delitzsch quote him, reads the exchange as prayer that God "allows ... to work upon Him." Using only the wording of 18:17-33, what would you point to as support for each reading, and is anything in the passage decisive?
3. Rashi and Ramban agree that fifty means ten for each of five cities, and then part ways. Which reading accounts better for Abraham's growing apologies in 18:27-32, in your judgment, and why?
4. Genesis 18 ends with "Abraham returned home" (18:33), and he learns the outcome only by looking from the same place the next morning (19:27-28). What does the passage report Abraham knowing, and not knowing, when the conversation ends?
5. Ezekiel 14:14 says that even Noah, Daniel and Job "could deliver only themselves." Read beside Genesis 18:32, do the two passages conflict, or do they describe different situations? What in each passage's wording would you use to decide?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 18:16-33 in order: the departure, the LORD's two reasons for disclosure, the announcement about the cities, and the six requests. Say what happens, not what it means. (b) Quote 18:25's closing question as this app's BSB gives it, "Will not the Judge of all the earth do what is right?", and say which Hebrew noun it shares with 18:19. (c) State what 18:20-21 says about Sodom, and what it does not say.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) the scene and where it sits between 18:1-15 and 19:27-29 (Context); (1:00-2:00) 18:17-19 and the shared vocabulary of 18:19 and 18:25 (Key Words); (2:00-3:00) the six requests and six answers, read off the table (Literary Design); (3:00-4:00) the three named disputes, held open on purpose (Positions); (4:00-4:30) where the passage travels later in Scripture, with each connection's evidence label (prompt 5); (4:30-5:00) the boundary in prompt 4.
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: by printing the six requests as a table, the lesson makes the scene look like a negotiation, which is exactly the "commercial" reading Delitzsch warns is only "seemingly" so, and it may push a learner toward Pulpit's "holy adroitness" over Ellicott's growing confidence or Calvin's "perturbed mind." Before answering, check what the table actually contains: the BSB's words, unedited, with no comment in either column. A second candidate, running the other way: setting Rashi's and Ramban's arithmetic beside Calvin and Gill may seem to treat a medieval Jewish calculation and a Reformation theology of God's nature as answers to the same question. Ask whether they are.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) Genesis 18:16-33 does not say what Sodom's sin was. It reports an "outcry" and a "grievous" sin (18:20) and leaves the content to Genesis 19 and later readers. (b) The passage does not state a general rule that any ten righteous people will spare any city. The LORD's six answers concern Sodom ("within the city of Sodom," 18:26), and Ezekiel 14:12-20 states that in the case it describes the righteous "could deliver only themselves" (14:14). (c) The passage does not say Abraham's prayer failed or succeeded. It records six requests granted (18:26-32), and the outcome is told in 19:27-29, where readers such as Henry and Benson describe it differently.

Four candidates this lesson considered and did not author: (a) **Moses' intercession in Exodus 32:11-14.** The scenes have a similar shape. God discloses a coming destruction to one man (Exodus 32:10), the man argues, and "the LORD relented" (32:14). But the arguments differ. Abraham pleads for the righteous, appealing to justice; Moses pleads for a guilty people, appealing to God's reputation among "the Egyptians" (32:12) and His oath to "Abraham, Isaac, and Israel" (32:13). No commentary read for this lesson on Genesis 18 or Exodus 32 draws the comparison. Gill, on Ezekiel 22:30, names the two side by side as examples of an intercessor ("as Abraham for Sodom, Moses for Israel"), which is a list rather than an argued link, so `no_warrant_yet` is still the honest grade. A rabbinic comparison of Noah, Abraham and Moses as intercessors in the Zohar was located but not read in a reliable translation (see Sources and Limits). (b) **Christ's intercession (Romans 8:34, "He is interceding for us"; Hebrews 7:25, "He always lives to intercede for them").** Matthew Henry, on 18:23-33, moves from Abraham's plea to Christ by contrast: Abraham pleaded "for the sake of the righteous," while Christ made intercession "Not by blaming the Divine law, nor by alleging aught in extenuation or excuse of human guilt; but by pleading HIS OWN obedience unto death." Neither New Testament verse mentions Abraham or Sodom, so this is devotional at most. (c) **Isaiah 41:8 and 2 Chronicles 20:7 ("Abraham My friend," "Abraham Your friend").** These are the Old Testament sources of the title James uses, and they are named inside the James row rather than given rows of their own, because neither cites Genesis 18. (d) **Amos 3:7, "Surely the Lord GOD does nothing without revealing His plan to His servants the prophets."** The Cambridge Bible and Poole both cross-reference it at 18:17 ("Shall I hide from Abraham"), but only as a parallel thought ("cf.," "See also"), and Amos does not mention Abraham, so this is `no_warrant_yet` as a connection row.
