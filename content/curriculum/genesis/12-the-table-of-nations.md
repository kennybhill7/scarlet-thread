Genesis 10 traces seventy named descendants of Shem, Ham, and Japheth -- a count this lesson checked directly against the real text rather than assuming (14 under Japheth, 30 under Ham, 26 under Shem; see Key Words) -- and this curriculum's own Genesis 9:18-29 lesson already pointed a reader here on purpose, naming this chapter as "the next unit's own territory," the place to see where Shem, Ham, and Japheth's lines actually go. That lesson also issued a specific warning this one inherits directly: Noah's curse in 9:25-27 names only Canaan, three times, with "no stated textual reach" into Ham's other three sons -- Cush, Mizraim, and Put (10:6) -- or their own, far larger set of descendants. This chapter is where that claim can actually be tested against the list it describes. Cush's line (10:7-12), Mizraim's line (10:13-14), and Canaan's own line (10:15-19) are each named here, and this lesson reports what each one's real, identifiable historical and geographic referents are -- Cush with the Nile region south of Egypt, Mizraim with Egypt itself, Canaan with the Levant -- without inventing a rank or hierarchy among the three lines that the text itself does not state anywhere in these thirty-two verses. The chapter is also, on a real and specifically named scholarly observation, an unusual kind of genealogy: it groups people by land, language, clan, and political nation rather than by father-to-son descent alone, stating that grouping three separate times in nearly identical words (10:5, 10:20, 10:31). And it carries two genuine textual puzzles this lesson reports rather than resolves: who Nimrod (10:8-12) actually was, and how this chapter's own repeated references to differing languages relate, in time, to Genesis 11:1's statement that "the whole world had one language" before Babel. Every quotation attributed to the BSB below was checked directly against the shipped corpus (`web/public/bible/BSB/1.json`, and the corresponding files for Deuteronomy, Acts, and Revelation) before this lesson was drafted, not drawn from memory or another translation -- see Sources and Limits for the exact method.

## Context

**What happens, in order (10:1-32).** The chapter opens by naming its own subject: "This is the account of Noah's sons Shem, Ham, and Japheth, who also had sons after the flood" (10:1). Japheth's line comes first (10:2-5): seven sons are named, three further sons of Gomer, four further sons of Javan, and a closing note that "the maritime peoples separated into their territories, according to their languages, by clans within their nations" (10:5). Ham's line follows (10:6-20): four sons -- Cush, Mizraim, Put, and Canaan (10:6) -- then five sons of Cush and two further sons of Raamah (10:7), then a four-verse digression naming Cush's son Nimrod specifically, "a mighty one on the earth" and "a mighty hunter before the LORD," whose kingdom began in Shinar (Babylon, Erech, Accad, Calneh) and extended into Assyria (Nineveh, Rehoboth-Ir, Calah, Resen) (10:8-12). Mizraim's own descendants are named next, seven peoples including a parenthetical note that the Philistines came from the Casluhites (10:13-14), then Canaan's own line: Sidon as firstborn, then ten more named peoples (Heth, the Jebusites, Amorites, Girgashites, Hivites, Arkites, Sinites, Arvadites, Zemarites, Hamathites), with a note that "the Canaanite clans were scattered" and a description of Canaan's own borders, from Sidon toward Gerar as far as Gaza, then toward Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah, and Zeboiim, as far as Lasha (10:15-19). Ham's line closes with the same grouping formula Japheth's did: "according to their clans, languages, lands, and nations" (10:20). Shem's line comes last and runs longest (10:21-31): Shem is called "the older brother of Japheth" (10:21, a clause this curriculum's own Genesis 9:18-29 lesson already flagged as grammatically ambiguous as to which brother is older); five sons are named (Elam, Asshur, Arphaxad, Lud, Aram), then four sons of Aram (10:22-23); the line narrows to one descendant per generation -- Arphaxad to Shelah to Eber (10:24) -- until Eber has two sons, Peleg, "because in his days the earth was divided," and Joktan (10:25); Joktan's own thirteen sons are then named (10:26-29) before a note on their territory (10:30) and the same closing formula a third time, "according to their clans, languages, lands, and nations" (10:31). The chapter's own final verse summarizes the whole: "All these are the clans of Noah's sons, according to their generations and nations. From these the nations of the earth spread out after the flood" (10:32). This is a plain retelling of what the text says, in its own sequence; what each piece means, and what it does not establish, is the rest of this lesson's work.

**Where this chapter sits.** This app's eleven-stage lens (`content/lens/eleven-stages.json`) names stage 4 "Genesis 10-11 -- The Nations and the Tower at Babel," with a machine-readable `chapters` field of `["1.10"]`, placing this chapter at the start of a new stage immediately after stage 3's flood unit (Genesis 6-9) closes. Genesis 10 is that new stage's first unit; Genesis 11:1-9's Tower of Babel narrative is the same stage's second unit and this lesson's own passage boundary stops at 10:32, before it -- the same restraint this curriculum's Genesis 8 lesson used for Genesis 9:11-17's covenant, and its Genesis 9:18-29 lesson used for this very chapter: a reader who wants the full Babel narrative should read that unit next, not treat this lesson's own silence about 11:1-9's events as settling anything about them in advance. Genesis 10:25's "the earth was divided" and 11:1's "one language" are both touched on below (Positions 3) precisely because they sit on either side of that boundary and raise a real question about how the two chapters relate in time -- a question this lesson reports, not one it answers on the next chapter's behalf.

**On Ham's four sons, stated plainly.** Genesis 10:6 names Cush, Mizraim, Put, and Canaan as Ham's four sons, in that order, with no further ranking statement attached to the list itself. Checked directly against the shipped BSB text of 10:7-20, the four lines receive different amounts of narrative attention -- Cush's line gets six verses, including the Nimrod digression; Mizraim's gets two; Canaan's gets five, including a border description; Put's gets none at all, no sons or further descendants named anywhere in this chapter -- but nothing in that difference in length states or implies a difference in standing among the four brothers themselves. This curriculum's own Genesis 9:18-29 lesson already named, and refuted on the text's own terms, a reading that applied Noah's curse on Canaan to Ham's descendants generally and, historically, to Black Africans specifically; that lesson's own textual case (Positions 4 there) is not repeated in full here, but its conclusion bears directly on how this chapter should be read: Cush is the geographic ancestor this lesson identifies below with the Nile region south of Egypt, and Mizraim with Egypt itself, and neither line carries any stated curse, consequence, or lesser status in this chapter's own thirty-two verses.

**Key words, read as observations.**

- **The grouping formula, repeated three times.** "According to their clans, languages, lands, and nations" (10:20) and "according to their clans, languages, lands, and nations" (10:31) are, checked directly against the BSB text of both verses, worded identically. Japheth's own closing note (10:5) uses different word order and vocabulary -- "their territories, according to their languages, by clans within their nations" -- and the chapter's final summary (10:32) drops two of the four terms, closing instead on "according to their generations and nations." That the Ham and Shem formulas match exactly while Japheth's and the chapter's own final summary both vary is a directly checkable feature of the text; what, if anything, the variation is doing is addressed in Literary Design below.
- **נִמְרוֹד** (Nimrod, 10:8-9) receives four verses of narrative digression -- "a mighty one," "a mighty hunter before the LORD," a named kingdom, named cities -- a sharp break from the chapter's otherwise spare "X was the father of Y" formula. Gill's Exposition and Keil and Delitzsch both report a traditional etymology connecting the name to the Hebrew root מָרַד (*marad*, "to rebel"): Gill states "the name of Nimrod is usually derived from 'to rebel,' because he was a rebel against God"; Keil and Delitzsch likewise write that "Nimrod from מרד, 'we will revolt,' points to some violent resistance to God." Both commentators report this as the name's traditional derivation, not as something the text itself states outright; Genesis 10:8-9 never uses the verb *marad* or explains the name at all. Whether Nimrod corresponds to a specific, identifiable historical figure is a live, named, unsettled question -- Positions (2) below reports several real proposals without adopting one.
- **פֶּלֶג** (Peleg, "division," 10:25) is given its name, the text states directly, "because in his days the earth was divided" -- the one place in this chapter where a name is explicitly, textually glossed with a meaning, rather than left for a reader to work out. The verb behind "was divided" (*niphlegah*) shares the same root as the name itself, checkable by comparing the two words' consonants directly. What event this division refers to is addressed in Positions (3) below.
- **שֵׁם וָעֵבֶר** -- Shem's own line narrows, generation by generation, through Arphaxad, Shelah, and Eber (10:21-25) before branching into Peleg and Joktan, and this is the same Eber-to-Peleg line Genesis 11:10-26's later, separate genealogy will pick up again and extend all the way to Abram -- a forward pointer this chapter plants without developing, consistent with the same restraint this lesson's own Context section states about not pre-empting Genesis 11's own territory.
- **The 70-name count, checked directly.** Counting every individually named son, grandson, or further descendant in the chapter's own text (not relying on any secondary source's tally): Japheth's line names 14 (7 sons of Japheth, 3 further sons of Gomer, 4 further sons of Javan); Ham's line names 30 (4 sons of Ham, 5 sons of Cush, 2 further sons of Raamah, Nimrod, 7 peoples descended from Mizraim, and 11 peoples descended from Canaan including Sidon); Shem's line names 26 (5 sons of Shem, 4 sons of Aram, Shelah, Eber, Peleg, Joktan, and 13 sons of Joktan). The total is 70, matching the number long associated with this chapter in Jewish tradition (the "seventy nations"); this lesson verified the count itself against the real text rather than citing that tradition's own number without checking it.

## Literary Design

**A genuinely distinctive genealogy, on one real, named scholarly judgment -- and genuinely disputed on another.** Wikipedia's own survey article on this chapter reports R. N. Whybray's judgment that the Table "appears to be quite unique: no comparable ancient texts exist," organizing people by a "broad network" of lateral relationships rather than the single father-to-son "chain" most genealogies trace. The same survey reports a different, more critical observation from Russell Gmirkin (2006): that despite this chapter's own repeated claim to group peoples "according to their clans, languages, lands, and nations" (10:20, 10:31), the "sons of Noah" are not, in fact, consistently organized by geography, language family, or ethnicity within that framework. David Ben-Gad HaCohen's own article for TheTorah.com develops the same observation independently, naming specific anomalies directly checkable against the text: Canaan is listed under Ham (10:6) despite Canaanite being a Semitic language, closer to Hebrew than to any language associated with Ham's other sons; the Hittites (10:15, as "Heth") are listed as Canaan's own son despite being Indo-European speakers from Asia Minor; and the Philistines (10:14) are listed as descending from Mizraim's son Casluh despite most historians associating Philistine origins with the Aegean, not Egypt. HaCohen's own proposed resolution is that the list reflects its ancient author's own geographic vantage point -- he argues specifically for a viewpoint based near Kadesh, in the Transjordan -- with genealogical "distance" standing in for geographic distance as the author perceived it, rather than a claim about linguistic or ethnic descent in the modern sense. This lesson reports Whybray's "unique" judgment and the Gmirkin/HaCohen critique together, without resolving the tension between them: both observations are about the same text, and a reader can hold "this is a genuinely distinctive kind of list" and "its own stated organizing principle does not cleanly match the groupings it actually contains" at the same time.

**The Nimrod digression as a structural break, independently checkable.** Every other descendant in this chapter receives, at most, a name and a father -- Mizraim's seven descendants (10:13-14) and nine of Canaan's eleven (10:16-18) get nothing more than a list of names. Nimrod alone (10:8-12) receives a title ("a mighty one on the earth"), a reputation ("a mighty hunter before the LORD," repeated in a popular saying the text itself quotes), a place of origin, a named sequence of cities he built or ruled, and a second territory he expanded into. This is a checkable difference in kind, not only in length, and multiple classic commentators read the break as deliberate: Barnes' Notes calls him "the first potentate of whom we have any record," and Gill's Exposition reports an old identification with "the same with Belus, the founder of Babel." William F. Albright is reported, at third hand (Albright, as cited by James Montgomery Boice, as quoted by David Guzik's own commentary -- none of the three independently read in Albright's own primary publication for this lesson), as calling the whole chapter a document that "stands absolutely alone in ancient literature, without a remote parallel," and the Nimrod digression is where that distinctiveness is most visible: a genealogy that, for four verses, becomes something closer to a short historical notice.

**A boundary-and-formula structure framing three unequal sections.** Japheth's line (10:2-5), Ham's line (10:6-20), and Shem's line (10:21-31) each close with a variant of the same grouping language, and each section grows longer than the last -- four verses for Japheth, fifteen for Ham, eleven for Shem, with the chapter's own final verse (10:32) functioning as a fourth, shorter close that covers all three sons together. This lesson reports the pattern as a checkable feature of how the text is built; what the lengthening sections and the varied formula are doing -- whether marking Ham's and Shem's own greater narrative importance to the rest of the book, or simply reflecting how much tradition or information the compiler had for each line -- is not settled by the pattern alone, and no source consulted for this lesson addresses that specific question.

## Positions

Three separate questions. Each is reported through its own named positions, from their own stated reasoning; none is adjudicated, and no position's statement here should be read as this lesson's own conclusion.

**1. What organizing principle actually governs this chapter's groupings -- real linguistic or ethnic descent, political alliance, or the compiler's own geographic vantage point?** This is the same tension Literary Design names above, stated here as a real, open question rather than a settled finding.

- **A genuinely distinctive attempt at a comprehensive ethnic and linguistic map.** On this reading, represented by Whybray's "quite unique" judgment and by the plain sense of the chapter's own three-times-repeated formula ("according to their clans, languages, lands, and nations"), the list's own stated organizing categories are close to its real, intended ones, even where a modern reader might group a people differently; the chapter's ambition -- a single genealogical account of every known nation -- is itself the point being reported.
- **A geographically- and politically-organized list whose stated categories do not fully match linguistic or ethnic reality.** Gmirkin (2006) and HaCohen both read the chapter's groupings as reflecting something other than strict ethnic or linguistic descent: political subordination, geographic proximity as the ancient author himself perceived it, or some combination -- with Canaan-under-Ham, the Hittites-under-Canaan, and the Philistines-under-Mizraim each read as evidence that "language family" cannot be the list's real, consistent organizing rule, whatever 10:5/10:20/10:31 themselves say about language.

Both readings can agree on every individual placement in the text; they disagree about what kind of claim the placements are making. This lesson does not decide between them.

**2. Who was Nimrod (10:8-12)?** Real, named historians have proposed several different identifications, and Wikipedia's own survey article states plainly that "historians have failed to match Nimrod with any historically attested figure" on a king list or other primary record -- a concession worth stating before the proposals themselves, since none of what follows should be read as settled.

- **Sargon of Akkad.** Yigal Levin (Bar-Ilan University), writing for TheTorah.com, argues this is the strongest match: "Nimrod's depiction is based on Sargon," reasoning that "both Nimrod and Sargon began their reigns in Sumer/Shinar, building Akkad and Babylon and continuing north to Assyria." A separate, longer academic treatment (Petrovich, *Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society* 56/2, 2013) argues the same identification at length by exegetical and archaeological means; that article was located but not independently read in full for this lesson (see Sources and Limits).
- **Naram-Sin, or a composite of Sargon and Naram-Sin.** Levin also proposes that "Nimrod" may be "the later composite Hebrew equivalent of the Sargonic dynasty's two most famous Kings of Kish: Sargon... and his grandson Naram-Sin," with the name itself possibly "corrupted in the biblical account from Naram to Nimrod" -- a different, more specific claim than the Sargon-alone reading above, from the same scholar.
- **Tukulti-Ninurta I, an Assyrian king.** Ronald Hendel is reported as suggesting "Nimrod's imperial ventures described in Genesis may be based on the conquests" of this later Assyrian monarch; Julian Jaynes is reported making a similar proposal.
- **The Assyrian god Ninurta, euhemerized into a human figure.** Hendel is also reported proposing that "the name Nimrod is probably a much later polemical distortion of the Semitic Assyrian god Ninurta" -- a different kind of proposal than the other three, since it reads "Nimrod" as a deliberately altered reference to a deity rather than to any historical king.
- **A legendary hero (Gilgamesh) or demigod, explicitly rejected by at least one named scholar.** Levin reports that "some scholars have suggested that Nimrod is based on a legendary god or demigod, such as the Sumerian gods Ninurta or Nergal or the Babylonian Marduk," and that "others have equated Nimrod with legendary Mesopotamian heroes such as Gilgamesh," but states that E. A. Speiser "correctly notes that despite the epic aspects of the biblical story, there is no textual evidence of Nimrod's being anything other than mortal" -- an argument against every reading on this bullet, made by a scholar Levin himself is reporting approvingly, not against his own preferred reading.

This lesson reports all five without ranking them, and states Wikipedia's own "historians have failed to match" concession as the honest frame around every one of them: real, careful, named scholars disagree, and no proposal here should be treated as the chapter's own settled historical referent.

**3. How does this chapter's own threefold reference to differing languages (10:5, 10:20, 10:31) relate, in time, to Genesis 11:1's statement that "the whole world had one language" before the events of Babel?** This is a real, checkable textual tension: this chapter states three times that Noah's sons' descendants were already divided "according to their... languages," while the very next chapter opens by stating there was, at that point, only one language on earth. Two different kinds of answer are in print.

- **A deliberate narrative anticipation, resolved by 10:25's own clause.** On this reading, the compiler lays out the genealogical and geographic result first (chapter 10) before narrating, in chapter 11, the event that produced it -- the same technique a family history might use in summarizing where a family's branches eventually settled before telling the story of how they got there. Several classic commentators tie 10:25's "the earth was divided" directly to this event: the Geneva Study Bible states "this division came by the diversity of language, as appears in Ge 11:9"; Keil and Delitzsch read the division as occurring "in consequence of the building of the tower of Babel"; Barnes' Notes argues the specific Hebrew verb behind "was divided" elsewhere refers to language being divided, and reads 10:25 the same way. On this reading, the tension is only apparent: chapter 10's own language references describe the end state chapter 11 goes on to explain, and 10:25 itself marks approximately when, within the genealogy's own generations, that explained event took place.
- **Evidence of two originally separate traditions, not fully harmonized by the text's final editor.** A different, more source-critical reading treats the same tension as a genuine seam: Wikipedia's own survey reports Ronald Hendel suggesting "a redactor may have combined two originally distinct tables" in assembling this chapter, which would make the language-before-Babel tension a byproduct of combining sources with different premises about when linguistic division occurred, rather than a single author's own deliberate anticipation.

Both readings agree on the raw textual fact (the tension is real and directly checkable by comparing 10:5/10:20/10:31 against 11:1); they disagree on whether it is a planned literary technique or a visible seam from the text's own compositional history. This lesson does not decide between them, and does not resolve what Genesis 11:1-9 itself says about the confusion of languages -- that is the next unit's own territory.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key: the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step, and a different passage element, situation, or response would be equally legitimate.

**Passage element.** Genesis 10:5, 10:20, 10:31. The chapter's own three-times-repeated formula records seventy named clans, languages, lands, and nations as the real, legitimate shape of human diversity descending from one family, without ranking any clan, language, or land above another anywhere in the formula itself.

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text names dozens of distinct peoples -- some of whom an Israelite audience would recognize as neighbors, rivals, or trading partners -- inside one shared genealogical frame, and the repeated closing formula treats "clans, languages, lands, and nations" as parallel, co-equal categories rather than a ranked scale. Inference: for an audience that could locate itself and its neighbors within this same list, encountering its own nation's origin recorded alongside Egypt's, Canaan's, and the Greeks' as equally descended from Noah's sons would have been a statement about shared human origin that the list's own structure makes without needing to argue for it. The Context section above supplies what the text itself records; nothing in it lets this lesson say more about what first hearers took from the list's own shape than that.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Recording real differences among people -- language, land, group of origin -- accurately and without collapsing them into sameness is compatible with, and does not by itself require, ranking those differences against one another; a list can be honest about diversity and silent about hierarchy at the same time, and that combination is a discipline a person can build on purpose.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: real, significant differences among people exist and are worth naming accurately; a context exists (a document, a roster, a conversation) where those differences get recorded or discussed; the temptation exists to let an accurate record of difference slide into an unstated ranking of one group over another. Not carried forward: this chapter's own genealogical form, its ancient geographic assumptions, and any claim that every modern categorization of people serves a purpose as legitimate as this chapter's own. The bridge holds only where these features match the modern case.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed; nothing in 10:1-32 is treated here as a promise that every modern system for recording group differences is wise or necessary -- that is a separate question this bridge does not answer.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. A construction project manager is building a staffing roster across several subcontracted crews from different regions and language backgrounds, tracking which crew speaks which language on-site for safety-briefing purposes and which crew is assigned to which part of the job.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: accurate categorization without ranking (not pretending there are no real differences to track, and not treating the roster's categories as a scale of worth). One faithful response is to keep the roster's language and crew-origin fields strictly functional -- answering "who needs the safety briefing translated, and into what" -- and to notice and correct, deliberately and promptly, any talk that drifts from "Crew A speaks Spanish, Crew B speaks Portuguese" into "Crew A works harder than Crew B," the same way this chapter names seventy real, different groups without its own formula ever ranking one above another.

**Cautions.** This example does not claim every workplace categorization of people by origin or language is as neutral as this chapter's own genealogical form, and it does not resolve whether tracking group membership for operational reasons is always wise -- a separate question with its own real risks. It should not be read as minimizing the real, documented history of exactly this kind of slide from "different" to "ranked": this curriculum's own Genesis 9:18-29 lesson already named, in detail, how this chapter's immediate predecessor was used to justify a racial hierarchy the text itself never states, and that history is the reason this caution is stated plainly rather than left implicit. The passage is not a workplace-management text; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing, and the learner may find it does not hold.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Positions (1) reports two real readings of this chapter's own organizing principle -- that it is a genuinely distinctive linguistic/ethnic map (Whybray), or that its stated categories (language, land, clan, nation) do not cleanly match the groupings it actually contains (Gmirkin, HaCohen). Having read the specific anomalies HaCohen names (Canaan under Ham, the Hittites under Canaan, the Philistines under Mizraim), does the chapter's own repeated formula still describe what it claims to describe, or does the formula claim more consistency than the list itself delivers?
2. Positions (2) reports five real, named, and mutually exclusive proposals for Nimrod's historical identity, alongside Wikipedia's own "historians have failed to match" concession. If no proposal can currently be confirmed, what is this chapter's own four-verse digression (10:8-12) actually doing in a genealogy that otherwise never stops to narrate anything about an individual -- and does the answer to that literary question depend at all on settling who Nimrod historically was?
3. Positions (3) reports two different explanations for why this chapter states differing languages already exist (10:5, 10:20, 10:31) while Genesis 11:1 states there was, at that point, only one. What would a reader need to find in the text of Genesis 10-11 itself -- not in either position's own larger framework -- to judge whether the tension is a deliberate narrative anticipation or a visible seam between two combined sources?
4. This chapter places Peleg and Joktan as Eber's only two named sons (10:25), then names thirteen of Joktan's own sons (10:26-29) before the chapter ends -- even though Genesis 11:10-26's later genealogy will pick up Eber's line again through Peleg specifically, never returning to Joktan at all. Why might a chapter whose narrative momentum clearly favors Peleg's branch still spend four verses naming Joktan's thirteen sons in full?

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first; the discipline of this prompt is that you do the forming, not that you pick from a list. If you are stuck, here is one worked candidate, stated at full strength: naming five different, mutually incompatible historical proposals for Nimrod (Positions 2) without choosing one is not careful scholarship, it is evasion dressed up as balance -- a chapter that devotes four whole verses to one specific individual is clearly asking to be read as history, and a lesson that reports "no one knows" five different ways has taught a learner nothing they could not have gotten from a single sentence. Before you answer it, weigh what Positions (2) actually reports: that real, named, currently-working scholars (Levin, Hendel) disagree with each other, and that Wikipedia's own survey states plainly that no identification has been confirmed against any primary historical record -- ask whether picking one proposal to present as settled would be more honest than reporting the real state of the question, or less. A second worked candidate, running the other direction: this lesson's own insistence that Cush, Mizraim, and Put carry no stated curse (Context, above) risks reading like special pleading, since it is exactly the kind of textual argument a reader could use to defend almost any group the text does not explicitly curse -- ask what makes this argument different from that general pattern, and check your answer against what Genesis 9:25-27 actually names (only Canaan, three times) rather than against what the argument needs to be true.

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Genesis 10:6-20 does not state, anywhere in its own wording, any difference in standing, blessing, or curse among Ham's four sons (Cush, Mizraim, Put, and Canaan). The different amount of narrative space each line receives (Context, above) is a checkable fact about the text's length, not a stated claim about the four brothers' relative worth. (b) Genesis 10:8-12 does not name, confirm, or require any specific historical identification for Nimrod. The text states only his descent (Cush's son), his reputation ("a mighty one," "a mighty hunter"), and a list of places; every proposal in Positions (2) -- Sargon, Naram-Sin, Tukulti-Ninurta, Ninurta, Gilgamesh -- is a later reader's or scholar's own argument from outside this specific passage, not a claim the text makes about itself.
