*Before you begin.* Genesis 34 tells of sexual violence against a young woman, and of a town's men killed and its women and children taken. If you are in immediate danger, call 911 or your local emergency number. In the United States, RAINN's National Sexual Assault Hotline is free, confidential and open 24/7: call 800-656-HOPE (4673), text "HOPE" to 64673, or chat at RAINN.org/hotline. Outside the United States, contact local sexual-assault services or your local emergency number.

Genesis 34 is the story of Dinah, Jacob's only named daughter, and it is told almost entirely by other people. She "went out to visit the daughters of the land" (34:1); Shechem son of Hamor, "the prince of the region," "took her and lay with her by force" (34:2). Then the men talk: Shechem, his father Hamor, Jacob's sons, and finally Jacob and two of his sons. The brothers answer "deceitfully" (34:13), require every male of the town to be circumcised, and on the third day Simeon and Levi kill them all (34:25); the other sons plunder the city (34:27-29). The chapter ends with a question no one answers: "Should he have treated our sister like a prostitute?" (34:31). Dinah does not speak once. This lesson reads the chapter slowly, says plainly what it records and what it does not, names one long tradition of reading as a misuse (blaming Dinah for going out), and reports how named Jewish and Christian readers judge the rest. Her birth (30:21) was covered in this curriculum's Genesis 30:1-24 lesson. Every BSB quotation was checked by script against this app's shipped corpus (see Sources and Limits).

## Context

**Where this unit sits.** Stage 5 of this app's eleven-stage lens (Genesis 12 through Malachi; machine-readable `chapters: ["1.12"]`). Jacob has made peace with Esau (Genesis 33) and has bought land outside "the city of Shechem" from "the sons of Hamor, Shechem's father" (33:18-19), where he built an altar (33:20). After this chapter God sends Jacob to Bethel, and "a terror from God fell over the surrounding cities, so that they did not pursue Jacob's sons" (35:5).

**Who speaks.** A script listing of every verse in Genesis 34 that contains quoted speech gives this order: Shechem to his father (34:4); Hamor, who had come "to speak with Jacob" (34:6), to the family (34:8-10); Shechem to "Dinah's father and brothers" (34:11-12); Jacob's sons (34:14-17); Hamor and Shechem to their townsmen (34:21-23); Jacob to Simeon and Levi (34:30); Simeon and Levi (34:31). Dinah is not among them.

**Key words, read as observations.** Every count below was made by this lesson's own script over the BSB, case-insensitively and on word boundaries.

- **Dinah.** The name occurs in nine BSB verses, all in Genesis: 30:21 (her birth), seven verses of this chapter (34:1, 3, 5, 11, 13, 25, 26) and 46:15 (the list of Jacob's family). None of the nine contains words spoken by her; she speaks nowhere in the BSB. Rachel Adelman (Jewish Women's Archive): Dinah "is acted upon and given no voice."
- **"Defiled."** The verb occurs three times in this chapter (34:5, 13, 27), 34:5 and 34:13 say Shechem "had defiled" her, and 34:27 says "their sister had been defiled." It is the narrator's word, not Dinah's.
- **"Outrage in Israel" (34:7).** The noun is *nebalah* (5039). In the BSB "outrage" occurs in five verses: Genesis 34:7, Deuteronomy 22:21, Joshua 7:15, Judges 19:23 and Jeremiah 29:23.
- **No mention of God.** "God" and "LORD" occur nowhere in the BSB of Genesis 34. Amy-Jill Levine notes this ("Not even a mention of God in the chapter"), adds that God is named at the end of chapter 33 and the start of chapter 35, and concludes, "Thus, silence does not mean absence."
- **Circumcision.** Words built on "circumcis-" (including "uncircumcised") occur in five verses (34:14, 15, 17, 22, 24). Genesis 17 introduced circumcision as the sign of God's covenant with Abraham (see this curriculum's Genesis 17 lesson).

**After 34:26.** Her brothers "took Dinah out of Shechem's house" (34:26). The narrative does not say what happened to her afterward; her name next appears in the list of those who went down to Egypt (46:15).

## Literary Design

**The verbs of 34:2.** In the Hebrew, as Bible Hub's interlinear sets it out, 34:2 has three verbs in a row after "saw her": *wayyiqqach otah* ("and he took her"), *wayyishkav otah* ("and lay with her"), *way'anneha* ("and violated her," Bible Hub's gloss of *'innah*, Strong's 6031, Piel). The BSB renders the last two together as "lay with her by force." Translations differ on the third verb, as Bible Hub's parallel page shows: NIV, the current NASB and CSB "raped her" (the NASB 1995 has "lay with her by force"); NRSV, like the BSB, "lay with her by force"; NET "forced himself on her, and sexually assaulted her"; ESV "humiliated her"; KJV "defiled her"; JPS 1917 and Brenton's Septuagint "humbled her." What the word means is Position 1. Whatever is decided there, the text itself records no word of consent from Dinah, no word from her at all, and, in 34:26, that she was in Shechem's house until her brothers took her out.

**Shechem's speech and Dinah's silence.** Verse 3 turns to Shechem's feelings: "his soul was drawn to Dinah ... He loved the young girl and spoke to her tenderly." Readers weigh that verse very differently. Adelman: "the reader's sympathies turn to Shechem even though he may have forced himself upon Dinah." Calvin reads it as attachment that does not excuse: "although he embraced Dinah with real and sincere attachment, yet, in this want of self-government, he grievously sinned." Ramban reads "spoke tenderly" as an effort to win a girl who "did not consent to him and she steadily protested and cried." Levine turns the verse into questions about Dinah, ending: "Will there have to be a third, and a fourth chance, as he continues to abuse, and then, after the anger subsides, to speak tenderly toward her?" The text reports what Shechem felt and said. It reports nothing Dinah felt or said.

**Two fathers.** Hamor speaks for his son (34:8-10). Jacob, when he hears, "remained silent about it until they returned" (34:5), and in the negotiation his sons answer, not he (34:13). Genesis Rabbah 80:6 reads his silence by Proverbs 11:12, in its translator's rendering "a man of understanding will be silent" (BSB "remains silent"); Levine asks, "Does Jacob act as a father should?" Jacob's only speech in the chapter is about his own danger (34:30); that is Position 3.

**The deal, told twice.** Jacob's sons offer: "we will give you our daughters and take your daughters for ourselves" (34:16). When Hamor and Shechem retell it at the city gate, the order is reversed, "Let us take their daughters in marriage and give our daughters to them" (34:21), and they add a reason the sons never gave: "Will not their livestock, their possessions, and all their animals become ours?" (34:23). Rashi noticed the reversal: the townsmen were told the version that would "induce them to consent to be circumcised." Jacob's sons answer "deceitfully" (34:13); Hamor and Shechem give their townsmen a different version (34:21-23). Dinah is the subject of the negotiation, not a party to it.

**The last word.** The chapter closes on the brothers' question, "Should he have treated our sister like a prostitute?" (34:31), with no reply. Adelman: "the brothers are given the last word." The narrative does not say whether that question answers Jacob's complaint in 34:30. Jacob's words in 49:5-7 are the next time Genesis comes back to the deed (see the connections).

## Positions

Four questions follow. The second is a reading this lesson names as a misuse; the others are reported through named readers in their own words and are not decided here.

**1. What does 34:2 say happened?** The dispute is about one Hebrew verb, *'innah*, and what it meant to the people who first told the story. Both sides read the same three verbs.

- *The verb describes forced sex.* Ramban, against two earlier readers, writes that "there is no need for this for all forced sexual connection is called 'affliction'" and cites two other places where the same verb describes forced sex, the captive woman of Deuteronomy 21:14 and the concubine of Judges 20:5. He concludes: "Scripture thus tells — in Dinah's praise — that she was forced, and she did not consent to the prince of the country." Calvin reaches the same view from 34:3: "whence it follows, that when she was unwilling and resisted, he used violence towards her." The Vulgate already rendered the verb "ravishing the virgin by force," as Alison Joseph (TheTorah.com) reports. Amy-Jill Levine calls it rape without qualification: "Rape leads to a slaughter." Kadari's survey of the midrash speaks throughout of "the rape of Dinah." Joseph, though she renders the verb "debase," ends: "in biblical society there was no social structure or vocabulary to call 'rape' what it was; Dinah may have been Shechem's victim, but she was also a victim of her times," and she commends Nahmanides as an example "of how we can read texts of sexual violence today."
- *The verb describes debasement of status, and the text does not address consent.* Shawna Dolansky (TheTorah.com, 2015) argues that *'innah* is used where no sex is involved (Deuteronomy 8:2; the Day of Atonement phrase she renders "afflict yourselves," Leviticus 16:31, BSB "humble yourselves") and that "the biblical expression for rape is ויחזק וישכב, 'to overpower and lie with,' not ʿinnah," a pairing found at Deuteronomy 22:25 and 2 Samuel 13:14 but not here. In her reading the word "denotes a downward movement in a social sense," and Dinah's status was lowered "whether or not she consented (the narrator never tells us)." Her conclusion: "this story is not about the rape of Dinah at all." Joseph agrees that the verb is "more properly rendered with 'debase'," while adding that "later interpreters of the story did understand the term 'innâ as something hurtful to the woman." She grants that "Nahmanides' understanding of the story is now the prevalent reading," and suggests that "the rape focus may be a result of later readers looking for a crime that better fits the punishment." Dolansky lists earlier studies that questioned the traditional reading, among them Lyn Bechtel's "What If Dinah is not Raped?" (*JSOT* 62, 1994) and Ellen van Wolde's "Does 'Innâ Denote Rape?" (*VT* 52, 2002); those studies were not read for this lesson and are reported only as her note cites them.
- *Seduction, abduction, or no single answer.* Keil and Delitzsch on 34:1 say Shechem "took her with him and seduced her." Adelman sums up the range: Dinah "is raped, seduced, and/or abducted by Shechem"; Joseph lists readings that "run the gamut from rape, to statutory rape, to consensual encounter, to teenage love affair." Adelman writes that "not all scholars agree that she was raped" and that "it is not clear that Shechem forced himself upon Dinah," while noting that the family's words ("defiled," "outrage") show what they presumed.

*A law the dispute leans on.* Several of these readers cite Deuteronomy 22:23-29. That law presumes that a betrothed woman taken in a town consented if no one heard her cry out, and puts her to death with the man (22:23-24), while one taken in the open country is presumed forced (22:25-27); a man who seizes an unbetrothed virgin must pay her father and marry her (22:28-29). Adelman reports it and adds: "The woman's will or testimony was simply not taken into account." It is cited here only as evidence of how an ancient law framed such a case. It is not a test to apply to any survivor today.

*What both sides grant.* Dolansky, who argues against the rape reading, writes: "To be fair, although the text never says that Dinah was forced into having sex, it never says that she engaged in it willingly either. Rather, the narrator is unconcerned with the question of Dinah's consent; Dinah herself does not speak a single word in the story." Joseph's conclusion, quoted above, names Dinah as "Shechem's victim." Adelman, reporting both readings: "if she was raped, her own pain and anguish must be heard over the violent clamor in defense of male honor."

*Objections each reading faces.* (1) The forced-sex reading: Dolansky answers that the Bible's own idiom for overpowering is absent from 34:2, and that the reading projects "notions of individualism and bodily autonomy" onto an ancient text. (2) The debasement reading: Ramban's examples (Deuteronomy 21:14; Judges 20:5) are uses of the same verb for sex without consent, and Calvin infers force from Shechem's effort to win her over afterward (34:3). (3) The seduction reading: Ramban reads the same verse 3 as Shechem answering a girl who "steadily protested and cried," and Keil and Delitzsch themselves put Dinah "between 13 and 15," beside Joseph's "statutory rape." This lesson does not decide the meaning of the word. It does not soften what the chapter records: Dinah was taken, she was in Shechem's house until her brothers took her out (34:26), and the text gives no sign that she agreed to any of it.

**2. A misuse: blaming Dinah for "going out" (34:1).** The verse says Dinah "went out to visit the daughters of the land." It gives her purpose and states no fault. In the chapter the words of wrong, "defiled" (34:5, 13, 27) and "an outrage in Israel" (34:7), name what Shechem did. A long line of readers has nonetheless found Dinah's going out to be the cause.

- *Who.* Genesis Rabbah 80:1 sets 34:1 beside the proverb of Ezekiel 16:44 about mothers and daughters and connects Dinah's going out with Leah's (30:16); 80:2, 80:3 and 80:12 each close by naming her going out as the cause, and 80:5 compares her going out to uncovering meat before a bird. Rashi on 34:1 repeats the link with Leah and the proverb. Tamar Kadari (Jewish Women's Archive) reports further midrashim (Tanhuma; Avot de-Rabbi Nathan) that fault her for wishing to be seen. Among Christian commentators, Calvin writes that "it is not to be doubted that Moses in part casts the blame of the offense upon Dinah herself"; Matthew Henry, Benson, Poole, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown and Ellicott, on 34:1, likewise fault her curiosity, her going without her father's leave, or her wish to be seen, and draw lessons for keeping daughters at home.
- *How.* These readers move from what 34:1 says (she went to visit) to a cause the verse does not state (that her going made her responsible), and then to a rule for women generally.
- *What answers it.* Modern scholars name this reading for what it is. Kadari calls the blaming dicta a sign of "the anxiety of the Rabbis at the thought of their wives and daughters leaving the protected home for the marketplace and the streets of the city." Alison Joseph, after quoting Genesis Rabbah and Midrash Yelamdenu: "These midrashim, which are cited by no less an authority than Rashi, are particularly cruel, blaming Dinah for what happens to her." Joseph leaves open whether the verse itself carries a criticism: it "may be understood as equivalent to the contemporary accusations against victims of sexual assault," but "Whether this is the meaning of the verse or not, the Rabbis indeed understood the verse as a criticism of Dinah." Amy-Jill Levine, writing in the Vatican newspaper's women's supplement: "some early commentators suggested that Dinah is to blame for the rape -- had she not gone out to visit the women, they reason, none of this would have happened. The Bible does not blame the victim, and neither should we." Within the medieval tradition, Ramban's reading of 34:2 holds that Scripture shows "in Dinah's praise" that she did not consent, though on 34:12 he says Scripture omits her beauty because it was "to her a stumbling-block of iniquity" and "speaks only in praise of the righteous women but not of this one."

*Other midrashim that move the cause.* Genesis Rabbah 80:4, and 79:8 as Kadari reports it, shift the cause to Jacob: his boast at 30:33, his keeping Dinah from Esau, or the name he gave his altar (33:20). Kadari also reports a midrash in which Shechem lured Dinah out with drummers and "kidnapped her." The first group still read the event as a punishment, now of her father, and are reported here as part of the history of interpretation, not as an answer to the misuse.

What can be checked: no verse in Genesis 34, and neither of the two other verses that name her (30:21; 46:15), says Dinah did wrong. A person harmed is not responsible for the harm because of where she went.

**3. Simeon and Levi: deceit and slaughter (34:13-31).** The text reports that the brothers answered "deceitfully" (34:13), that Simeon and Levi "slaughtered every male" (34:25), that the other sons took "women and children" (34:29), and that Jacob spoke of the danger to "I and my household" (34:30). It gives no narrator's verdict. Readers range from defense to condemnation.

- *Justified, or at least not to be called deceit.* Genesis Rabbah 80:8 asks of "with guile," "what do you think, that there is deceit here?" and answers with the verse's own reason, "As he had defiled Dina their sister"; Rashi on 34:13 follows it: "there was no deception (מרמה) in this because he had defiled Dinah their sister." In Genesis Rabbah 80:12 the Rabbis give Jacob the line "The barrel was clear, and you rendered it murky," and Rabbi Yehuda bar Simon has the brothers answer, "The barrel was murky, and we rendered it clear." Maimonides gives a legal ground: in his account non-Jews are bound to set up courts, and "For this reason, all the inhabitants of Shechem were obligated to die. Shechem kidnapped. They observed and were aware of his deeds, but did not judge him" (*Mishneh Torah*, Kings and Wars 9:14, a ruling within Jewish law on the obligations of non-Jews). Outside the 66 books, Judith (9:2, in the King James Apocrypha) prays to the "Lord God of my father Simeon, to whom thou gavest a sword to take vengeance of the strangers, who loosened the girdle of a maid to defile her."
- *Right anger, wrong deeds.* Keil and Delitzsch: "Their indignation was justifiable enough" (they support it by citing the nineteenth-century punishment of "seduction" by death among Arabs; this lesson does not take up that comparison, and Genesis 34 says nothing about later peoples), but "the deception they practised, the abuse of the covenant sign of circumcision as a means of gratifying their revenge, and the extension of that revenge to the whole town, together with the plundering of the slain, were crimes deserving of the strongest reprobation." Ellicott calls Jacob's rebuke "singularly weak, and the retort of his sons just," and goes on: "but in avenging this wrong they had committed a crime of a deeper dye." Barnes says the brothers' answer "justifies the retribution which has fallen on the Shekemites," then adds, "But it does not justify the executioners for taking the law into their own hands, or proceeding by fraud and indiscriminate slaughter." Benson, on 34:31: "No, he should not; but, if he do, must they be their own avengers?"
- *Condemned.* Ramban rejects Maimonides' argument: "if so, our father Jacob should have been the first to obtain the merit of causing their death," and if the deed was right, why did Jacob "curse their wrath a long time after that"? Ramban himself holds that the Shechemites had broken the laws binding on all peoples, but "it was not the responsibility of Jacob and his sons to bring them to justice," and he reads 49:5-7 as Jacob cursing them "for they had done violence to the men of the city whom they had told in his presence ... we will become one people." Calvin, on 34:25: "there is no reader who does not readily perceive how dreadful and execrable was this crime"; the brothers "form a design against friends and hospitable persons, in a time of peace." Calvin then turns the deed against the Jews of his own day, telling them not to be proud of their descent from Levi; Genesis 34 says nothing about later Jews, and this lesson does not take the remark up. Calvin also holds that God used the deed against the city: "The sons of Jacob acted indeed wickedly," but in this way Shechem's act (Calvin's word is "fornication") "was ... divinely condemned." Matthew Henry: "Simeon and Levi were most unrighteous." Adelman: Shechem's apparent attachment "makes the brothers' deceit and wholesale slaughter all the more heinous." Levine: "Destruction, murder, enslavement, and rape do not resolve the original crime; they rather escalate the violence."
- *Dinah lost from view.* Joseph reads the revenge as concerned with the family's honor and a polemic against marriage with Canaanites: "If anything, this demonstrates even less concern for Dinah's welfare than the laws in Exodus and Deuteronomy." Levine asks of Dinah, "Would she rejoice in Shechem's murder? ... Or would she be revolted by the violence?"

*Objections each reading faces.* (1) The defense: Ramban's question, why Jacob would curse a righteous deed (34:30; 49:5-7); and Barnes's objection to "fraud and indiscriminate slaughter." (2) The middle reading: it divides what the text joins, since 34:13 gives the defilement as the reason for the deceit itself (Genesis Rabbah 80:8; Rashi). (3) The condemnation: Genesis Rabbah 80:10 and Rashi on 34:25 call them "Dinah's brothers" because they risked their lives for her, and Ellicott, who condemns the deed, still calls the brothers' retort "just." (4) The reading that Dinah is lost from view: Ramban on 34:1 says the two brothers "were envious for her sake and avenged her cause." This lesson does not decide between them.

**4. Circumcision used as a weapon (34:13-24).** The brothers say that giving their sister "to an uncircumcised man would be a disgrace to us" (34:14) and require circumcision of "every one of your males" (34:15). The killing takes place "while they were still in pain" (34:25).

- *A covenant sign profaned.* Calvin: "they falsely use the sacred name of God as a pretext," and "in a sense, trample upon the sacred covenant of God, of which alone they make their boast." Barnes: "The employment of circumcision, too, which was the sign of the covenant of grace, as a means of deception, was a heinous aggravation of their offence." Keil and Delitzsch name "the abuse of the covenant sign of circumcision" among the brothers' crimes. Levine: "Circumcision is a sign of the covenant between God and the people Israel. The brothers debase this sign."
- *The townsmen's motive.* Several of the same readers also fault the Shechemites. Calvin says that to change religion "so carelessly betrays, on their part, a gross contempt of God," though he adds that Simeon and Levi "were not, on that account, excusable." Matthew Henry: "The Shechemites submitted to the sacred rite, only to serve a turn, to please their prince, and to enrich themselves, and it was just with God to bring punishment upon them"; his next sentences turn on the brothers (Position 3). Hamor's own words in 34:23 give a material motive.
- *A rite without covenant.* Ramban, reporting the brothers' view: "The covenant represented by the circumcision of the inhabitants of Shechem had no validity in the eyes of Jacob's sons for it was done to curry favor with their master." Calvin agrees from another side: the brothers "wickedly sever the sign from the truth which it represents; as if any one, by laying aside his uncircumcision, might suddenly pass over into the Church of God."

*Objections each reading faces.* (1) The profanation reading: Ramban's report suggests the brothers did not regard the Shechemites' rite as the covenant at all. (2) The townsmen's-motive reading: Calvin himself first grants that their consent "may be ascribed to modesty and humanity" before rejecting that, and says it does not excuse the killers. (3) The rite-without-covenant reading: Barnes and Keil and Delitzsch still call what was misused "the sign of the covenant of grace" and "the covenant sign." This lesson does not decide between them.

*If this chapter touches your own life.* Positions 1-4 report how readers have interpreted an ancient story, and they are not a judgment on anyone living. If someone is using this chapter, or any text, to tell you that what was done to you was your fault, that is not a question for a study note. The RAINN hotline in the note at the top (800-656-HOPE, or text "HOPE" to 64673) and, in immediate danger, 911 are there for that. A reader working any of this through in their own life needs a trained advocate or counselor, or a pastor they trust, not a study note.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge. It models the shape; it is not an answer key. It does not build from 34:2 or from the killing, and it does not ask anyone to imagine harm.

**Passage element.** Genesis 34 (BSB), read for who speaks. Shechem, Hamor, Jacob's sons, Jacob, Simeon and Levi all speak; Dinah, whose name the chapter uses seven times, does not.

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the chapter's words of wrong ("defiled," "an outrage in Israel") are tied to the family's response (34:5, 7, 13, 27), and the closing question is about "our sister" (34:31). Inference: a first audience may have heard the story chiefly as a matter of the household's honor; Joseph and Dolansky read it that way. Ramban and Levine read it with Dinah at the center. The narrator states no moral.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When a person who was harmed is not heard in an account, a reader can notice the silence, say plainly when any voice given to her is the reader's own (as Levine does), and never fill the silence with blame.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: an account of harm in which the person harmed is discussed but not heard. Not carried forward: the violence itself, the revenge, any claim about what Dinah thought, and any judgment in Positions 1-4.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a reading practice applied by analogy; not a command. Promise scope: none. Nothing here promises that careful reading will repair any harm.

**Modern domain and situation.** A study group. A group is reading Genesis 34 aloud, and someone remarks of verse 1 that Dinah brought it on herself by going out.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: attention to the text. One faithful response: point the group back to what 34:1 says (she went to visit the women of the land) and to where the chapter's words of wrong fall (34:5, 7, 13); say plainly that no verse faults her and that being harmed is not the fault of where someone went (Position 2); and continue without asking anyone in the room to share personal experience.

**Cautions.** This bridge is about reading a text together. It is not counsel for anyone affected by sexual violence. If someone discloses harm, the right next step is to listen and to make sure they know of RAINN (above) or, in danger, 911, not to discuss it as a study question.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them.

1. Dinah's name appears seven times in Genesis 34 and she never speaks. What does a reader learn from listing who does speak, and in what order?
2. Ramban and Dolansky read the same verb in 34:2 and reach different conclusions about what it describes. What evidence in the chapter does each reading depend on?
3. Jacob's complaint in 34:30 concerns his own danger; his words in 49:5-7 curse his sons' anger. What does each passage let a reader say about Jacob's view, and what does neither say?
4. Calvin calls the use of circumcision a trampling of the covenant; Ramban says the brothers did not regard the Shechemites' rite as covenant at all. Which details in 34:13-24 does each reading depend on?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 34:1-31 in about eight steps, saying what happens, not what it means. (b) List everyone who speaks in the chapter, in order. (c) State in one sentence what the text records about Dinah's consent and voice.
2. **Five-minute outline.** (0:00-0:30) the crisis note, read as written; (0:30-1:30) the chapter in order and who speaks; (1:30-2:30) Position 1 on the verb of 34:2, with what both sides grant; (2:30-3:30) Position 2, the misuse and its answers; (3:30-4:30) Positions 3 and 4; (4:30-5:00) the connections.
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: the reading of *'innah* as debasement makes light of what happened to Dinah. Weigh it against Dolansky's own sentence that the text "never says that she engaged in it willingly either," against what she argues the word measured, and against Ramban's uses of the same verb for forced sex (Deuteronomy 21:14; Judges 20:5). A second candidate, running the other way: calling Simeon and Levi's deed simply "condemned" ignores that the chapter ends on their question. Weigh it against 49:5-7.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and give the textual basis. (a) It does not record anything Dinah said, thought or wanted. (b) It does not say she bore any fault; no verse says so. (c) It does not give a narrator's verdict on Simeon and Levi; Jacob's words in 34:30 and 49:5-7 are a character's. (d) It does not say what became of Dinah after 34:26; the midrashim Kadari reports (that she married Simeon, or Job) are later traditions. (e) It does not say whether God approved the deed; 35:5 reports "a terror from God" on the surrounding cities without comment on Simeon and Levi.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** The connections attached to this lesson:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genesis 49:5-7 | explicit_reference | strong | Jacob names "Simeon and Levi," "their swords" and their killing "in their anger"; "Simeon and Levi" occurs only in 34:25, 34:30 and 49:5; Shechem is not named, so strong rather than explicit |
| Genesis 27:35 | motif | plausible | "Deceitfully" in Genesis only here and at 34:13, the same Hebrew *bemirmah*; Keil and Delitzsch link the sons' craft to Jacob's, but no source read cites 27:35 here |
| 2 Samuel 13:12-14 | motif | strong | Tamar and Amnon: the same verb *'innah*, the noun *nebalah*, and the clause "such ... is not done" with "in Israel"; Adelman, Dolansky, Joseph and Levine pair the stories; neither names the other |

Choose one row and defend it, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` for a candidate. No connection to the New Testament is authored, because none was found that the evidence supports: no New Testament verse quotes Genesis 34 or names Dinah or Simeon and Levi's deed; Acts 7:16 names "the sons of Hamor at Shechem," but in Stephen's summary of a burial-plot purchase (compare 33:19), not this chapter's events; and no source read for this lesson proposes one (Levine's mention of Jesus' words about visiting prisoners is a pastoral aside about Shechem, not a textual link). Two candidates were considered and not authored. (a) **John 4:5**, where Jesus comes to Sychar, "near the plot of ground that Jacob had given to his son Joseph," recalls Jacob's purchase in 33:19 and 48:22 (which Genesis Rabbah 80:10 ties to this chapter), not the events of 34. (b) **Judith 9:2** praises Simeon's sword, but Judith is outside this app's 66-book versification, so it is reported in Position 3 in prose only. A thread connection is not required of every lesson, and this one has none.

## Sources and Limits

- **BSB text and counts.** Every BSB quotation in this lesson and its three connection files was printed by a Node script from `web/public/bible/BSB/*.json` before drafting and checked afterward by a second script asserting that each quoted fragment is a literal substring of the verse cited. Every count in the lesson and connection files was made by script, case-insensitively and on word boundaries, and the output is kept with the lesson's working files.
- **Hebrew and translations.** Word-level Hebrew comes from Bible Hub's interlinear and lexicon pages (6031, 4820), and renderings of 34:2 from its parallel page. No critical edition or printed lexicon was consulted.
- **Modern scholars.** Rachel Adelman and Tamar Kadari (Jewish Women's Archive), Shawna Dolansky and Alison L. Joseph (TheTorah.com) and Amy-Jill Levine (*L'Osservatore Romano*, 2024) were read directly in pages fetched by raw HTTP request and saved. No Tikva Frymer-Kensky text on Dinah was read; the Jewish Women's Archive article used is Adelman's. Lyn Bechtel, Ellen van Wolde, Meyer Gruber and N. Wyatt (cited by Dolansky as questioning the rape reading) and Caroline Blyth (2010) and Susanne Scholz (cited by Dolansky as reading it as rape) were not read; they are reported only as cited. Neusner, Midrash Yelamdenu and the Vulgate are reported only as Joseph quotes them.
- **Jewish sources.** Rashi (Rosenbaum and Silbermann), Ramban (Chavel), Ibn Ezra (Strickman and Silver), Genesis Rabbah 80 (The Sefaria Midrash Rabbah, 2022) and Maimonides' *Mishneh Torah*, Kings and Wars 9 (Touger) were read through Sefaria's API. Maimonides' ruling is a legal text addressed to Jewish law about the obligations of non-Jews; it is reported only as a reading of this chapter. Rashi's and Genesis Rabbah 80:5's gloss on the two verbs of 34:2 as two different acts, and Ibn Ezra's reading of the verb as normal intercourse with a virgin (which Joseph says "has the effect of making the word 'innâ disappear"), are not discussed here; Joseph analyzes both.
- **Christian commentaries.** Calvin (his editor's notes are not used), Matthew Henry (Concise), Barnes, Benson, Poole, Gill, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, Ellicott, the Cambridge Bible, the Pulpit Commentary and Keil and Delitzsch were read on Bible Hub pages fetched raw and searched for each quoted string. Calvin's comment on 34:25 includes a remark aimed at Jews of his own day that this lesson does not quote or take up; Genesis 34 says nothing about later Jews. Hengstenberg, Kurtz, Kalisch and Wordsworth are reported only as the Pulpit Commentary cites them.
- **Judith.** Judith 9:2 was read in the King James Apocrypha on kingjamesbibleonline.org. Jubilees and other Second Temple retellings of Genesis 34 were not read.
- **Not read.** No Eastern Orthodox source, no Catholic commentary on this chapter (Levine's article appeared in a Vatican publication but is her own), and no modern critical commentary on Genesis (Westermann, Wenham, Sarna, Alter) was read. That is a gap in sourcing, not a claim about those readers.
- **"Honor" violence.** One source read, Keil and Delitzsch on 34:31, supports the brothers' indignation by citing nineteenth-century Arab punishment of "seduction" by death; Position 3 discloses this and does not take it up. No source read documents Genesis 34 being cited to justify present-day violence in the name of family honor.
- **Safety resource.** RAINN's site blocks automated requests and this lesson used no browser. RAINN's contact details are copied from this curriculum's registry entry (source-rainn-national-sexual-assault-hotline), where they were read on rainn.org on 2026-10-08, and are worded as in the curriculum's Genesis 29 lesson. 911 is the US emergency number. These are US services; readers elsewhere should use local services.
- **Scope.** Jacob's purchase at Shechem (33:18-20), the journey to Bethel (Genesis 35) and Jacob's last words (Genesis 49) belong to their own units. Dinah's birth was covered in this curriculum's Genesis 30:1-24 lesson.
