Matthew 19:1-15 holds two scenes. In the first, Pharisees test Jesus with a question about divorce: "Is it lawful for a man to divorce his wife for any reason?" (19:3, BSB). Jesus answers from Genesis, the Pharisees answer from Moses, and Jesus gives a saying with an exception: "whoever divorces his wife, except for sexual immorality, and marries another woman, commits adultery" (19:9). His disciples object, and He speaks of those who "live like eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven" (19:12). In the second scene the disciples turn children away, and Jesus says, "Let the little children come to Me, and do not hinder them!" (19:14). This lesson checks the two Genesis quotations, the law the Pharisees cite, the Greek of the exception and the Jewish debate behind the question. It then reports three disputes in each tradition's own published words without choosing among them. Lesson 11 reported the earlier divorce saying (5:31-32); this lesson cross-refers to it rather than repeating it. Every BSB quotation below was checked by script against this app's own Bible files.

*A note before starting.* Teaching on the permanence of marriage has been pressed on people who are being hurt at home. The U.S. Catholic bishops write that "Some abused women believe that church teaching on the permanence of marriage requires them to stay in an abusive relationship," and that "no person is expected to stay in an abusive marriage." If someone is hurting you, a study note is not the help you need. In the United States, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is free and confidential: call 1-800-799-7233, text START to 88788, or visit thehotline.org. If you are in immediate danger, call 911 or your local emergency number. A pastor or counselor you trust, ideally someone outside any group that is pressing you, can also help. Positions below says more.

## Context

**What happens, in order (19:1-15).** "When Jesus had finished saying these things, He left Galilee and went into the region of Judea beyond the Jordan" (19:1). In Matthew, "Jesus had finished" occurs in five verses only (7:28; 11:1; 13:53; 19:1; 26:1, by script), and each follows a block of Jesus' teaching; here it follows chapter 18 (lessons 38-39). Pharisees ask their question (19:3); Jesus answers from Genesis (19:4-6), they cite Moses (19:7), and He answers (19:8-9). The disciples object (19:10), and He answers them (19:11-12). Then children are brought to Him, the disciples turn them away, and Jesus overrules them (19:13-15).

**The question, and a debate it may echo (19:3).** The Mishnah, the early code of rabbinic law, records a dispute between the schools of Shammai and Hillel over Deuteronomy 24:1. In the William Davidson translation on Sefaria (Gittin 9:10), "Beit Shammai say: A man may not divorce his wife unless he finds out about her having engaged in a matter of forbidden sexual intercourse [devar erva]." Beit Hillel say "He may divorce her even due to a minor issue, e.g., because she burned or over-salted his dish." Rabbi Akiva says "He may divorce her even if he found another woman who is better looking than her." Each side argues from the verse's wording, "some unseemly matter [ervat davar]." The Talmud's discussion (Gittin 90a) has the two schools argue over the two words, and has Beit Hillel conclude that "even a wife who is divorced due to adultery is permitted to remarry." On the next page (90b), Rabbi Elazar says of "anyone who divorces his first wife, even the altar sheds tears over him," citing Malachi 2:13-14. The bracketed glosses are the translator's.

Whether Matthew 19:3 is asking Jesus to take a side in this debate is itself read two ways. The Catholic NABRE notes say "for any cause whatever" is "peculiar to Matthew and has been interpreted by some as meaning that Jesus was being asked to take sides in the dispute between the schools of Hillel and Shammai." They go on: "It is unlikely, however, that to ask Jesus' opinion about the differing views of two Jewish schools, both highly respected, could be described as 'testing' him." Lesson 11 reports Wyatt Graham's own conclusion that Jesus held "a third position, one closer to Shammai than Hillel." In this app's BSB, Mark's question has no "for any reason": "Is it lawful for a man to divorce his wife?" (Mark 10:2).

**Two quotations from Genesis (19:4-6).** Jesus asks, "Have you not read that from the beginning the Creator 'made them male and female,'" then quotes Genesis 2:24 (19:5). The first words come from Genesis 1:27, which ends "male and female He created them" (BSB). The English verbs differ; the Greek does not. All nine editions Bible Hub lists for 19:4 read *arsen kai thēly epoiēsen autous*, the same five words that end Genesis 1:27 in Swete's Septuagint. In the New Testament of this app's BSB, "male and female" occurs only at Matthew 19:4 and Mark 10:6 (script).

**"Moses order" and "Moses permitted" (19:7-8).** "Why then," the Pharisees ask, "did Moses order a man to give his wife a certificate of divorce and send her away?" (19:7). The law they mean is Deuteronomy 24:1: "he may write her a certificate of divorce, hand it to her, and send her away from his house" (BSB). The next verses forbid the first husband to take her back after a second marriage (24:2-4). Jesus answers, "Moses permitted you to divorce your wives because of your hardness of heart; but it was not this way from the beginning" (19:8). The Greek verbs differ as the English does: *eneteilato*, "commanded," in the Pharisees' mouth (19:7), and *epetrepsen*, "permitted," in Jesus' answer (19:8). Mark has the same two verbs with the speakers reversed: Jesus asks, "What did Moses command you?" and they answer, "Moses permitted" (Mark 10:3-4), though Jesus then calls it a "commandment" (10:5). "Certificate of divorce" occurs seven times in the BSB, two of them in Deuteronomy 24 (script; see the connection below).

**The saying and its exception (19:9).** "Now I tell you that whoever divorces his wife, except for sexual immorality, and marries another woman, commits adultery." The Greek word behind "sexual immorality" is *porneia*. Three textual facts can be checked on Bible Hub's Greek pages.

- The exception is worded differently from 5:32. There all nine editions read *parektos logou porneias*. Here seven read *mē epi porneia* and the two Textus Receptus editions read *ei mē epi porneia*. The NABRE notes say the clause "occurs also in Mt 19:9, where the Greek is slightly different."
- Five editions (SBL 2010, the Byzantine Majority, the Greek Orthodox Church text and both Textus Receptus editions) add a second clause, *kai ho apolelymenēn gamēsas moichatai*, "and he who marries a divorced woman commits adultery." Nestle 1904, Westcott-Hort, its NA27 variant line and Tischendorf do not. The BSB prints no second clause in 19:9.
- In this app's BSB, "except for sexual immorality" occurs only at Matthew 5:32 and 19:9 (script). Mark 10:11 and Luke 16:18 have no exception. Of the Gospel sayings, only Mark adds the case of a woman who "divorces her husband" (Mark 10:12). The words built on "divorc-" occur nine times in seven verses of Matthew: 1:19 (Joseph), 5:31-32 and 19:3-9.

**The disciples' reply and the eunuch saying (19:10-12).** The disciples say, "If this is the case between a man and his wife, it is better not to marry" (19:10). Jesus answers, "Not everyone can accept this word ... but only those to whom it has been given" (19:11), and names three groups: "eunuchs who were born that way; others were made that way by men; and still others live like eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven" (19:12). Here the BSB translates by sense. All nine Greek editions read *eunouchisan heautous*, "made themselves eunuchs," which the BSB renders "live like eunuchs." The Catholic NABRE translates by sense too ("some, because they have renounced marriage"), and its note adds, "literally, 'have made themselves eunuchs.'" In the New Testament of this app's BSB, "eunuch" or "eunuchs" occurs only here and in Acts 8:27-39, the Ethiopian official (script). The NABRE notes call 19:10-12 "peculiar to Matthew."

**The children (19:13-15).** Children are brought "for Him to place His hands on them and pray for them," and "the disciples rebuked those who brought them" (19:13). Jesus says, "Let the little children come to Me, and do not hinder them! For the kingdom of heaven belongs to such as these" (19:14); Mark 10:14 and Luke 18:16 have the same sentence with "kingdom of God." One chapter earlier, Jesus said, "unless you change and become like little children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven" (18:3), the saying lesson 38 treats.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 19:1-15 is built, not on what the arrangement is meant to teach.

**"From the beginning," twice.** Jesus' first answer appeals to what the Creator did "from the beginning" (19:4); His second ends, "it was not this way from the beginning" (19:8). In Matthew the phrase occurs only in these two verses and at 24:21 (script). The Pharisees' appeal to Moses (19:7) sits between the two.

**"Accept," three times.** The answer to the disciples opens, "Not everyone can accept this word" (19:11), and closes, "The one who can accept this should accept it" (19:12). Whether "this word" points back to the divorce saying or forward to the eunuch saying is one of the questions the readings below answer differently.

## Positions

Three disputes attach to this passage: what the exception in 19:9 permits, what "for the sake of the kingdom" asks, and whether 19:13-15 bears on baptizing infants. A fourth section names a misuse. Each tradition is reported from its own published words. None of the readings below is ranked.

**What does "except for sexual immorality" (19:9) permit?** This question bears on who may divorce and remarry, and on what terms. Lesson 11 reported the Catechism §§2382-2384, Metropolitan Kallistos Ware's account of Orthodox practice, and a Wikipedia summary of the Protestant reading at 5:32; here each tradition is read again from documents read for this lesson.

*Roman Catholic.* The Catechism teaches that Jesus "unequivocally taught the original meaning of the union of man and woman as the Creator willed it from the beginning: permission given by Moses to divorce one's wife was a concession to the hardness of hearts. The matrimonial union of man and woman is indissoluble" (§1614). The next paragraph adds that "Jesus has not placed on spouses a burden impossible to bear, or too heavy - heavier than the Law of Moses," and that "he himself gives the strength and grace to live marriage in the new dimension of the Reign of God" (§1615). Between the baptized, "a ratified and consummated marriage cannot be dissolved by any human power or for any reason other than death" (§2382). The Catechism also allows that "The separation of spouses while maintaining the marriage bond can be legitimate in certain cases provided for by canon law," and that civil divorce, if it "remains the only possible way of ensuring certain legal rights, the care of the children, or the protection of inheritance, it can be tolerated and does not constitute a moral offense" (§2383). It distinguishes a spouse who "is the innocent victim of a divorce decreed by civil law" and "has not contravened the moral law" (§2386). Where a marriage was never valid, a Church tribunal "can declare the nullity of a marriage, i.e., that the marriage never existed," and then "the contracting parties are free to marry, provided the natural obligations of a previous union are discharged" (§1629). On the exception itself, the NABRE translates it "unless the marriage is unlawful." Its notes say the absolute sayings in Mark, Luke and Paul are those that "most scholars agree ... represent the stand of Jesus," and that the "unlawfulness" refers to "a situation peculiar to his community: the violation of Mosaic law forbidding marriage between persons of certain blood and/or legal relationship (Lv 18:6–18)." They note that "Matthew's 'exceptive clauses' are understood by some as a modification of the absolute prohibition," and conclude: "In this interpretation, the clause constitutes no exception to the absolute prohibition of divorce when the marriage is lawful."

*Eastern Orthodox.* The bishops of the Orthodox Church in America, in their encyclical *On Marriage*, introduce 19:9 with the words "The Lord Himself specifically condemned divorce," and write: "The Church does not allow divorce any more than she allows sin; she does not permit something which the Lord Himself has specifically condemned." They continue: "The Church can and does practice mercy and forgiveness," and offers "the opportunity for repentance and forgiveness and the possibility for a new beginning, with final judgment resting in the hands of the Lord." "The permission to remarry according to the 'Order of Second Marriage' may eventually be granted divorced persons," on a decision by the diocesan bishop, and "A period of penance may be imposed." "While tolerating a second marriage and in certain cases a third, the Church completely forbids a fourth marital union." The OCA's answer to a reader's question says the service for a second or third marriage "bears a penitential character," and that such marriages "are performed by 'economy'—that is, out of concern for the spiritual well being of the parties involved and as an exception to the rule, so to speak." Its 1992 synodal statement adds that "Widowed and divorced persons who remarry do so not as their right but as recipients of a special gift of God's mercy." These OCA documents do not tie the permission to the wording of 19:9; Kallistos Ware, quoted in lesson 11, does.

*Protestant (Reformed and Presbyterian sources).* The Westminster Confession (chapter 24, in the text the Orthodox Presbyterian Church publishes) teaches: "In the case of adultery after marriage, it is lawful for the innocent party to sue out a divorce: and, after the divorce, to marry another, as if the offending party were dead" (24.5). It adds that "nothing but adultery, or such willful desertion as can no way be remedied by the church, or civil magistrate, is cause sufficient of dissolving the bond of marriage," with "a public and orderly course of proceeding" (24.6). Calvin, on 19:9: "an exception is added; for the woman, by fornication, cuts herself off ... from her husband, and sets him at liberty." He applies the same to a husband's adultery, and reads the words on whoever marries "her that is divorced" as "restricted to unlawful and frivolous divorces." The Wikipedia article on Matthew 5:32, a tertiary source, reports that this reading "was first advanced by Desiderius Erasmus, and received the backing of Martin Luther, John Calvin, and most other major Protestant thinkers"; Erasmus's own text was not read for this lesson. The same article reports that Zwingli and Bullinger read *porneia* more widely. A recent Presbyterian study reads the Confession's "desertion" widely. The Presbyterian Church in America's committee on domestic abuse (report of 2022, which says it is "information" and not "binding or obligatory") holds that "domestic abuse is a form of desertion because the abuser's violence creates a forced separation between spouses that is equivalent to abandonment," a position it attributes to an earlier PCA committee on divorce and remarriage.

*Objections each reading faces, from named sources.* The Catholic reading of *porneia* as an unlawful union meets Calvin and the Westminster Confession, quoted above, who read the clause as a real ground for ending a valid marriage. Augustine, quoted in the Catena Aurea as the Wikipedia article reproduces it, read *porneia* as adultery ("it is no question at all that it is allowed for the fornication by carnal sin"), while holding that the separated wife must "abstain from a fresh marriage as long as her husband lives"; his reading meets the NABRE's unlawful-union reading, not the Catechism's teaching on the bond. The Orthodox practice of permitting a second marriage meets the Catechism's §2382: a ratified and consummated marriage "cannot be dissolved by any human power." The same paragraph stands against the Protestant reading. The NABRE notes also object to it: the absolute sayings "represent the stand of Jesus," and the clause refers to a situation "peculiar to his community." The wider readings meet Calvin's own limit: "Those who search for other reasons ought justly to be set at nought, because they choose to be wise above the heavenly teacher." Calvin was writing about grounds such as leprosy and incurable illness, not about abuse, which he does not discuss on this verse.

The readings disagree about whether the exception names an invalid union (the reading in the Catholic NABRE notes; the Catechism allows separation with the bond intact, §2383), an exception that mercy may grant (the Orthodox reading), or a ground on which a valid marriage may end and the innocent party remarry (the Protestant reading). This lesson reports them and does not decide among them. A reader working this question through in their own marriage needs a pastor, priest or counselor of their own tradition, not a study note.

**What does "for the sake of the kingdom of heaven" (19:12) ask?**

*Roman Catholic.* The Catechism quotes 19:12 in full and teaches that "Christ himself has invited certain persons to follow him in this way of life, of which he remains the model" (§1618). "Virginity for the sake of the kingdom of heaven is an unfolding of baptismal grace," and a sign "which also recalls that marriage is a reality of this present age which is passing away" (§1619). It holds the two together: "Esteem of virginity for the sake of the kingdom and the Christian understanding of marriage are inseparable, and they reinforce each other" (§1620).

*Eastern Orthodox.* The OCA's 1992 synodal statement: "The 'angelic way' of monastic life is especially blessed for men and women 'able to receive this' as those so 'assigned' and 'called' (cf. Matthew 19:10-12; 1 Corinthians 7)." It adds that "Celibacy and virginity are also to be defended and protected from vilification and ridicule."

*Protestant.* Calvin reads 19:11 as a limit: "the gift of continence is a special gift," and "it is not free to all to make what choice they please." "For the sake of the kingdom," he writes, means that unmarried persons should have this aim: "that, being freed from all cares, they may apply themselves more readily to the duties of piety." He denies that celibacy is meritorious in itself: "it is not in itself more pleasing to God than fasting."

*A further reading.* The NABRE notes report that "Some scholars take the last class to be those who have been divorced by their spouses and have refused to enter another marriage," so that "this word" (19:11) would refer back to the divorce saying.

*Objections each reading faces.* Against the reading of vowed celibacy as a calling one may choose, Calvin argues that "This castration ... is not left to free will," and he opposes vows of celibacy at length (his polemic is not reproduced here). Against Calvin's reading, Chrysostom, preaching on 19:11, says the saying was given so "that you might learn that the conflict is great, not that you should suspect any compulsory allotments. For it is given to those, even to the willing." Against the divorced-spouse reading, the NABRE notes judge it "more likely that it is rather those who have chosen never to marry, since that suits better the optional nature of the decision."

**A literal reading, and what answered it.** Eusebius reports that the young Origen, while teaching in Alexandria, "took the words, There are eunuchs who have made themselves eunuchs for the kingdom of heaven's sake, Matthew 19:12 in too literal and extreme a sense," and castrated himself (*Church History* 6.8). Chrysostom, preaching on this verse, says Jesus "means not the excision of the members, far from it, but the putting away of wicked thoughts." The first canon of the Council of Nicaea (325) bars from the clergy any man who, in good health, mutilated himself, while admitting those made eunuchs by surgery, by barbarians or by their masters. No tradition reported in this lesson reads 19:12 as a call to harm the body. If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, in the United States you can call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org.

**Do 19:13-15 bear on baptizing infants?** The scene names laying on of hands and prayer; it does not mention baptism. Readers disagree about whether it bears on the practice.

*Roman Catholic.* The Catechism teaches that "The sheer gratuitousness of the grace of salvation is particularly manifest in infant Baptism" (§1250), and that "The practice of infant Baptism is an immemorial tradition of the Church" (§1252). Quoting this saying, it says that "Jesus' tenderness toward children which caused him to say: 'Let the children come to me, do not hinder them,'" allows "hope" for children who die unbaptized, and adds: "All the more urgent is the Church's call not to prevent little children coming to Christ through the gift of holy Baptism" (§1261). The NABRE notes are more reserved: "This account is understood by some as intended to justify the practice of infant baptism," based "principally on the command not to prevent the children from coming, since that word sometimes has a baptismal connotation in the New Testament; see Acts 8:36." The Catechism also reports that "The Eastern Churches" give Communion to newly baptized "little children, recalling the Lord's words" (§1244); that is a Catholic description of Eastern practice, and no Orthodox source on this verse was read.

*Reformed and Lutheran.* Calvin calls the narrative "highly useful," because it shows that Christ receives those "not yet of age to know how much they need his grace," and writes "that we employ this passage as a shield against the Anabaptists." His reasoning: "if they were partakers of the spiritual gifts, which are represented by Baptism, it is unreasonable that they should be deprived of the outward sign." Heinrich Meyer, a 19th-century German Lutheran theologian (Wikipedia, tertiary, for the identification), finds here "the warrant, but, according to 1 Corinthians 7:14, not the necessity, for infant baptism."

*Baptist.* John Gill, an 18th-century English Particular Baptist (Wikipedia, tertiary, for the identification), notes that the children were brought "that he should put his hands on them, and pray; not that he should baptize them, nor did he." He adds, "Christ's entire silence about the baptism of infants at this time, when he had such an opportunity of speaking of it to his disciples, had it been his will, has no favourable aspect on such a practice." The Second London Baptist Confession holds that those "who do actually profess repentance towards God, faith in, and obedience to, our Lord Jesus Christ, are the only proper subjects" of baptism (29.2).

*The children as a pattern.* Chrysostom reads the scene as a lesson in humility: "that by choice we should practise these things, which young children have by nature." Gill reads the words the same way: the children are "lively emblems" of what converted persons should be. The Pulpit Commentary also notes that Jesus says "of such," not "of these," "intimating that it is not to the age, but to the disposition and character, that he refers," though it holds, too, that "From this passage has been derived a cogent argument for infant baptism."

*Objections each reading faces.* Against reading the scene as warrant for infant baptism, Gill: the evangelists are silent and "Christ baptized none." Against Gill, Calvin: "the laying on of hands was not a trifling or empty sign, and the prayers of Christ were not idly wasted in air." Against reading "such as these" as disposition only, Calvin holds that "Under this term he includes both little children and those who resemble them." Against making the verse carry the rite, the NABRE notes present the baptismal reading as one held "by some."

**A misuse to name plainly: divorce teaching and abuse.** Sayings on the permanence of marriage, this one among them, have been used to keep people in abusive marriages. Two documents answer that use directly, and a third condemns abuse in the family more generally. The U.S. Catholic bishops' *When I Call for Help* (2002) says: "As bishops, we condemn the use of the Bible to support abusive behavior in any form." Besides the lines quoted at the top of this lesson, they write that abused women "may fear that they cannot re-marry in the Church," and answer: "Violence and abuse, not divorce, break up a marriage." They encourage abused persons who have divorced "to investigate the possibility of seeking an annulment." The OCA's 1992 statement does not speak to divorce teaching here, but says, "All forms of physical, spiritual, psychological and emotional abuse of men, women and children are to be condemned," and that the "headship" of the husband "is not to be interpreted as supportive in any way of abusive behavior." The PCA committee's report lists among the ways abused people are exposed to further harm the case "when authorities, whether civil or ecclesiastical, require abuse victims to stay in abusive relationships." As for the text, 19:3-9 answers a question about a husband sending his wife away; nothing in 19:1-15 tells anyone to remain where they are being harmed. The traditions handle separation, divorce and remarriage after abuse differently, as the sources above show; this lesson does not rank them. If this is your situation, the referral at the top of this lesson is for you.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key. Your own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 19:13-14: "the disciples rebuked those who brought them" (19:13), and Jesus answered, "Let the little children come to Me, and do not hinder them!" (19:14, BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the disciples stop people they judge unimportant, and Jesus reverses them. Inference, the lesson's own: disciples who had just heard "become like little children" (18:3) were blocking the very people Jesus had named as their pattern.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Those who serve Jesus can become the obstacle between Him and people they consider unimportant.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the disciples' habit of screening people; Jesus' welcome of those with least standing. Not carried forward: the laying on of hands, or any settled answer to the baptism question in Positions.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a narrative example and a direct command to disciples, carried over by analogy. Promise scope: 19:14 says "the kingdom of heaven belongs to such as these." It promises nothing about a particular child's future, and none is claimed here.

**Modern domain and situation.** Church life. A volunteer at a church's front door notices that a family with a loud, restless child keeps being steered to the overflow room "so others can worship."

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: welcome over convenience. One faithful response: the volunteer raises it with the leaders, asks the family what would help them, and helps make room for them in the main service.

**Cautions.** Welcoming children includes keeping them safe. Churches should follow their own child-safeguarding policies and the law; welcome never means unsupervised access to children. This bridge is a worked example for the learner to test, not counsel for any particular person or church.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Mark sets the absolute saying "inside the house" (Mark 10:10), and Matthew gives an exception twice (5:32; 19:9). What would you need to know about how the Gospels were written to judge whether Matthew's clause states something Mark assumed, or adds something Mark lacks?
2. Jesus answers the Pharisees from Genesis before He answers them from Moses. Does the order of His answers (19:4-6, then 19:8) bear on any reading in Positions, or only on how the argument is built?
3. Does "this word" in 19:11 point back to the divorce saying or forward to the eunuch saying? What in 19:10-12 helps you decide?
4. The disciples in 19:13 are the same ones who were told "become like little children" in 18:3. Why might Matthew place these scenes so close together?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 19:1-15 in order. (b) Quote 19:9 in the BSB's own words. (c) Name two things Matthew has that Mark 10:1-16 lacks, and one thing Mark has that Matthew lacks.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the question and the Mishnah's record; (1:00-2:00) Genesis, Moses and the Greek; (2:00-3:30) the exception, with three traditions and one objection each; (3:30-4:15) the eunuch saying, Origen and what answered him; (4:15-5:00) the children and baptism, and the misuse of divorce teaching.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: "Mark and Luke have no exception, so Matthew's clause must be a later softening." Before answering, note that the Catholic NABRE notes and the Protestant Westminster Confession both treat Matthew's clause as part of Scripture to be explained, though they explain it differently. A second candidate, running the other way: "The exception plainly allows divorce for adultery; any other reading is special pleading." Before answering, look again at the NABRE note on Leviticus 18 and at the Greek difference between 5:32 and 19:9.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 19:1-15 does not mention baptism; whether it bears on infant baptism is argued from other texts and from practice. (b) Matthew 19:12 does not tell anyone to harm the body; every tradition reported here reads it otherwise. (c) Matthew 19:3-9 does not tell anyone to stay where they are being harmed; it answers a question about a husband sending his wife away.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words of both texts. The six attached to this lesson:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genesis 1:27 | quotation | explicit | "Have you not read"; the quoted words in 19:4 match the Septuagint's ending of Genesis 1:27 |
| Genesis 2:24 | quotation | explicit | Authored earlier (conn-gen2-marriage-matt19); quoted in 19:5 |
| Deuteronomy 24:1 | explicit_reference | explicit | The speakers name Moses; "certificate of divorce" (*biblion apostasiou*) shared, but summarized, not quoted |
| Matthew 5:31-32 | motif | strong | "Except for sexual immorality" only here and at 19:9 in the BSB; different Greek wording |
| Mark 10:1-16 | parallel | strong | Same two scenes in the same order; Mark has no exception and adds 10:12 |
| Isaiah 56:3-5 | motif | plausible | Eunuchs given a place among God's people; only the Pulpit Commentary, on Bible Hub's 19:12 page, names the link |

The two Genesis rows are this lesson's thread connections to the Old Testament: Jesus answers a question about Moses' law by going back to creation. The Isaiah row is graded low on purpose. The shared word is real, and one commentator compares the texts, but nothing in Matthew points to Isaiah. Malachi 2:16 ("For I hate divorce," BSB), which the Talmud cites at Gittin 90b, was considered and not authored: Matthew 19 does not quote or name it, and linking it would argue one reading of the passage.

## Sources and Limits

- **Read directly, with raw copies saved:** every source in the frontmatter list except Graham, including the Mishnah and Talmud in Sefaria's William Davidson translation (its bracketed glosses are the translator's), the Catechism, the NABRE notes, three OCA documents, the PCA report, the Westminster Confession, Calvin, Chrysostom, Eusebius, the Nicene canons, and Bible Hub's commentary, Greek and Septuagint pages. *When I Call for Help* was fetched on 2026-10-15 and checked against the copy saved on 2026-10-08 for the lesson on Matthew 16.
- **Tertiary and secondhand:** the Erasmus, Zwingli, Bullinger and Augustine reports come from Wikipedia's Matthew 5:32 article; none of their own texts was read. Gill's denomination comes from Wikipedia. Carson and Graham are reported as lesson 11 reported them. Meyer's identification comes from Wikipedia. No Lutheran, Anglican, Baptist or Methodist statement on 19:9 was read; the wider Protestant history is Wikipedia's.
- **Orthodox sources.** The Greek Orthodox Archdiocese of America's pages returned HTTP 403 and archived copies could not be fetched, so no GOARCH text is quoted. The Hong Kong Metropolis page quoting Kallistos Ware returned an error on 2026-10-15; its use here is only lesson 11's. Orthodox practice is reported from the Orthodox Church in America; practice can differ between jurisdictions, and this lesson did not check how. No Orthodox source on 19:13-15 was read.
- **Bible Hub** reproduces public-domain commentaries without page numbers; attributions follow its section labels. Calvin and Gill use polemical language about other churches here; it is not reproduced. Greek readings are as Bible Hub prints them; no critical apparatus or Hebrew lexicon was consulted.
- **Eusebius.** Eusebius's own account of the act is mixed; his praise of it is not reproduced.
- **Not covered:** modern critical commentaries on Matthew; the Qumran texts the NABRE notes mention; civil divorce law anywhere. 19:4-6 and 19:12 are cited on several sides of current debates over sexuality, gender and intersex people; this lesson does not report those debates.
- **Counts and quotations.** Every count or "only" claim was produced by a case-insensitive, word-boundary Node script over this app's BSB files, and its output was kept. Every BSB quotation in this lesson and its five new connection files was checked by script against `web/public/bible/BSB/*.json`, before and after drafting.
- **Referrals.** The National Domestic Violence Hotline's number and text route were confirmed on thehotline.org, and its "free, confidential" support on its About page, and the 988 Lifeline's call, text and chat routes on 988lifeline.org, both on 2026-10-15. Both are U.S. services; readers elsewhere should use local services. Numbers can change; confirm before relying on them.
