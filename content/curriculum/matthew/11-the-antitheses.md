Matthew 5:21-48 is the material the Salt and Light lesson in this curriculum already identified as the destination of 5:17-20's four-verse build-up: that lesson's own Literary Design section reads 5:17-20 as moving from a general claim about Jesus's relationship to "the Law or the Prophets" (5:17, BSB) to a comparative statement that "unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven" (5:20, BSB), ending exactly where 5:21's first "You have heard that it was said" begins. What follows is six units built on one recurring pattern -- a cited saying from "the ancients," then Jesus's own counter-statement, introduced each time by some form of "But I tell you" -- addressing anger and murder (5:21-26), lust and adultery (5:27-30), divorce (5:31-32), oaths (5:33-37), retaliation (5:38-42), and love of enemies (5:43-48). Two of these six units carry real, long-running, named disagreement among entire Christian traditions and individual scholars, with real practical consequence for people's lives: what 5:31-32 permits regarding divorce and remarriage, and what 5:38-42's "do not resist an evil person" actually instructs. This lesson reports both in the terms their own participants state them -- the same discipline the Salt and Light lesson already practiced on the four "fulfill" traditions -- and treats a third, narrower dispute over oaths more briefly. The other three units are treated as Context and Literary Design rather than forced into named Positions, since this lesson did not find a comparably substantive, named scholarly division behind "do not murder," "do not commit adultery," or "love your enemies" themselves (as opposed to the real word-study question behind 5:48's "perfect," which Questions to Carry below leaves open rather than resolving).

## Context

**The six-unit refrain, and one real variation in its own wording.** Each unit opens with a citation formula and closes the citation with a direct quotation in single marks. The six opening formulas are not word-for-word identical, a checkable fact against this app's own BSB wording: "You have heard that it was said to the ancients" (5:21, murder; 5:33, oaths); "You have heard that it was said" (5:27, adultery; 5:38, retaliation; 5:43, enemies); and "It has also been said" (5:31, divorce) -- the only one of the six that does not open with "you have heard." Whether this variation marks anything beyond stylistic variety is not something this lesson resolves; it is stated here only as a checkable textual fact, not an argument.

**Unit 1 -- murder and anger (5:21-26).** The citation is "Do not murder" (5:21, BSB). Exodus 20:13 and Deuteronomy 5:17 state the same prohibition in this app's own translation -- both read "You shall not murder," word for word identical to each other -- though Matthew's own citation phrases it more briefly than either Decalogue text ("Do not murder" against "You shall not murder"), a real wording difference worth stating directly rather than overlooking (see the connection to Exodus 20:13 below). Jesus's counter-statement in 5:22 names three escalating consequences for three escalating offenses: being angry with a brother, calling him "Raca," and calling him "You fool." Per the Wikipedia article on Matthew 5:22 used here, "Raca" is most commonly read as a reference to the Aramaic *reka*, which "literally means 'empty one,' but probably meant 'empty headed,' or 'foolish'" -- and the same article reports a real disagreement over just how severe an insult it is, with David Hill reading it as "very grievous" against R.T. France reading it as "a minor slur." The same article identifies "the council" in 5:22 as "generally seen as a reference to the Sanhedrin, the council of leading religious thinkers that acted as the central court in Jerusalem," and reports the verse's three consequences as "an escalating scale of punishment," a structural observation attributed there to France. The unit then turns from speech to a concrete case: reconciling with "your brother" before completing a sacrifice at the altar (5:23-24) and reconciling with "your adversary" before a court renders judgment (5:25-26) -- two distinct relationships (brother; legal adversary) and two distinct settings (temple; court), both treated as more urgent than a pending religious or legal obligation.

**Unit 2 -- adultery and lust (5:27-30).** "Do not commit adultery" (5:27, BSB) states the same prohibition Exodus 20:14 and Deuteronomy 5:18 state -- both read "You shall not commit adultery" in this app's own translation, word for word identical to each other, though again phrased more briefly in Matthew's own citation than in either Decalogue text. Jesus's counter-statement relocates the offense: "anyone who looks at a woman to lust after her has already committed adultery with her in his heart" (5:28, BSB) -- the text's own wording locating the act "in his heart," not qualifying how that location relates to the external command it follows. The eye-gouging and hand-cutting that follow (5:29-30) are read, per the Wikipedia article on Matthew 5:29 used here, as hyperbole by most commentators: "Most commentators take this verse as a clear example of hyperbole," and the same article reports a structural argument for that reading from a scholar named only as Jones, "that the mention of only the right eye makes it clear that Jesus is not meaning for this to be taken literally, as the left eye would be just as likely to lead into sin." R.T. France is reported in the same article calling the verse's severity "unparalleled in the contemporary literature," while a dissenting minority reading is also reported: "De Bruin has argued that Jesus's original commands were meant to be taken literally." This lesson does not treat the hyperbole question as a named Positions-level dispute, since the article itself reports near-consensus on one side with one identified dissent, not a comparably balanced division.

**Unit 3 -- divorce (5:31-32).** The citation in 5:31 paraphrases rather than quotes Deuteronomy 24:1's longer sentence: "If a man marries a woman, but she becomes displeasing to him because he finds some indecency in her, he may write her a certificate of divorce, hand it to her, and send her away from his house" (Deuteronomy 24:1, BSB). What counts as "indecency" is not defined by that verse itself, and a documented first-century Jewish debate over exactly that question sits directly behind Matthew 19:3's Pharisees asking Jesus, "Is it lawful for a man to divorce his wife for any reason?" (BSB) -- a question this lesson's Positions section below addresses further. Jesus's own counter-statement names one exception by name: "anyone who divorces his wife, except for sexual immorality, brings adultery upon her" (5:32, BSB). A real, checkable textual wrinkle sits alongside this exception: Mark's and Luke's versions of the same teaching carry no such clause at all. Mark 10:11 reads, "Whoever divorces his wife and marries another woman commits adultery against her" (BSB), and Luke 16:18 reads almost identically, with neither verse naming an exception. The Wikipedia article on Matthew 5:32 used here reports two competing explanations for this difference without choosing between them: that "Matthew was adding more detail than the other gospels for his more Jewish audience," against the view that "the exception was not mentioned in the other Gospels because it was so obvious as to be implicit to contemporary readers." This lesson's Positions section below reports how different Christian traditions read the Greek word behind "sexual immorality" (*porneia*) and what consequence, if any, follows from it.

**Unit 4 -- oaths (5:33-37).** Unlike the murder, adultery, and retaliation units, 5:33's citation does not match any single Old Testament verse's exact wording. It summarizes a cluster of related passages that share a theme -- truthful, kept vows -- rather than one shared sentence: "You must not swear falsely by My name" (Leviticus 19:12, BSB); "If a man makes a vow to the LORD... he must not break his word; he must do everything he has promised" (Numbers 30:2, BSB); and "If you make a vow to the LORD your God, do not be slow to keep it" (Deuteronomy 23:21, BSB). Jesus's counter-statement, "I tell you not to swear at all" (5:34, BSB), is followed by three grounding clauses, each introduced by "for": heaven "is God's throne," earth "is His footstool," Jerusalem "is the city of the great King" (5:34-35, BSB) -- a repeated rhetorical pattern naming why each proposed substitute for swearing by God's own name still, on this unit's own logic, invokes God. The unit closes with a positive instruction, "let your 'Yes' be 'Yes,' and your 'No,' 'No.'" (5:37, BSB), which recurs in close to identical wording at James 5:12 (see the connection below). This lesson's Positions section below reports a real, long-running dispute over how literally "not at all" should be read.

**Unit 5 -- retaliation (5:38-42).** "Eye for eye and tooth for tooth" (5:38, BSB) shares its core phrase with three Torah locations -- Exodus 21:24, Leviticus 24:20, Deuteronomy 19:21 -- that are not identical sentences to each other (they list different numbers of paired body parts and sit inside different surrounding clauses, a checkable fact against this app's own BSB wording; see the connection to Exodus 21:24 below). The Wikipedia article on Matthew 5:38 used here reports this principle, known as *lex talionis*, functioning historically as "a moderate rule compared with the blood feuds described in Genesis 4:23-24" -- a limit on proportional punishment, not a license for unlimited retaliation -- and reports R.T. France stating that "by Jesus' era such punishments were no longer in practice, rather they had been replaced with fines judged to be of equal value to the damage caused." Jesus's counter-statement, "I tell you not to resist an evil person" (5:39, BSB), is followed by three concrete cases rather than abstract grounding clauses: a slap on the right cheek (5:39), a lawsuit over a tunic (5:40), and forced labor for "one mile" (5:41). Real, substantive, named scholarly disagreement attaches to what these three cases instruct, reported in full in Positions below.

**Unit 6 -- love of enemies (5:43-48).** "Love your neighbor" (5:43, BSB) matches Leviticus 19:18's wording; its paired clause, "Hate your enemy," does not match any Old Testament verse at all. The Wikipedia article on Matthew 5:43 used here states plainly, "Nowhere in the Old Testament does it directly state that one should hate one's enemies," and reports the closest documented parallel to this exact pairing as "the rules of the Qumran community," which the same article states "stated that believers should love everyone God has elected and hate everyone he has cast aside." The same article explains, "In Jesus' time neighbour was interpreted to mean fellow Israelites, and to exclude all others" -- a first-century narrowing of Leviticus 19:18's own scope that this lesson reports as a checkable claim about period usage, not an argument this lesson is making about what Leviticus 19:18 itself originally meant. Jesus's counter-statement, "love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (5:44, BSB), is grounded not in an abstract principle but in God's own observed behavior: "He causes His sun to rise on the evil and the good, and sends rain on the righteous and the unrighteous" (5:45, BSB). The unit, and the whole six-unit section, closes with "Be perfect, therefore, as your heavenly Father is perfect" (5:48, BSB). The Greek word behind "perfect," *teleios*, is reported by the Wikipedia article on Matthew 5:48 used here as meaning, per Thayer's Greek Lexicon, "brought to its end, finished; lacking nothing necessary to completeness" -- and the same article reports Albert Barnes and William Barclay both reading the word as completeness of function rather than sinless perfection, while John Gill is reported holding that "this perfection is to be restrained to the subject Christ is upon, love to men." The same article also notes that Luke's version of this saying (Luke 6:36, BSB) reads "Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful" rather than "perfect" -- a real synoptic difference this lesson leaves open in Questions to Carry rather than resolving here.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 5:21-48 is built, not on what that construction is meant to teach.

**Three units cite a short, identifiable Old Testament command; three draw on more diffuse material.** "Do not murder" (5:21) and "Do not commit adultery" (5:27) each cite a Decalogue command that recurs word for word in exactly two Torah locations (Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5, which agree with each other exactly); Matthew's own citation of each command is a shorter phrasing than either Torah location's own wording, as Context above states, not one identical sentence repeated three times. "Eye for eye and tooth for tooth" (5:38) likewise shares its core phrase with three Torah locations that are not identical sentences to each other or to Matthew's own citation of them, as Context above also states. The other three units draw on more diffuse material: 5:31's divorce citation paraphrases Deuteronomy 24:1 rather than quoting it; 5:33's oath citation summarizes a thematic cluster of three passages rather than one sentence; and 5:43's second half, "Hate your enemy," is not an Old Testament quotation at all. This three-and-three grouping is a checkable fact about how precisely each citation maps onto nameable Old Testament wording, not a claim about which units matter more.

**A grounding-clause pattern that appears in some units and not others.** The oaths unit states three explicit reasons, each introduced by "for" (5:34-35), tying each proposed object of swearing back to God. The enemies unit grounds its command in a single stated fact about God's own behavior (5:45) rather than a chain of "for" clauses. The murder and retaliation units ground their counter-statements in concrete cases instead -- an altar scene and a courtroom scene (5:23-26); a slap, a lawsuit, and forced labor (5:39-41) -- with no abstract "for" clause attached to any of them. This is a checkable variation in how each unit chooses to make its case, not a hierarchy among the six.

**The section's own closing line doubles as the closing line of the whole discourse begun at 5:17.** Read in sequence, the six units move from offenses addressed at the level of internal disposition (anger, lust) through formal structures that bind people to each other and to truth (divorce, oaths) to conduct toward opponents and outsiders (retaliation, enemies), ending at 5:48's "Be perfect, therefore, as your heavenly Father is perfect." Matthew 6:1 then opens a new topic (giving, prayer, and fasting) with its own fresh address ("Be careful not to perform your righteous acts before men to be seen by them," BSB). That 5:48 sits at the hinge between these two blocks is a checkable fact about where Matthew places it in the text's own order; what that placement is meant to accomplish is not something this lesson decides.

## Positions

Three separate, named disputes attach to this passage. The first two carry the most substantive, long-running disagreement and are reported at length; the third is reported more briefly, since its practical stakes, while real, are narrower.

**What does Matthew 5:31-32 permit regarding divorce and remarriage?** This is the passage's most consequential dispute for people's actual lives -- it bears directly on who may remarry, and in some traditions on who may receive communion or be ordained -- and this lesson reports it with the same even-handedness the Salt and Light lesson in this curriculum already gave the four "fulfill" traditions, declining to rank the positions below.

The Catholic Church's Catechism (§§2382-2384, read directly for this lesson) states that "a ratified and consummated marriage cannot be dissolved by any human power or for any reason other than death" (§2382), and that entering a new union after a divorce constitutes "a situation of public and permanent adultery" even where civil law recognizes it (§2384). On this reading, Matthew 5:32's "except for sexual immorality" is not read as naming a ground on which a valid marriage may be dissolved; a reading reported, though not from a magisterial document, by a Catholic apologetics writer citing Bruce Metzger -- himself reported via the Wikipedia article on Matthew 5:32 used here -- holds that the Greek word behind "sexual immorality" (*porneia*) may instead refer to "marriage within the prohibited Levitical degrees," that is, a union invalid from the start rather than a valid marriage later broken. On this reading, the exception describes which unions were never real marriages to begin with, not a ground for ending one that was.

The Eastern Orthodox Church, per the Orthodox Metropolis of Hong Kong and Southeast Asia's statement of its position (quoting Metropolitan Kallistos of Diokleia's book *The Orthodox Church*, read directly for this lesson), permits both divorce and remarriage, grounding that permission in this exact exception clause: "Since Christ allowed an exception to His general ruling about the indissolubility of marriage, the Orthodox Church also is willing to allow an exception." A second or third marriage is permitted "by 'economy' (*oikonomia*)... as an exception to the rule," and its own rite "bears a penitential character" distinct from a first marriage's, with canon law "absolutely forbid[ding] a fourth" marriage. On this reading, the exception clause does real, exception-making work -- it is not read, as in the Catholic reading above, as describing marriages that were never valid.

Protestant churches, per the Wikipedia article on Matthew 5:32 used here, "have traditionally read *porneia* as adultery" -- a reading "first advanced by Desiderius Erasmus" that "received the backing of Martin Luther, John Calvin, and most other major Protestant thinkers," permitting the innocent party to divorce and remarry when the other commits adultery. This reading is not monolithic within Protestantism itself: the same article reports Huldrych Zwingli and Heinrich Bullinger reading *porneia* more broadly, "as referring to all manner of marital immorality, including spousal abuse and abandonment," a wider ground than adultery alone.

A real complication sits underneath all three traditions above: a documented Jewish legal debate that predates Jesus by roughly a generation. The Mishnah (Gittin 9:10, quoted by Wyatt Graham, read directly for this lesson) records the House of Shammai permitting divorce "only because he has found grounds for it in unchastity," against the House of Hillel permitting it for any cause at all, even "if she spoiled his dish." Matthew 19:3's question to Jesus, "Is it lawful for a man to divorce his wife for any reason?" (BSB), echoes this exact debate. Graham argues, quoting D.A. Carson, that Jesus does not simply adopt Shammai's position: Jesus "has come close to the school of Shammai on the grounds for exceptions, while taking a far more conservative stance than Shammai on who may remarry." Graham's own stated conclusion is that Matthew 19 "does not explicitly point to Jesus taking one side or the other," and that Jesus more likely holds "a third position, one closer to Shammai than Hillel, but not parallel to it." This matters for the three traditions above because each one is, in effect, proposing its own answer to a question a first-century debate already shows was live and contested before Jesus spoke.

A further layer comes from outside the Gospels. Paul writes to the Corinthian church that "a wife must not separate from her husband" and "a husband must not divorce his wife," explicitly naming this as the Lord's own instruction rather than his: "not I, but the Lord" (1 Corinthians 7:10-11, BSB). He then adds, naming the shift explicitly as his own apostolic judgment rather than the Lord's ("I, not the Lord," 7:12), an instruction for a case neither Matthew 5:32 nor 19:9 addresses: a believer whose unbelieving spouse chooses to leave. "The believing brother or sister is not bound in such cases" (7:15, BSB). What exactly "not bound" permits is itself read differently across the traditions above (see the connection to 1 Corinthians 7:10-15 below), which is a further sign of how much is genuinely unsettled, not a fourth tradition this lesson is separately naming.

These three traditions, and the complications layered underneath them, are not simply three labels for one agreed conclusion. All three read Matthew 5:32 as stating a real exception in some sense; they disagree, with real consequence for actual people's remarriage and standing in their own church, about whether that exception describes an invalid marriage never truly formed (the Catholic reading), a case where the general rule of permanence may still be set aside (the Orthodox reading), or straightforward grounds for the innocent party to remarry (the Protestant exception-view reading, itself not of one mind on how broad "sexual immorality" reaches). This lesson states what each tradition's own document says and does not resolve which is correct; a reader working through this question for their own life, rather than as a matter of study, needs a qualified pastor or counselor from within their own tradition, not a study note.

**What does "I tell you not to resist an evil person" (5:39) instruct?** This dispute is about method rather than outcome -- no named position below argues Jesus is commanding personal revenge -- but the three readings below genuinely disagree about what nonviolence itself requires in practice, and this lesson reports all three in their own terms.

R.T. France, reported via the Wikipedia article on Matthew 5:39 used here, reads the Greek word behind "resist" (*anthistemi*) as carrying "a far more restricted meaning in the original Greek," translating it as something closer to "do not resist by legal means" -- a reading that connects the unit's first case (the cheek) to its second (a lawsuit over a tunic, 5:40) as two instances of the same instruction: do not use the legal system to retaliate against someone who has wronged you personally. Eduard Schweizer is reported in the same article comparing the verse's language to Deuteronomy 19:18 and Isaiah 50:8, both legal-context passages, supporting this narrower reading.

Walter Wink, in an essay read directly and in full for this lesson (*Jesus' Third Way*, from *The Powers That Be*, 1998), argues against both this legal-nonresistance reading and against what he calls the "traditional interpretation" of simple nonresistance to evil. Wink notes explicitly that his own reading departs from pacifism as commonly practiced: "Curiously enough, some pacifists have also bought the nonresistance interpretation, and therefore have rejected nonviolent direct action and civil disobedience as coercive and in violation of the law of Christ." His own argument rests on the Greek word Matthew uses for "resist," *antistenai*: he argues it is "most often used in the Greek version of the Old Testament as a technical term for warfare," describing "the way opposing armies would march toward each other until their ranks met" -- so that, on his reading, Jesus is not counseling passivity but refusing one particular method (armed confrontation), not resistance itself. Wink then reads each of the three cases as a specific act of nonviolent, assertive defiance rather than compliance: striking someone on the right cheek with a fist would naturally fall on the left cheek, he argues, so that "the only feasible blow is a backhand" -- a blow meant "to insult, humiliate, degrade" an inferior rather than injure an equal -- and turning the other cheek "makes it impossible for the master to use the backhand again," forcing a choice the social order had no answer for. The tunic-and-cloak case, he argues, pictures "the poorest of the poor" sued over their last possession, so that surrendering both garments leaves the debtor "stark naked" in court. Wink's own stated reason: "Nakedness was taboo in Judaism, and shame fell less on the naked party than on the person viewing or causing the nakedness" -- so the shame lands on the creditor, not the debtor. The second-mile case, he argues, exploits a real feature of Roman military law: a soldier could lawfully impress a civilian to carry his pack "a single mile," so that voluntarily carrying it a second mile placed the soldier himself "in jeopardy with [his] superiors" for exceeding the lawful limit. Wink states his own conclusion for all three together: "Jesus is not telling us to submit to evil, but to refuse to oppose it on its own terms... finding a third way, one that is at once assertive and yet nonviolent."

A third, older reading treats the unit as commanding something closer to what Wink explicitly argues against: literal, absolute nonresistance extending to a refusal of retaliatory force in any form, including armed defense and war. Menno Simons, quoted in Bert Friesen's survey article (read directly for this lesson, its Anabaptist/Mennonite section treating Matthew 5:38-42 by name), states the instruction plainly: "All Christians are commanded to love their enemies; to do good unto those who abuse and persecute them; to give the mantle when the cloak is taken, the other cheek when one is struck." On this reading, unlike Wink's, the point of turning the cheek or giving the cloak is not strategic exposure of an unjust system but a direct, literal refusal to retaliate in any form -- a reading this same Anabaptist tradition has historically extended to refusing military service and the swearing of judicial oaths (see the oaths dispute below).

A caution belongs alongside all three readings. The Wikipedia article on "Turning the other cheek" used here reports John Nolland cautioning that the Matthean saying is "not necessarily an endorsement" of any single social-political strategy at all: the unit's limited range of examples allows Jesus to "make the fundamental point in all sharpness without the need to grapple with the very real difficulty of defining boundaries of applicability." This lesson reports France's, Wink's, and the Anabaptist tradition's readings as three real, named, and substantively different accounts of what "do not resist" requires in practice; it does not resolve which is correct, and Nolland's caution above is a reason a reader might hold any one of them with less certainty than its own strongest advocates do, not a fourth position in its own right.

**Does "swear not at all" (5:34) forbid every oath, including a courtroom oath?** A narrower dispute, but a real and long-running one with its own practical consequence (court testimony, military and civic oaths, ordination vows). The Wikipedia article on Matthew 5:34 used here reports Quakers and Mennonites as the most consistent modern representatives of a literal reading: "Quakers and Mennonites firmly reject all oaths," a position that "has led to their persecution by governments that insist on oath taking" and has produced the legal alternative of an affirmation in place of an oath. The same article reports Leo Tolstoy reading the verse the same way, to the point that it "led him to support the abolition of all courts." Against this, the article reports John Calvin arguing "that only oaths counter to God are wrong" -- a conditional reading that most Christian traditions have followed. The Westminster Confession of Faith (chapter 22, read directly for this lesson) states this conditional reading in its own terms: "A lawful oath is part of religious worship, wherein, upon just occasion, the person swearing solemnly calleth God to witness," adding that such an oath "is warranted by the Word of God, under the new testament as well as under the old" (22.1-2). The Catholic Church's Catechism (§§2153-2154, read directly for this lesson) reaches a similar conclusion from a different document: quoting Matthew 5:34 itself ("you shall not swear falsely... But I say to you, Do not swear at all," §2153), it states that "the tradition of the Church has understood Jesus' words as not excluding oaths made for grave and right reasons (for example, in court)" (§2154). Both traditions permit what Quakers, Mennonites, and Tolstoy refuse outright. This lesson reports both sides without resolving which reads "not at all" correctly; Questions to Carry below asks what in the text's own wording would actually settle it.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage states in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. This is a model of the shape, not an answer key -- the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 5:23-24: "So if you are offering your gift at the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there before the altar. First go and be reconciled to your brother; then come and offer your gift" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the instruction interrupts a sacrifice already in progress -- the gift is already "at the altar" -- and requires leaving it there, unfinished, to go find the brother first. Inference: for a first-century audience bringing an actual animal or grain offering to the Jerusalem temple, this instruction carried a real cost (a trip away from the altar, possibly a delay of days if the brother was not nearby) and a real claim: that an unresolved wrong against another person stands in front of an act of formal worship, not beside it. The Context section above states the verse's own wording directly; this inference about the real cost such an interruption would have carried for an actual worshiper goes one step further than that cited wording and is labeled as this lesson's own step.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** An unreconciled wrong against another person is not a separate matter from one's standing before God that can be addressed later; the passage orders the two, putting reconciliation first, even at the cost of an interrupted act of worship.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the ordering itself -- that a known, specific, unresolved wrong against another person takes priority over continuing a planned act of worship or devotion -- and the detail that the text does not wait for the worshiper to feel ready, ascribe blame correctly, or finish the religious act first. Not carried forward: the specific first-century setting (a temple altar, an animal or grain offering) or any claim about how long a modern "interruption" must be to count as taking this seriously.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a command carried by direct analogy from the passage's own stated instruction ("leave your gift... first go"), not an inference stretched from a narrative example. Promise scope: none claimed; 5:23-24 states an instruction, not a promise about what will happen once reconciliation is attempted.

**Modern domain and situation.** Community volunteer leadership. A volunteer coordinator for a local nonprofit is scheduled to lead a Sunday-morning planning meeting for an upcoming fundraiser. The night before, she remembers an unresolved disagreement with a fellow volunteer from two weeks earlier -- a sharp exchange over how a previous event's budget was handled that neither of them has addressed since.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: a direct, named act of reconciliation attempted before resuming the shared task, not avoidance disguised as "staying professional" and not a public apology staged for others to see. One faithful response: before the meeting starts, she privately and specifically names what happened ("I was short with you about the budget numbers, and I don't think I handled that well") and asks to reset, rather than letting the meeting proceed as though nothing happened or waiting for the other volunteer to raise it first.

**Cautions.** This does not mean every planned task must be delayed until every relational tension is fully resolved -- some disagreements need more time, a third party, or professional mediation before a direct conversation would do more good than harm, and reconciliation is not always available on one party's timetable. It does not fit a situation involving real safety concerns, harassment, or an imbalance of power between the two people involved; that calls for a supervisor, a formal process, or another qualified person, not a private attempt at reconciliation modeled on this passage. The passage states an ordering principle for a specific case (an offering, a known wrong); the bridge above is an analogy the learner is testing, not a general rule that every task must stop for every disagreement.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Mark 10:11-12 and Luke 16:18 give Jesus's teaching on divorce and remarriage with no exception clause at all, while Matthew 5:32 and 19:9 both include "except for sexual immorality." 4.
