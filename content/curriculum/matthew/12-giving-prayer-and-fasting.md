Matthew 6:1-18 opens a new section of the Sermon on the Mount while keeping its immediate predecessor's direct, second-person address running: Jesus is still telling "you" what righteousness is to look like, now turning from how the disciples treat other people (5:21-48) to how they practice three specific, named pieces of Jewish piety -- giving to the needy (6:1-4), prayer (6:5-15), and fasting (6:16-18). All three units are built on the same three-part shape, stated plainly enough in the text's own repeated words to check directly: a warning against doing the practice "to be seen by men," an instruction to do it in a way only God can see, and a promise that "your Father, who sees what is done in secret, will reward you" (BSB, repeated at 6:4, 6:6, and 6:18). Inside the middle unit sits the single most memorized text this curriculum has reached so far, the Lord's Prayer (6:9-13) -- and this lesson does not draft its wording from memory or from any other translation; every quotation below was checked directly against this app's own shipped BSB file before a word of this lesson was written. That discipline matters more here than almost anywhere else in this curriculum, because the prayer's most familiar English wording in wide cultural memory is the KJV's, not the BSB's, and because the prayer's own closing line raises a genuine, documented textual-criticism question that this lesson reports rather than silently resolving either way.

## Context

**What the text says, in order (6:1-18).** A general warning opens the unit: "Be careful not to perform your righteous acts before men to be seen by them. If you do, you will have no reward from your Father in heaven" (6:1, BSB). Giving (6:2-4): do not "sound a trumpet before you, as the hypocrites do in the synagogues and on the streets, to be honored by men" (6:2, BSB); instead, "do not let your left hand know what your right hand is doing, so that your giving may be in secret" (6:3-4, BSB). Prayer (6:5-15) runs longest: a warning against praying "standing in the synagogues and on the street corners to be seen by men" (6:5, BSB); an instruction to "go into your inner room, shut your door, and pray to your Father, who is unseen" (6:6, BSB); a further warning against pagan "babble" (6:7, BSB); the Lord's Prayer itself (6:9-13); and a two-verse coda on forgiveness (6:14-15) that the Context notes below treat separately, since it uses different English wording than verse 12. Fasting (6:16-18) closes the unit on the same pattern: hypocrites "disfigure their faces to show men they are fasting" (6:16, BSB), while the disciple is told to "anoint your head and wash your face, so that your fasting will not be obvious to men, but only to your Father" (6:17-18, BSB).

**Three named pillars of piety, addressed in a fixed order.** The Wikipedia article on Matthew 6 (used here) states that verses 1-18 "address the three most important outward expressions of Jewish piety, almsgiving, prayer, and fasting" -- a plain description of the unit's own content, checkable against the text's own three headings (giving, prayer, fasting) in the order Matthew prints them. The same article reports Dale Allison's reading of this section's relationship to what comes immediately before it: 5:21-48 states "what to do," and 6:1-18 is "a sort of commentary" on it, functioning as a short "cult-didache" that teaches "how to do it." This lesson states Allison's own framing as his framing, not as a settled fact about Matthew's compositional intent.

**"Hypocrites" names a role, not just a character flaw.** The Greek word behind "hypocrites" (6:2, 6:5, 6:16, BSB) has a theatrical background: the Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:2 (used here) reports that in Classical Greek a hypocrite was "an actor who pretended to be another person on stage," and quotes Isidore's explanation that the term is "derived from the appearance of those who in the shows are disguised in masks," and Augustine's comparison of such people to an actor playing Agamemnon, who is not Agamemnon but "feigns to be so." The word's background does not by itself settle how literally Matthew's hypocrites are being compared to stage actors; it is reported here as a checkable fact about the word's own history.

**Whether "sound a trumpet" (6:2) names a real practice is itself disputed, before any theological question is reached.** The same article reports that "there is no evidence that the Pharisees... actually blew upon trumpets to publicize their giving," and that this absence of corroborating evidence has produced at least four distinct named proposals, reported fully in Positions below.

**The prayer's own address and structure (6:9-13) are built around two matching triads, with one real grammatical tension inside them.** "So then, this is how you should pray: ‘Our Father in heaven, hallowed be Your name. Your kingdom come, Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven" (6:9-10, BSB; the BSB's own opening quotation mark before "Our Father" is not closed until 6:13) states three petitions addressed to God ("Your name... Your kingdom... Your will") before the prayer turns to three petitions addressed to human need: "Give us this day our daily bread... forgive us our debts... lead us not into temptation" (6:11-13, BSB). The Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:9 (used here) reports Green's observation that the hallowing of God's name is "deliberately the first among the three petitions in the prayer," confirming the three-petition count for the "Your" half directly. The Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:10 (used here) notes a further, separate ambiguity: "in the original Greek the phrase 'in earth, as it is in heaven' is... uncertain whether this phrase is intended to only modify the last petition, or all three" -- a live question about scope within that first triad, not a dispute this lesson resolves. A further, checkable tension sits between this prayer's own grammar and the private instruction that introduces it: verse 6 tells the disciple, in the singular, to "go into your inner room, shut your door, and pray" privately, while the prayer Jesus then gives, starting at verse 9, addresses God throughout in the plural -- "Our Father," "give us," "forgive us," "lead us." The same Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:9 reports France's reading that the plural pronoun points to communal rather than strictly private prayer. This lesson states the tension (a privately-addressed instruction followed by a communally-worded model prayer) as a plain textual observation, without resolving what it implies about how the prayer was meant to be used.

**Two different English words render two different Greek words across verses 12 and 14-15.** "Forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors" (6:12, BSB) uses "debts" and "debtors"; two verses later, "if you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you" (6:14, BSB) uses "trespasses" instead. The Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:14-15 (used here) states plainly that "these verses parallel Matthew 6:12, but while that one speaks of debts this one speaks of trespasses" -- naming this as a real difference in the underlying Greek vocabulary, not simply a stylistic variation an English reader is free to treat as interchangeable. The same article reports Dale Allison noting "a similar sequence in Mark 11:23-25 and Luke 17:3-6" tying prayer to interpersonal forgiveness, and Michael David Coogan's observation that "reciprocity is vital in the economy of God" in this passage. Separately, the Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:12 (used here) reports Luz's observation that this verse is unique in "so closely relating the two notions" of divine and human forgiveness, and notes that "the Aramaic word for debts could also mean sins or errors" -- background relevant to why Luke's parallel (see Connections, below) renders the same petition with a different English word again.

**The doxology most English readers expect to hear is not in this app's own shipped text at 6:13.** The Lord's Prayer as given in the BSB ends: "And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one" (6:13, BSB) -- checked directly against `web/public/bible/BSB/40.json`. There is no "for Yours is the kingdom and the power and the glory forever, Amen" in the BSB's own rendering of this verse. This is a plain, checkable fact about what this app's Bible text contains; it is not, by itself, a judgment about which textual tradition is right, which is a real, named dispute reported fully in Positions below.

**"Disfigure their faces" (6:16) names an ancient fasting practice, not a vague gesture.** The Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:16 (used here) reports that the underlying Greek word (aphanizo, literally "make invisible") most likely refers to "putting ash on the face to darken one's complexion" together with leaving the face unwashed and the hair untrimmed, so that onlookers would recognize the fast. The same article reports a narrower disagreement over how to translate the word itself: Jerome argued it should be rendered "destroy" rather than "externalize," while Pseudo-Chrysostom read the practice as outright theatrical performance of sorrow the hypocrite does not actually feel. The underlying historical practice (ash, neglect of normal grooming) is not itself disputed by these sources; only the sharpest English rendering of the verb is.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 6:1-18 is built, not on what that construction is meant to teach.

**A refrain repeated verbatim across all three units.** "Truly I tell you, they already have their full reward" closes the warning half of the giving unit (6:2, BSB), the prayer unit (6:5, BSB), and the fasting unit (6:16, BSB) -- the identical clause, word for word, in all three places. A second refrain closes the instruction half of all three units just as exactly: "your Father, who sees what is done in secret, will reward you" (6:4, 6:6, 6:18, BSB, each with only the minor grammatical adjustments required by context). These two refrains are what make the three-part shape a directly checkable textual feature of 6:1-18, rather than merely this lesson's own paraphrase of a shared theme.

**Each unit's warning, contrast, and promise occupy the same three slots in the same order.** Giving: warning (6:2) / secret practice (6:3-4a) / promise (6:4b). Prayer: warning (6:5) / secret practice (6:6a) / promise (6:6b) -- with a second, separate warning-and-correction pair inserted afterward (6:7-8) before the model prayer itself (6:9-13) and the forgiveness coda (6:14-15). Fasting: warning (6:16) / secret practice (6:17) / promise (6:18). The prayer unit is the only one of the three that is interrupted by extra material between its warning-and-secret-practice pair and anything resembling a promise; the Lord's Prayer and the forgiveness coda occupy the space where the other two units simply state the promise and move on.

**The prayer's own two triads are themselves parallel in form to the giving and fasting units' warning/contrast structure.** Three petitions address God's own concerns first (name, kingdom, will, 6:9-10); three address human need second (bread, forgiveness, deliverance, 6:11-13) -- a God-first-then-us ordering that mirrors, on a smaller scale within the prayer itself, the larger unit's own move from warning (about human-facing display) to the Father "who sees... in secret." Whether this nested parallel was a deliberate compositional choice is not something this lesson's own observation of the pattern can establish on its own.

## Positions

Four separate, named disputes attach to this passage. Each is reported here in terms its own participants use, not resolved.

**Does "sound a trumpet" (6:2) describe a real practice, or something else?** No source consulted for this lesson reports direct archaeological or documentary evidence of almsgivers literally blowing trumpets in First Temple or Second Temple Judea, and four different named proposals fill that gap. Harold Fowler argues it is "unlikely they would have been so brazen" as to actually sound a trumpet, reading the line as rhetorical exaggeration rather than a report of an observed practice. Jack Lewis proposes a possible connection to "autumn public fasts... accompanied by the blowing of horns," a different and more specific ritual occasion than private almsgiving, though the Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:2 (used here) reports this connection as itself contested. Lachs considers literal trumpet-blowing "improbable" and proposes instead that "trumpet" may be a transcription error for a similar-sounding Hebrew word referring to "sophar chests" (collection boxes for alms), making the image a case of textual confusion rather than an ancient practice at all. Davies and Allison reject that specific mistranslation theory while allowing that Matthew "could be making a pun based on the two similar words" -- a middle position that keeps "trumpet" as Matthew's own deliberate word choice without requiring that real trumpets were ever actually sounded.

**What is "babble like pagans" (6:7) actually criticizing -- length, repetition, or something else?** The underlying Greek word (battalogein) "is unknown outside this verse, appearing in no other literature contemporaneous with the text," per the Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:7 (used here), which leaves its precise sense to context and named scholarly argument rather than independent attestation. France reads the criticism as targeting a ritual format: in the Greco-Roman world, "Gentile prayer was portrayed as repeated incantations that had to be perfectly recited, but where the spirit and understanding of the prayer were secondary." Fowler reads it similarly, as a critique of sheer verbal quantity: "the Jews believed the pagans needed to incessantly repeat their prayers, as their gods would not answer them" otherwise. Schweizer argues against both of these specifically: he "does not feel battalogein is a reference to repetition, but to nonsense," holding that the Jews of that era believed pagans "had forgotten the true name of God," so that their prayers were filled with "long lists of meaningless words" rather than merely repeated ones. This is a genuine disagreement about what kind of defect is being named -- too much repetition of real content, versus content that was never coherent to begin with -- not a dispute over whether the verse criticizes something.

**Does the doxology belong to the earliest recoverable text of Matthew 6:13?** This is the passage's most consequential textual-critical question, and this lesson reports both a minority and a majority position by name.

One position, reported through James Snapp Jr.'s blog (read directly for this lesson), which in turn cites manuscript data compiled by Jonathan Borland, argues for the doxology's authenticity on manuscript-count grounds: Borland's count finds the doxology present in roughly 98.5-98.6% of all Greek manuscripts of this verse, and reports that "all of the manuscripts from the 900s and earlier that have the phrase contain the doxology completely intact, letter for letter." This position also appeals to the Didache, an early Christian document (commonly dated to the early-to-mid 100s) that attests a related doxology form in its own version of the Lord's Prayer, which Snapp's post treats as "an extremely weighty witness for the inclusion of the doxology in the original text of Matthew 6:13" given its early date. The Didache's own text was not independently read for this lesson; this report rests on Snapp's characterization of it, flagged further in Sources and Limits.

The majority position among modern textual critics, reported through the Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:13 (used here), holds the opposite: "it is absent from the oldest and best manuscripts of Matthew, and most scholars do not consider it part of the original text" (citing Nicholas Ayo). The same article reports R. T. France's observation that "the doxology appears in at least ten different forms in early texts before becoming standardized" -- read, on this view, as evidence that the wording was still unsettled and growing rather than fixed from the start. David Hill proposes the doxology may derive from 1 Chronicles 29:11, and Charles Ellicott raises the possibility that it was "interpolated by transcribers to make the text... harmonise with the liturgies" used in congregational worship. On manuscript evidence specifically, the two positions are not actually in tension about which individual manuscripts contain or lack the doxology -- both report that early Alexandrian witnesses (Sinaiticus and Vaticanus) and other early witnesses lack it while the great majority of later Greek manuscripts have it. What divides them is how to weigh a large body of later, uniform manuscripts against a smaller number of earlier, non-uniform ones -- a real methodological disagreement in New Testament textual criticism generally, not unique to this verse.

This app's own shipped BSB text has, as a matter of plain fact, taken the majority-modern-scholarship side: it omits the doxology at 6:13, matching the ESV, NIV, NLT, and most other widely used modern translations (per the same Wikipedia article's own listing), against the NKJV and 21st Century King James Version, which retain it.

**Which Gospel's version of the Lord's Prayer is closer to its earliest form, and which direction did the other one change?** The prayer appears in both Matthew (6:9-13) and Luke (11:2-4) but not in Mark, which the Wikipedia article on the Lord's Prayer (used here) reports has led two-source-hypothesis scholars to trace both versions to a shared source (Q) behind Matthew and Luke. Even granting a shared source, a further and separate disagreement exists about which Gospel's own wording sits closer to it. Joachim Jeremias holds that "in the early period, before wordings were fixed, liturgical texts were elaborated, expanded and enriched" -- on this reading, Matthew's longer form grew out of a shorter original closer to Luke's own wording. Michael Goulder, Thomas J. Mosbo, and Ken Olson hold the opposite: they "see the shorter Lucan version as a reworking of the Matthaean text, removing unnecessary verbiage and repetition" -- on this reading, Luke is the one who changed an already-fuller original. This lesson reports both directions without choosing one; see the connection to Luke 11:2-4 below for the specific wording differences this dispute is about.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage states in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. This is a model of the shape, not an answer key -- the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 6:3-4: "But when you give to the needy, do not let your left hand know what your right hand is doing, so that your giving may be in secret. And your Father, who sees what is done in secret, will reward you" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the saying imagines a single person's own two hands as if they could keep secrets from each other -- a deliberately impossible-sounding image, since hands do not have separate minds, used to press a point harder than a simple "do not tell anyone" would. Inference: for the saying's own force to land, the point is not only that other people should not know about the gift; it is that the giver's own internal accounting of the gift -- the mental tally of having been generous -- is itself what the image targets, since the left hand standing for the giver's own awareness has to stay as uninformed as the actual bystanders the warning in 6:2 names. This inference goes one step beyond the plain wording the Context section quotes directly, and is labeled here as this lesson's own step.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** A generous act's own stated purpose (directing attention to the Father who rewards it, not to the giver) is undermined the moment the giver begins keeping score of their own generosity for their own benefit, whether that scorekeeping is shown to others or kept entirely to oneself.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the refusal to let an act of generosity become raw material for the giver's own self-regard, whether displayed publicly or rehearsed privately. Not carried forward: the specific left-hand/right-hand image, or any claim that anonymous giving is inherently superior to acknowledged giving in every circumstance -- receipts, tax documentation, and organizational accountability are not what this verse addresses.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy from a vivid, rhetorically pressed image, not a procedural rule about anonymity as such. Promise scope: 6:4 states a reward from the Father in the text's own words, but does not specify its timing, nature, or amount; nothing here warrants predicting a specific present-life outcome for a specific act of secret giving.

**Modern domain and situation.** Nonprofit board service. A board member has quietly covered a shortfall in the year-end fundraising goal from personal funds. Staff later draft a public thank-you email to the full donor list and ask whether they may name this board member as the one who "saved the year-end campaign."

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: declining a public credit claim for the gift without concealing it from anyone with a legitimate need to know (the organization's own financial records, its auditors). One faithful response: the board member asks staff not to name them publicly in the donor email, while ensuring the gift is recorded accurately and completely in the nonprofit's internal books, since accurate bookkeeping is a different thing from public credit.

**Cautions.** This does not mean financial transparency itself is wrong: nonprofits have real legal and fiduciary duties to record every gift accurately, and the "secrecy" in view at 6:3-4 is about self-promotion, not about hiding a transaction from an organization's own accounting. It also does not mean every public acknowledgment of a gift violates this text -- a donor who permits their name on a building or program without having sought or engineered that recognition is in a different position than the hypocrites of 6:2, who actively "sound a trumpet" to produce their own praise. The passage is not a nondisclosure policy; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing, and a different reader may find it does not hold for their own case.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. France and Fowler read "babble like pagans" (6:7) as a criticism of repetition and sheer verbal quantity; Schweizer reads the same word as a criticism of incoherent content, not repetition at all. What would you need to see -- about how battalogein or related words are used elsewhere, or about what first-century pagan prayer actually sounded like -- to judge between a "too much repetition" reading and a "meaningless content" reading, rather than letting each reader pick whichever fits their own assumptions about ancient paganism?
2. The doxology dispute at 6:13 turns partly on how to weigh a large body of later, uniform Greek manuscripts (which overwhelmingly include it) against a smaller number of earlier, non-uniform witnesses (which lack it, in several different forms where it does appear at all). What general principle would you need to hold about how New Testament textual criticism should weigh manuscript age against manuscript quantity, before you could say which side's weighing is more defensible here specifically -- and would that same principle, applied consistently, change your view of any other disputed verse you already have an opinion about?
3. Lachs explains "sound a trumpet" (6:2) as a possible transcription error for a different Hebrew word; Davies and Allison accept the same two similar-sounding words as evidence of a deliberate pun instead. Both readings can explain the same two facts (the odd specificity of "trumpet" and the existence of a similar-sounding alternate word) without contradicting each other on the facts themselves. What would distinguish a textual-history explanation (a word got confused in transmission) from an authorial-wordplay explanation (Matthew chose the word on purpose for its sound), rather than leaving the choice to each reader's own assumption about how carefully Matthew composed this verse?
4. Jeremias reads Matthew's longer Lord's Prayer as a liturgical expansion of something closer to Luke's shorter version; Goulder, Mosbo, and Olson read Luke's shorter version as a compression of something closer to Matthew's longer one. If you could only examine one kind of evidence to decide between these two directions -- the Didache's own version of the prayer, other known examples of how early Christian communities handled liturgical texts, or something else entirely -- what would you choose, and what result would actually move you off your current guess?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) state in order what 6:1-18 says happens or is instructed, unit by unit, before saying anything about what any of it means for Christian practice today. (b) Name the two refrains that repeat word for word across all three units (the "full reward" warning and the "sees what is done in secret" promise), and say at which verses each one falls. (c) Recite the Lord's Prayer's own six petitions in order (three addressed to God, three addressed to human need), without adding or removing anything from the BSB's own wording at 6:9-13 -- including without adding a doxology the BSB itself does not print there.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the plain three-part shape across giving, prayer, and fasting, and the two verbatim refrains that make it checkable (see Literary Design); (1:00-2:00) the Lord's Prayer's own two-triad structure and the private-instruction/communal-wording tension at verses 6 and 9 (see Context); (2:00-3:00) the doxology question at 6:13 -- state plainly that the BSB omits it, then name at least one scholar on each side of why (see Positions); (3:00-4:00) one more of the three remaining named disputes (the trumpet, the babbling, or the Matthew/Luke direction question), naming at least two participants in their own terms; (4:00-5:00) one open question from above, stated on purpose as the last thing taught.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first; the point of this prompt is that you do the forming, not that you pick from a list. If you are stuck, here is one worked candidate, stated at full strength: reporting two sides of the doxology question without choosing one is itself evasive -- either the BSB's own wording is trustworthy at 6:13 or it is missing something Jesus actually said, and a study lesson that will not say which is quietly teaching that it does not matter whether a Bible translation is complete. Before you answer it, state what this lesson's own Positions section already does: it names specific manuscripts and specific scholars on both sides (not an unnamed "some scholars"), it reports that the *disagreement* on dating and manuscript quality is a real, open question in the field of textual criticism generally, and it states as a separate, plain fact (not an opinion) that the BSB has sided with the majority of modern English translations in omitting the doxology -- which is a different claim from saying the omission is beyond dispute. A second worked candidate, running the other direction: if 98.5% of all Greek manuscripts include the doxology, is a methodology that systematically prefers a smaller number of older manuscripts over an overwhelming later majority really more reliable, or does it just prefer "older" as a tiebreaker for reasons that themselves deserve scrutiny?
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 6:1-18 does not, in its own wording, specify how publicly a good deed, a prayer, or a fast may become known before it counts as having been done "to be seen by men" -- the text states the wrong motive and the right one, not a measurable threshold of visibility that separates them. (b) The Lord's Prayer's own text (6:9-13) does not settle, by itself, whether Jesus intended it as a fixed liturgical formula to be recited word for word or as a model pattern for a disciple's own prayer in different words; both readings are compatible with "this is how you should pray" (6:9, BSB), and the verse does not choose between them.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection from this passage and defend it: name its type, its evidence label, and point to the words in both texts that support that grade.
