Matthew 8:18-34 is one journey with three stops. Jesus sees the crowd and "gave orders to cross to the other side of the sea" (8:18, BSB). Before the boat leaves, two men offer to follow him and get answers that name the cost (8:19-22). On the water, a storm nearly swamps the boat while he sleeps, and he stills it with a rebuke (8:23-27). On the far shore, two violent demon-possessed men meet him, the demons are sent into a herd of pigs, and the town asks him to leave (8:28-34). The next verse closes the loop: "Jesus got into a boat, crossed over, and came to His own town" (9:1, BSB). This lesson works on three questions. First, what the text says, checked against this app's own BSB wording and against Mark's and Luke's tellings of the same events. Second, where Old Testament threads are visible, and how strong each one is. Third, where named interpreters disagree: over what "the Son of Man" means at 8:20, over the hard saying "let the dead bury their own dead" at 8:22, and over why Matthew has two demoniacs where Mark and Luke have one. The lesson reports those disagreements and does not settle them.

## Context

**What happens, in order.** A scribe says, "Teacher, I will follow You wherever You go" (8:19, BSB). Jesus answers, "Foxes have dens and birds of the air have nests, but the Son of Man has no place to lay His head" (8:20, BSB). Another disciple asks, "Lord, first let me go and bury my father" (8:21, BSB), and Jesus says, "Follow Me, and let the dead bury their own dead" (8:22, BSB). The narrator then says, "When He got into the boat, His disciples followed Him" (8:23, BSB). "Suddenly a violent storm came up on the sea, so that the boat was engulfed by the waves; but Jesus was sleeping" (8:24, BSB). The disciples wake him: "Lord, save us! We are perishing!" (8:25, BSB). He answers, "You of little faith... why are you so afraid?" and only then "rebuked the winds and the sea, and it was perfectly calm" (8:26, BSB). "The men were amazed and asked, 'What kind of man is this? Even the winds and the sea obey Him!'" (8:27, BSB). On the other side, "in the region of the Gadarenes," Jesus meets "two demon-possessed men coming from the tombs," so violent "that no one could pass that way" (8:28, BSB). They shout, "What do You want with us, Son of God?" and "Have You come here to torture us before the appointed time?" (8:29, BSB). The demons ask, "If You drive us out, send us into the herd of pigs" (8:31, BSB). Jesus says one word, "Go!", and "the whole herd rushed down the steep bank into the sea and died in the waters" (8:32, BSB). The herdsmen report everything, "including the account of the demon-possessed men" (8:33, BSB), and the townspeople "begged Him to leave their region" (8:34, BSB).

**"The Son of Man" appears here for the first time in Matthew.** A search of this app's shipped BSB text of Matthew finds the phrase "Son of Man" first at 8:20, and 29 times in the whole Gospel. What the phrase means is disputed by named scholars. Positions below reports that dispute.

**Two would-be followers, and one hard saying.** Matthew's two speakers are described differently. The first is "one of the scribes" (8:19, BSB), and the second is introduced as "Another of His disciples" (8:21, BSB). The hard saying at 8:22 is reported in Positions below. One thing is plain on the page: the burial request is about a father, and the reply does not tell the man to do something else first. It tells him to follow now.

**Matthew's storm against Mark's and Luke's, checked in the shipped text.** All three Gospels tell the storm, and the differences are small but real. In Matthew the disciples say, "Lord, save us! We are perishing!" (8:25). In Mark they say, "Teacher, don't You care that we are perishing?" (Mark 4:38, BSB). In Luke they say, "Master, Master, we are perishing!" (Luke 8:24, BSB). The order of events differs too. In Matthew, Jesus questions the disciples' faith *before* he calms the sea (8:26). In Mark he asks, "Why are you so afraid?... Do you still have no faith?" *after* the calm (Mark 4:39-40, BSB), and in Luke he asks, "Where is your faith?" after the calm as well (Luke 8:24-25, BSB). Matthew's "You of little faith" is a phrase this Gospel uses again in its second storm, at 14:31. Augustine, in his *Harmony of the Gospels* (II.24), set the three wordings side by side and proposed that "the whole utterance may perhaps have gone thus: Why are you fearful? Where is your faith, O you of little faith?" with each evangelist recording one part. That is one named way of reading the differences, reported here without endorsing it.

**Gadarenes, Gerasenes, Gergesenes: a real textual and geographic question.** This app's BSB reads "the region of the Gadarenes" at Matthew 8:28 and "the region of the Gerasenes" at both Mark 5:1 and Luke 8:26. The manuscripts do not agree. A list of variants compiled from modern critical editions (Wikipedia's "Textual variants in the Gospel of Matthew," a tertiary source) gives three main readings at Matthew 8:28. "Gadarenes" is read by Codex Vaticanus (B), Codex Ephraemi (C), Codex Koridethi (Θ), and Syriac versions. "Gergesenes" is read by a corrector of Sinaiticus, the majority of later manuscripts, and Origen; it is also the King James Version's reading. "Gerasenes" is read by Latin and some Coptic witnesses. The first hand of Sinaiticus has a fourth spelling, "Gazarenes." Origen explains, in his own words, why he preferred "Gergesa." In his *Commentary on John* (VI.24, read here in the 1896 Menzies translation) he writes that "Gerasa is a town of Arabia, and has near it neither sea nor lake," that "in a few copies we have found, into the country of the Gadarenes," and that "Gergesa, from which the name Gergesenes is taken, is an old town in the neighbourhood of the lake now called Tiberias, and on the edge of it there is a steep place abutting on the lake." The Wikipedia article on this miracle gives the distances: Gadara is about 10 kilometres from the lake, Gerasa "well over twice as far," and the identification with a lakeside site at Kursi is disputed. These are the facts as reported. This lesson does not decide which reading Matthew originally wrote or exactly where the event took place.

**Two men in Matthew, one in Mark and Luke.** Checked in the shipped text, Matthew has "two demon-possessed men" (8:28) who speak in the plural: "What do You want with us" (8:29). Mark has "a man with an unclean spirit" (Mark 5:2, BSB), who says, "What do You want with me, Jesus, Son of the Most High God? I beg You before God not to torture me!" (Mark 5:7, BSB) and gives his name as Legion, "for we are many" (Mark 5:9, BSB). Luke also has one "demon-possessed man from the town" (Luke 8:27, BSB). Matthew's account is much shorter. It has no name "Legion," no chains, no scene of the healed man "clothed and in his right mind," and no commission to go home and tell, all of which Mark includes (Mark 5:3-5, 5:15, 5:19-20). Named explanations of the difference in number are reported in Positions below.

**The phrase "before the appointed time" (8:29) is Matthew's alone.** In Matthew the demons ask, "Have You come here to torture us before the appointed time?" (8:29, BSB). Mark's version has no time reference ("I beg You before God not to torture me!", Mark 5:7). Luke's has none either ("I beg You not to torture me!", Luke 8:28, BSB), though Luke adds that the demons begged Jesus "not to order them to go into the Abyss" (Luke 8:31, BSB). So in Matthew alone, the demons speak as if they expect a judgment at a fixed future time, and as if Jesus' arrival has come early. One manuscript tradition varies the wording. The first hand of Codex Sinaiticus is reported to read "destroy us before our time" (Wikipedia's variant list, tertiary). Two older readers took the phrase to point to final judgment. Wikipedia's "Matthew 8:29" article, citing Robert Witham (1730), reports the belief that "the appointed time" was "thought to be Judgement day," with reference to 2 Peter 2 and Jude. Augustine, as excerpted in the Catena Aurea, offered three possibilities, the last being "because this was before the day of judgment, when they should be punished with eternal damnation." That the phrase looks ahead to a future judgment is a plain observation from the text. How it fits a larger picture of the end is beyond what this lesson takes up.

**A note on "demon-possessed."** This passage describes demonic possession in the terms of its own setting, and this lesson reports what the text says without offering a diagnosis or a theory of possession. Some readers will recognize, in Mark's fuller account of a man "cutting himself with stones" (Mark 5:5, BSB), something close to what they or someone they love has lived through. If that is you, a study note is the wrong place to turn. Please contact a doctor, a counselor, or a pastor who knows you. In the United States you can call or text 988 (the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline) at any hour, and anywhere you can call your local emergency number if anyone is in immediate danger.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 8:18-34 is built, not on what the construction is meant to teach.

**A round trip, framed by crowds and a town.** The unit opens with Jesus seeing "a large crowd" and ordering the crossing (8:18). It closes with "the whole town" coming out and begging him to leave (8:34), and the next verse brings him back across (9:1). A crowd on one shore and a town that asks him to go on the other frame the journey. That is a checkable feature of where Matthew places these lines. What, if anything, Matthew intends by the contrast is not stated.

**"Follow" carries over from the conversation into the boat.** The verb sits in three consecutive verses: the scribe's "I will follow You wherever You go" (8:19), Jesus' "Follow Me" (8:22), and then the narrator's "His disciples followed Him" (8:23), straight into the boat where the storm will hit. Matthew places the storm immediately after two conversations about the cost of following, and the disciples who "followed Him" are the ones in danger. vanThanh Nguyen (*Asian American Theological Forum*, 2025) notes the arrangement from the other side: where Luke sets these sayings on a road toward Jerusalem, "Matthew's sayings serve as the introduction of the miraculous stilling of the storm."

**A question, then an answer from the other shore.** The men in the boat ask, "What kind of man is this?" (8:27). The very next speakers in Matthew's text, the demons, address him as "Son of God" (8:29). Matthew does not link the two, but they sit side by side on the page. John Chrysostom, as excerpted in the Catena Aurea, made exactly this connection: "Because there were who thought Christ to be a man, therefore the daemons came to proclaim His divinity, that they who had not seen the sea raging and again still, might hear the daemons crying." Matthew's second storm story ends with a more direct answer from inside the boat: "Truly You are the Son of God!" (14:33, BSB). Comparing the two storms is left as an open question below.

**The question in 8:27, and where an Old Testament reader's mind would go.** "What kind of man is this? Even the winds and the sea obey Him!" (8:27, BSB). In Israel's Scriptures, commanding the sea is something said of God. "You rule the raging sea; when its waves mount up, You still them" (Psalm 89:9, BSB). "You stilled the roaring of the seas, the pounding of their waves" (Psalm 65:7, BSB). And in the psalm whose sequence most resembles this scene (sea, storm, cry, calm), "He calmed the storm to a whisper, and the waves of the sea were hushed" (Psalm 107:29, BSB). This is an observation about what an Old Testament-literate reader would have had in mind when hearing the disciples' question, not a verdict about what Matthew means by it. Matthew does not cite any of these psalms here. The connection to Psalm 107 is graded below as an allusion at a plausible level, with the reasons stated.

## Positions

Three named disputes attach to this passage. None is settled here.

**What does "the Son of Man" mean at 8:20?** The phrase's meaning on Jesus' lips is one of the oldest open questions in Gospel study. The Wikipedia article "Son of man (Christianity)" (tertiary) reports that James D. G. Dunn and Delbert Burkett both say that "after 150 years of debate, no consensus on its meaning has emerged," and quotes Reginald H. Fuller calling it "a can of worms." Two positions bear directly on this verse.

One position reads the phrase as a title drawn from Daniel 7:13-14, where Daniel sees "One like the Son of Man coming with the clouds of heaven" who is "given dominion, glory, and kingship" (BSB). Craig Blomberg (*Jesus and the Gospels*, 2009, read here only as excerpted on a Redemption Seminary lecture page) writes that "Daniel 7:13 probably provides more directly relevant background" and that "'Son of Man' winds up being a very exalted title for Jesus." He addresses this very saying, with a concession: "Even in the sayings that seem least dependent on a Danielic background, hints of either suffering or exaltation seem present. For example, when the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head, the ignominy of Jesus' itinerant ministry is highlighted." Matthew's own later uses of the phrase do echo Daniel's wording: "coming on the clouds of heaven" (24:30 and 26:64, BSB).

A second position, associated above all with Geza Vermes, reads the phrase through Aramaic idiom. Philip Alexander and Martin Goodman's British Academy memoir of Vermes (2020) summarizes his 1967 argument: "In the Aramaic of Jesus, Vermes argued, the term bar nash/bar nasha simply meant a human being, but in certain circumstances could be used as a circumlocution for the first person pronoun 'I'." They add that Vermes found the idiom "still clear in Jesus' sayings in the Gospels" in some cases, "whereas in other cases 'son of man', under the influence of Daniel 7, seems to be used as a title for a messianic figure." On this reading, 8:20 can be heard as a man speaking of his own homelessness in an ordinary Aramaic way, with no allusion to Daniel's heavenly figure.

These two positions disagree about what a first hearer of 8:20 would have heard. They do not have to disagree about the rest of Matthew, where the Danielic wording becomes explicit. One unreliable source is worth flagging. The Wikipedia article "Matthew 8:20" asserts that "among Christian scholars the consensus is that it is a reference to Daniel 7:13-14," but its only citation is the same Redemption Seminary page quoted above, and its sister article reports no consensus. This lesson does not repeat the "consensus" claim.

**What does "let the dead bury their own dead" (8:22) mean?** Bockmuehl, as quoted by Nguyen, calls it a saying with hardly an equal: "There is hardly one logion of Jesus which more sharply runs counter to law, piety and custom." Four named readings are reported here, all through secondary sources (see Sources and Limits).

*The "spiritually dead" reading.* Let those who are spiritually dead bury the physically dead. Augustine, as excerpted in the Catena Aurea, puts it this way: "Thy father is dead; but there are also other dead who shall bury their dead, because they are in unbelief." Gordon Franz (Associates for Biblical Research, 2009) reports that Joseph Fitzmyer calls this "the 'majority interpretation'." Cornelius a Lapide, reported by Wikipedia, adds that "Christ does not intend to condemn the burial of the dead" and reads the saying as teaching that God's summons comes before every human one. Chrysostom, in the Catena, makes a related point: the saying "does not condemn natural affection to our parents, but shows that nothing ought to be more binding on us than the business of heaven."

*A deliberate break with law and custom.* Nguyen cites Martin Hengel's *The Charismatic Leader and His Followers* (1981) for "an interpretation that emphasizes a break with law and custom," and Franz cites E. P. Sanders (1985) among "critical scholars" who "suggest that Jesus was encouraging His disciples to break the fifth commandment." On this reading the saying's offense is the point.

*A Nazirite-style dedication.* Nguyen reports that Markus Bockmuehl (*Journal of Theological Studies*, 1998) points to the Torah's own two exceptions to the duty of burying a parent, the high priest and the Nazirite (Leviticus 21:11-12; Numbers 6:6). On Nguyen's summary of Bockmuehl, the saying is "not a deliberate attack on the Torah as suggested by Martin Hengel," and a religious duty of dedication instead takes precedence over the family obligation.

*Secondary burial.* Byron McCane (*Harvard Theological Review*, 1990, reported by both Franz and Nguyen) argues that the request concerns not the first burial but the reburial of the bones in an ossuary about a year after death. Franz summarizes: "When Jesus stated: 'Let the dead bury their own dead,' He was referring to two different kinds of dead in the tomb." Franz also reports McCane's objection to the majority reading: the text says "their own dead," so both uses of "dead" refer to the physically dead. Nguyen accepts the secondary-burial setting and extends it in his own direction, arguing that the practice served to preserve an elite family's social status and that the saying challenges that status.

These readings differ on whether the father has just died or was buried months ago, on whether "dead" means the same thing both times, and on whether the saying overrides the Torah, applies one of the Torah's own exceptions, or does neither. They agree that Jesus' call comes first. This lesson does not choose among them. One pastoral word belongs here. None of these readings claims the verse tells anyone today to skip a parent's funeral. A reader who is grieving a father or mother, or torn between a family duty and a sense of calling, needs a pastor who knows them, not a study note.

**Why two demoniacs in Matthew and one in Mark and Luke?** The difference is checkable (see Context). The explanations are not.

Augustine, in his *Harmony of the Gospels* (II.24, Salmond translation), proposed that "one of these parties was a person of some kind of superior notability and repute, whose case was particularly lamented by that district," so that Mark and Luke "judged it proper to make mention only of the one person, in connection with whom the fame of this deed had been spread abroad the more extensively." On this harmonization, both accounts are accurate: there were two men, and Mark and Luke name the better-known one.

Michael Licona, reported secondhand by the Wikipedia article on this miracle, places the difference within the literary habits of ancient biography. He observes that Matthew "shortens the story dramatically... as it frequently abbreviates stories from Mark, and writes of two men." The same paragraph goes on to say that the variations in the Synoptics "are typical of ancient historical biographies," without making clear whether that sentence is Licona's own claim or the article's. Licona's own wording and argument were not read for this lesson.

These two readings differ on whether the number "two" is a detail Mark and Luke left out or a feature of how Matthew retold Mark. This lesson reports both and does not decide between them. It also does not report a third, unnamed suggestion found on Wikipedia, that one man was "much more violent" than the other, because that article flags the suggestion as having no source.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key. Your own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 8:19-20: "Teacher, I will follow You wherever You go." Jesus replied, "Foxes have dens and birds of the air have nests, but the Son of Man has no place to lay His head" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the scribe makes an open-ended promise ("wherever You go"), and Jesus' reply neither accepts nor refuses it. He names what "wherever" will actually involve: no settled home. Inference: for this scribe, a man with a recognized place in his community, the reply invites him to count the cost before committing, not after. The text does not say how the scribe responded.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** An honest invitation names its real cost up front, and an honest commitment is made with that cost in view rather than in the first rush of enthusiasm.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a sincere, enthusiastic offer met with a plain statement of what it will really require. Not carried forward: homelessness as a requirement, Jesus' particular authority, or any claim that every enthusiastic offer is suspect. The scribe is not rebuked in the text.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a principle carried by analogy from a narrated exchange, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none. Nothing in 8:19-20 promises any outcome to someone who counts the cost first.

**Modern domain and situation.** Volunteering. A church member, moved by a moving appeal, tells the youth director on Sunday that she will lead the high-school group "for as long as you need me." The role actually means two evenings a week, a background check, weekend retreats, and at least a two-year commitment for continuity with the students.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: an honest naming of cost before a commitment is accepted. One faithful response: the director thanks her and, before saying yes, lays out the real schedule and duration, asks her to talk it over with her family for a week, and makes clear that a "no" or a smaller role is a welcome answer.

**Cautions.** This does not mean that every eager volunteer should be met with discouragement, or that commitments made quickly are always unwise. It also does not mean that a person's limits (health, caregiving, work) are a lack of faithfulness. The bridge is an analogy you are testing, not a rule for recruiting.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Matthew tells two storm stories, 8:23-27 and 14:22-33. The first ends with "What kind of man is this?" (8:27) and the second with "Truly You are the Son of God!" (14:33). Both include the words "You of little faith" (8:26; 14:31). What would you need to check in Matthew's own sequence between chapters 8 and 14 to decide whether the second scene is meant to answer the first, or whether they are simply two separate events told in a similar way?
2. Matthew places "Follow Me" (8:22) one verse before "His disciples followed Him" into the boat (8:23), while Luke sets the same sayings on a road. If Matthew's arrangement is deliberate, what does that suggest about how the storm should be read? And what evidence would distinguish a deliberate arrangement from an editor simply grouping material by theme?
3. The demons in Matthew expect a fixed "appointed time" (8:29), and the townspeople ask Jesus to leave even after two violent men are freed (8:34). Matthew does not say why the town reacted this way. What in the text (the herdsmen's report in 8:33, the loss of the herd) could support an answer, and where would you be supplying a motive the text does not state?
4. The scholars quoted in Positions disagree on what "the Son of Man" means at 8:20. If a reader had only Matthew 1-8 and had never seen Daniel 7, what would the phrase most naturally mean to them, and does it matter that Matthew's own later uses (24:30; 26:64) quote Daniel's "clouds of heaven"?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 8:18-34 in order (the two would-be followers, the storm, the two demoniacs and the pigs, the town's request), before saying what any of it means. (b) Quote, in the BSB's own words, the disciples' question at 8:27 and the demons' question about time at 8:29. (c) Name two checkable differences between Matthew's account of the demoniacs and Mark's.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the round trip from 8:18 to 9:1 and the "follow" link from 8:19-22 into 8:23 (see Literary Design); (1:00-2:00) the storm, with Matthew's order of rebuke-then-calm against Mark's and Luke's calm-then-rebuke (see Context); (2:00-3:00) the 8:27 question and the Old Testament texts where God stills the sea, presented as an observation, not a verdict (see Literary Design and the Psalm 107 connection); (3:00-4:00) Gadarenes, Gerasenes, Gergesenes, and two men against one, with Origen and Augustine as named voices (see Context and Positions); (4:00-5:00) one of the three Positions disputes, with at least two named sources.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: "Matthew has two demoniacs and Mark has one; Matthew says Gadarenes and Mark says Gerasenes. If the Gospels cannot agree on how many men or which town, why trust either account?" Before you answer, state what the lesson actually established: the differences are real and checkable; the manuscripts themselves vary on the place-name (with Origen arguing from geography in the third century); and named interpreters from Augustine to Licona have proposed different accounts of the numbers. Here is a second candidate, running the other way: "Calling the Psalm 107 and Jonah links only 'plausible' undersells them. The disciples' question in 8:27 makes sense only if Jesus is doing what Scripture says God alone does." To answer, separate what the text shows (the question, the psalms' wording) from what an Old Testament reader would infer, and say which named source, if any, argues each step.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 8:22 does not, in its own wording, say whether the man's father had just died or had died long before. That question drives the difference between the "spiritually dead" and secondary-burial readings in Positions, and it is answered, if at all, from outside the verse. (b) Matthew 8:28-34 does not say why the townspeople asked Jesus to leave. Any motive (fear, economic loss, something else) is an inference beyond the text's statement that "they begged Him to leave their region" (8:34, BSB).
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words of both texts. The five attached to this lesson:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Psalm 107:23-30 | allusion | plausible | Same sequence (sea, storm, cry, calm) and God "calmed the storm" (107:29); no citation formula; one named patristic voice (Chrysostom) makes the link |
| Jonah 1:3-16 | allusion | plausible | A sleeper in a boat in a great storm, fear of perishing, a calm, awed "men"; Jerome calls Jonah "a type", but Matthew 8 does not name Jonah |
| Daniel 7:13-14 | motif | plausible | First "Son of Man" in Matthew; Matthew later quotes Daniel's "clouds of heaven", but at 8:20 the link is debated (Blomberg against Vermes) |
| Luke 9:57-62 | parallel | strong | The foxes saying and "let the dead bury their own dead" are near-verbatim; the speakers, setting and Luke's third follower differ |
| Mark 5:1-20 | parallel | strong | Same event sequence after the storm; one man against two, Gerasenes against Gadarenes, and "before the appointed time" only in Matthew |

Defend one row in your own words, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` instead. Two candidates were considered and not authored. **Isaiah 65:4**, which describes a rebellious people "sitting among the graves... eating the meat of pigs" (BSB), shares tombs and pigs with this scene. But no named source read for this lesson argues that Matthew alludes to it, and two shared nouns are not enough to establish that someone deliberately evoked the other text. **1 Kings 19:19-21**, Elisha's "Please let me kiss my father and mother goodbye, and then I will follow you" (19:20, BSB), is close to Luke's third would-be follower (Luke 9:61), a scene Matthew does not include. Any link would run to Luke, not to Matthew 8.

Chrysostom, Jerome and Augustine on chapter 8 were read in the Catena Aurea (Oxford, 1841-45) on isidore.co. The Catena is itself a medieval anthology, and its attributions and translations were not checked against critical editions. Wikipedia's reproduction of one Catena passage printed "ceased" where isidore.co's text reads "ceases"; this lesson follows isidore.co. A web-search summary attributed the Psalm 107 sentence to Pseudo-Origen, but the Catena text read directly attributes it to Chrysostom (Hom. xxviii).
- **The Chrysostom sentence on Psalm 107 is bracketed in the Catena as "Ps 107:25".** In this app's BSB, 107:25 is the verse where God "raised a tempest" and 107:29 is the calming verse. The Catena's English ("He spake, and the storm of wind ceases") may reflect a Latin text whose verb can be read either way. This was not checked, and the connection rationale reports the bracket as printed.
- **The manuscript evidence for Gadarenes, Gergesenes and Gerasenes, and for "destroy us before our time" at 8:29, comes from Wikipedia's variant list, not from a printed critical apparatus.** The sigla were not checked against NA28 or UBS5.
- **Timothy Keller's argument for a Jonah parallel was read only as excerpted by The Gospel Coalition (2011), and it concerns Mark's storm, not Matthew's.** It is cited in the Jonah connection only for that reason.
- **No modern critical commentary on Matthew (Davies and Allison, France, Luz) was read directly for this lesson.** The Psalm 107 and Jonah links in particular deserve checking against those commentaries and against the Greek of Matthew compared with the Septuagint, which this lesson did not do.
- **The count of "Son of Man" in Matthew (first at 8:20; 29 times) was taken from this app's English BSB text by script.** Wikipedia reports 30 for the Greek of Matthew; the difference was not investigated.
