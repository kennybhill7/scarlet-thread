Matthew 9:1-17 is three short scenes in a row, and each one turns on an objection. A paralyzed man is carried to Jesus, Jesus tells him his sins are forgiven, and some scribes object in their own minds (9:1-8). Jesus calls a tax collector named Matthew, eats with "tax collectors and sinners," and Pharisees object to his disciples (9:9-13). John the Baptist's disciples then ask why Jesus's disciples do not fast (9:14-17). Each objection gets a short answer: a question and a healing, a proverb about doctors and a line of Scripture, and three images (a bridegroom, a patch, new wine). The middle answer is the first thing to study here, because it is an explicit quotation of the Old Testament. Jesus tells his critics to go and learn a saying from Hosea, and this lesson does exactly that: it reads Hosea 6:6 in this app's own Berean Standard Bible (BSB), then checks what sits underneath the English. Every BSB quotation below was checked against the app's own Bible files, before drafting and again afterward.

## Context

**What happens, in order (9:1-17).** Jesus crosses the lake and comes "to His own town" (9:1, BSB), which Matthew 4:13 has already named: "He went and lived in Capernaum." Men bring "a paralytic lying on a mat," and "When Jesus saw their faith," He said to the paralytic, "Take courage, son; your sins are forgiven" (9:2, BSB). Some scribes say to themselves, "This man is blaspheming!" (9:3, BSB). Jesus asks, "Which is easier: to say, 'Your sins are forgiven,' or to say, 'Get up and walk?'" (9:5, BSB), says "so that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (9:6, BSB), and tells the man to go home, which he does. The crowds "were filled with awe and glorified God, who had given such authority to men" (9:8, BSB). Next Jesus sees "a man named Matthew sitting at the tax booth" and says "Follow Me" (9:9, BSB). At a meal, "many tax collectors and sinners came and ate with Him and His disciples" (9:10, BSB); the Pharisees ask the disciples why; Jesus answers with a proverb ("It is not the healthy who need a doctor, but the sick," 9:12, BSB) and the Hosea saying (9:13). Finally "John's disciples" ask, "Why is it that we and the Pharisees fast so often, but Your disciples do not fast?" (9:14, BSB). Jesus answers with the bridegroom (9:15), a patch of unshrunk cloth (9:16), and new wine in old wineskins (9:17).

**What the scribes say in Matthew, and what they say elsewhere.** The question many readers remember, "Who can forgive sins but God alone?", is not in Matthew. In this app's BSB it is Mark 2:7 ("Why does this man speak like this? He is blaspheming! Who can forgive sins but God alone?") and Luke 5:21 ("Who is this man who speaks blasphemy? Who can forgive sins but God alone?"). Matthew's scribes say only, "This man is blaspheming!" (9:3, BSB). So what Matthew leaves the reader to work out, the other two Gospels have the scribes say aloud. The older commentaries read Matthew's charge in light of Mark and Luke. Ellicott says that for a man to forgive in this way "was to claim a divine attribute," and Matthew Poole writes, "It seemeth that it was then agreed on all hands, that forgiving of sins was the prerogative of God alone" (both read on Bible Hub's Matthew 9:3 commentary page). The Positions section below reports where these readers part ways.

**One verse belongs to Matthew alone.** The crowd's response in 9:8, that God "had given such authority to men," has no counterpart in Mark 2:12 ("We have never seen anything like this!") or Luke 5:26 ("We have seen remarkable things today"). The plural "men" is the puzzle. It is a checkable fact that Jesus is one man and the verse says "men"; what Matthew means by recording it is the first named dispute below.

**"Matthew" here, "Levi" in Mark and Luke.** Matthew 9:9 calls the tax collector "Matthew"; Mark 2:14 calls him "Levi son of Alphaeus"; Luke 5:27 calls him "a tax collector named Levi." Matthew's own list of the Twelve then names "Matthew the tax collector" (10:3, BSB), with a "James son of Alphaeus" in the same verse. The commentaries on Bible Hub's Matthew 9:9 page all treat Matthew and Levi as one man. The Cambridge Bible writes, "The identification of Matthew with Levi can scarcely be seriously disputed," and Barnes notes, "The Jews were in the habit of giving several names to the same person." They give different reasons for the two names. Bengel says, "It is possible that Matthew did not like the name which he had borne as a publican," and the Cambridge Bible thinks the name Matthew was probably taken when he became a follower of Jesus. These are harmonizations offered by the commentators. No Gospel says in its own words that Levi and Matthew are the same person.

**"Matthew's house" is the BSB supplying a name.** The BSB reads "dining at Matthew's house" (9:10). The Greek at that point, per Bible Hub's interlinear, is *en tē oikia*, "in the house," and Wikipedia's article on Matthew 9 says the house "is often understood to refer to Matthew's house, because in Luke 5:28 the house is clearly that of Matthew known also as Levi" (in this app's BSB the house is named at Luke 5:29, "Levi hosted a great banquet for Jesus at his house"). This is a small, checkable case of a translation making a reasonable inference visible in the English. It is not an error, but a reader should know the name is not in Matthew's Greek at that spot.

**The Hosea quotation, checked three ways.** Matthew 9:13 in this app's BSB reads in full:

> "But go and learn what this means: 'I desire mercy, not sacrifice.' For I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners." (Matthew 9:13, BSB)

The saying comes from Hosea 6:6, which the BSB renders: "For I desire mercy, not sacrifice, and the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings" (Hosea 6:6, BSB). Three checks follow.

- *English.* Matthew's quoted words, "I desire mercy, not sacrifice," are exactly the BSB's wording of Hosea's first clause. Matthew leaves out the opening "For" and the entire second clause about "the knowledge of God."
- *Greek.* Bible Hub's interlinear gives Matthew's Greek as *Eleos thelō kai ou thysian*, which glossed word for word reads mercy / I desire / and / not / sacrifice. The Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament) text of Hosea 6:6 printed at ellopos.net reads *dioti eleos thelō kai ou thysian*: the same words, with Hosea's "for" (*dioti*) at the front. Matthew's clause matches that Septuagint wording exactly. That page does not say which printed edition of the Septuagint it uses, and this lesson did not check other editions.
- *Hebrew.* Bible Hub's interlinear shows the noun behind "mercy" in Hosea 6:6 as *chesed* (Strong's 2617), followed by *welo*, "and not," before "sacrifice." Matthew's Greek "and not," the Septuagint's "and not," and the Hebrew's "and not" all agree.

A note that only shows up below the English: the BSB uses three different English words for the same Hebrew noun within Hosea. *Chesed* is "loyalty" at 6:4 ("your loyalty is like a morning mist"), "mercy" at 6:6, and "loving devotion" at 2:19 ("I will betroth you in righteousness and justice, in loving devotion and compassion"), per Bible Hub's interlinears for each verse. In Hosea's Hebrew, the people's failing *chesed* in 6:4 and the *chesed* God desires in 6:6 are the same word, two verses apart. An English reader of Hosea 6 cannot see that, and an English reader of Matthew 9:13 sees only "mercy."

**The words "go and learn."** Two commentators on Bible Hub's Matthew 9:13 page identify the opening words as a teacher's phrase of the time. Meyer says it "corresponds to the Rabbinical form" of sending a student off to study, and Gill says it "is a phrase used by the Jews" when about to explain a passage of Scripture. Both are reported here as those commentators' claims. This lesson did not check rabbinic sources directly.

**The same quotation comes back.** Matthew quotes the same clause a second time: "If only you had known the meaning of 'I desire mercy, not sacrifice,' you would not have condemned the innocent" (12:7, BSB), in a dispute about the Sabbath. Ellicott notes the recurrence. Neither Mark nor Luke quotes Hosea 6:6 in its parallel to either scene: Mark 2:17 has no quotation, and Luke 5:32 adds "to repentance" instead.

**The fasting question, and three Gospels' wording.** In Matthew the question comes from "John's disciples" (9:14). In Mark "people came to Jesus and asked" (2:18), and in Luke an unnamed "they" asks (5:33). Matthew's Jesus asks, "How can the guests of the bridegroom mourn while He is with them?" (9:15, BSB), where Mark 2:19 and Luke 5:34 both have "fast." The Pulpit Commentary notes the difference and judges that Matthew's word "seems the more original," which is that commentary's judgment, not something the texts settle. Two endings also differ. Matthew alone closes "and both are preserved" (9:17, BSB), and Luke alone adds, "And no one after drinking old wine wants new, for he says, 'The old is better.'" (5:39, BSB). Luke's patch is also different: "No one tears a piece of cloth from a new garment and sews it on an old one" (5:36, BSB), where Matthew's patch is "unshrunk cloth" (9:16).

**Being "taken from them" (9:15).** Meyer writes, "It is to be observed that this is the first occasion in Matthew on which Jesus alludes to His death," and the Cambridge Bible makes the same point. Matthew 9:15 itself says only that the bridegroom "will be taken from them," and then "they will fast." The reading of this as Jesus's death is those commentators' reading, a natural one given where Matthew's story goes, but the verse does not name death.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 9:1-17 is put together, not on what the arrangement is meant to teach.

**Three objections, three different audiences.** The objection comes from scribes, unspoken and "to themselves" (9:3); then from Pharisees, put to the disciples and not to Jesus (9:11); then from John's disciples, put to Jesus directly (9:14). Each time Jesus is the one who answers. This is checkable sequence. Whether Matthew built it as a deliberate progression is not something the sequence alone shows.

**A question answered with a question, then sayings.** In the first scene Jesus answers a thought with a question ("Which is easier...?", 9:5) and then an action. In the second he answers with a proverb (9:12), a quotation (9:13a), and a statement of purpose ("I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners," 9:13b). In the third he answers with images: a wedding (9:15), clothing (9:16), wine (9:17). No scene repeats another's form.

**The word authority ties the first scene to what comes before it.** The crowds at the end of the Sermon on the Mount were amazed "because He taught as one who had authority, and not as their scribes" (7:29, BSB). In 9:6 Jesus claims "authority on earth to forgive sins," scribes are the objectors, and in 9:8 the crowds glorify God for "such authority." The repeated word, and scribes appearing in both places, is checkable. This lesson does not claim Matthew intended a link.

**Eating, then not eating.** The second scene is a meal ("dining," "ate," "eat," 9:10-11) and the third is a question about fasting (9:14-15). The move from one to the other is plain on the page. Whether it is more than placement is left open.

## Positions

Three questions in this passage get different answers from named readers. They are reported here in those readers' own words and not settled. The older commentators quoted are read through Bible Hub's commentary pages; the modern scholars are reported through Wikipedia, as flagged in each case.

**1. What does Matthew claim when the crowd thanks God for giving "such authority to men" (9:8)?** One group reads the plural as the crowd's limited understanding, which Matthew records without endorsing. Ellicott says of the crowd, "They rested in the thought of a delegated authority," without going further. Barnes writes, "It expresses no sentiment of the evangelist about the nature of Christ, but is a record of their feelings." Matthew Poole says the crowd thanked God for giving power to men because "they would still own Christ no more than a man." Read this way, the crowd's words fall short of the scribes' charge in 9:3, which treats forgiving sins as a divine act.

Meyer reads the plural differently. He rejects the view that "only Jesus is meant" and takes the word as "men generally,—the human race." In his reading the crowd "regarded it as a new gift of God to humanity" and praised God for it. That reading treats the plural as the crowd's real meaning, not a mistake Matthew is exposing.

A related split concerns the kind of authority 9:6 claims. Ellicott, on 9:6, says Jesus forgave as "the representative of mankind," "exercising a delegated power" the Father had given him. The same Ellicott, on 9:3, says the scribes rightly saw that forgiving in this way was "to claim a divine attribute." The Pulpit Commentary reads 9:6 against Daniel 7:13 (see the connection below). These commentators do not deny that Matthew presents Jesus as doing what God does; they differ on how the "Son of Man" title, the delegated language, and the crowd's "to men" fit together. Matthew's own wording supplies all three pieces, the scribes' charge (9:3), the Son of Man's authority (9:6), and the crowd's praise (9:8), and does not comment on how they relate.

**2. How absolute is "not sacrifice" (9:13)?** Meyer reads the negative strictly: "The negative is absolute," he writes, "God does not desire sacrifice instead of mercy, but mercy instead of sacrifice." Barnes reads it as a familiar Hebrew way of stating a preference: "This is not a declaration on the part of God that he was opposed to 'sacrifices'," he writes, and the saying is "a Hebrew mode of speaking" equivalent to "I prefer mercy to sacrifice." Ellicott says the saying "asserted the superiority of ethical to ceremonial law." The Pulpit Commentary raises a different question, whose mercy is meant: it allows that Jesus may be using the words "only as a summary of an important truth, that God prefers to show mercy rather than to insist on sacrifice," which would make the mercy God's own toward sinners rather than mercy God requires of people. One checkable detail bears on these readings without settling them: in Hosea's own verse, the parallel second line is worded as a comparison, "the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings" (Hosea 6:6, BSB), where Bible Hub's interlinear glosses the Hebrew as "above burnt offerings." Matthew does not quote that second line.

**3. What are the old garment and the old wineskins (9:16-17)?** This is the passage's clearest dispute about continuity and discontinuity: whether what Jesus brings fits inside the existing religious life of Israel, replaces part of it, or replaces it entirely. The readings below differ on what "old" refers to.

- *The Pharisees' traditions, not the Law itself.* Barnes paraphrases Jesus: "my 'new' doctrines do not match with the old rites of the Pharisees." Wikipedia's article on the parable summarizes John Calvin along similar lines, as taking the old garment and wineskins to be the Pharisees' "mentality and oral tradition," "not in accord with the proper teachings of the law" (that is Wikipedia's summary of Calvin; this lesson did not read Calvin's own commentary on the passage).
- *The forms of piety John's disciples still kept.* Meyer reads the patch saying as: "The old forms of piety amid which John and his disciples still move are not suited to the new religious life emanating from me." This reading ties the images closely to the question about fasting that prompted them.
- *Judaism as a system.* The Cambridge Bible writes, "The old garment is Judaism. Christianity is not to be pieced on to Judaism to fill up its deficiencies." Wikipedia reports R. T. France as saying that "Jesus has brought something new, and the rituals and traditions of official Judaism cannot contain it," and Joel B. Green as saying Jesus "pits his own, new way against the old way of the Pharisees and their scribes" (both reported through Wikipedia; their own commentaries were not read here).
- *The disciples' readiness, a pastoral reading.* Matthew Henry reads the images as caution about pacing demands on new followers: "Great caution and prudence are necessary, that young converts may not receive gloomy and forbidding ideas of the service of our Lord."
- *Total separation.* Wikipedia reports, through Joseph B. Tyson, that the second-century teacher Marcion used the passage to justify a "total separation between the religion that Jesus and Paul espoused and that of the Hebrew Scriptures." It is reported here as the far end of the discontinuity reading, which helps a reader place the others.

Matthew's own ending, "and both are preserved" (9:17), is a detail any of these readings has to account for, and Luke's "The old is better" (5:39) is one Luke's readers have to account for. This lesson does not decide among the readings.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked example of crossing the bridge from what the passage says in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. It models the shape; it is not an answer key. The learner's own attempt is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 9:10-12: at a meal, "many tax collectors and sinners came and ate with Him and His disciples," the Pharisees ask, "Why does your Teacher eat with tax collectors and sinners?", and Jesus answers, "It is not the healthy who need a doctor, but the sick" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the objection is about who Jesus eats with, and the answer is a proverb about a doctor's patients. Inference: the proverb accepts the critics' label ("sick") for the people at the table and gives that label as the reason for being there, not a reason to stay away. Both steps go beyond the cited wording and are this lesson's own.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Someone's reputation for being in the wrong is not, in itself, a reason to avoid their company, and can be the very reason to keep it.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: sharing a table with people others steer clear of, and not letting criticism of that company decide whether it continues. Not carried forward: any claim that the modern person stands where Jesus stands in the scene, or that the people they eat with are "sinners" in the text's sense. The proverb is about where a doctor goes; the bridge takes only its direction (toward the person, not away), not the doctor's role.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a principle drawn by analogy from a narrative example and a proverb, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none. The passage makes no promise about what happens when someone shares a table with people who have a bad reputation.

**Modern domain and situation.** Neighborhood life. A volunteer is planning a block cookout. One neighbor was recently in a public dispute with the homeowners' association, and two other neighbors ask that he not be invited, saying his presence will make things awkward.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: including someone despite social pressure, without dismissing the people raising the concern. One faithful response: the volunteer invites him, tells the two neighbors privately that the cookout is open to the whole street, and makes no public comment on the dispute itself.

**Cautions.** This does not mean every concern about a person's presence is just about reputation. If someone poses a real safety risk, has harassed or threatened a neighbor, or is under a protective order, that is a matter for the people affected and, where needed, the police or a qualified professional, not a study note. The passage is about Jesus eating with people the religious leaders labeled; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing, and it may not fit a given case.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Matthew's scribes say only "This man is blaspheming!" (9:3), while Mark's and Luke's say why: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" If Matthew worked from Mark (a common hypothesis about how the Gospels relate, not argued in this lesson), what would you need to know about Matthew's habits elsewhere to judge whether he shortened the charge because he assumed readers knew the reason, or for some other purpose? Would the answer change how much weight 9:3 can carry on its own?
2. Hosea's people fail in *chesed* at 6:4 and God desires *chesed* at 6:6. Is God desiring something from the people (the *chesed* they lacked), or speaking of his own *chesed*? How would you test that against Hosea 6 itself before bringing in Matthew? And does your answer bear on the Pulpit Commentary's question of whose mercy Matthew 9:13 has in view?
3. Matthew alone ends the wineskin saying with "both are preserved" (9:17), and Luke alone adds "The old is better" (5:39). Which of the readings in Positions fits each Gospel's ending most easily, and which has to work hardest? Is it possible that the two Gospels point different ways on continuity?
4. The BSB writes "Matthew's house" (9:10) where the Greek has "the house." How would you decide whether a translation should make that kind of inference visible in the text, put it in a footnote, or leave it out? Find one other place in this passage where you would want to check the Greek before relying on an English detail.

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) tell the three scenes of 9:1-17 in order, naming who objects in each scene and to whom. (b) Say which words in 9:13 are a quotation, which book they come from, and what Matthew leaves out of that verse. (c) Name two details in Matthew's account of the paralytic that are missing from Mark's, or the reverse.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the three scenes and three objections (see Context and Literary Design); (1:00-2:30) the Hosea 6:6 quotation checked in English, Greek and Hebrew, including *chesed* behind "loyalty," "mercy," and "loving devotion" (see Context); (2:30-3:30) one difference each between Matthew and Mark or Luke in all three scenes, including Matthew/Levi and "mourn"/"fast" (see Context and the parallel connections); (3:30-4:30) one of the three named disputes, giving at least two readers in their own words (see Positions); (4:30-5:00) one question from Questions to Carry, stated on purpose as the last thing taught.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one worked candidate: "The lesson spends more time on the scribes' wording and the crowd's 'to men' than on the obvious point, that Jesus forgives sins, which only God does. Reporting the commentators' fine distinctions blurs what all three Gospels plainly show." Before answering, note what the lesson does say: it quotes the scribes' charge in all three Gospels, reports Ellicott and Poole treating forgiveness as God's prerogative, and states that the commentators quoted do not deny Matthew presents Jesus as doing what God does. A second candidate, running the other way: "Matthew pointedly drops 'Who can forgive sins but God alone?' and alone adds 'authority to men.' A careful reader should ask whether Matthew is presenting Jesus's authority as something given to a man, not as a claim to be God. A lesson that leans on Mark and Luke to read Matthew is not reading Matthew." How would you answer each, using only Matthew's own wording first?
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 9:2-7 does not say that the man's paralysis was caused by his sins. Jesus forgives and then heals, and the text does not connect the two causally; any such connection is a reader's inference. (b) Matthew 9:15 does not say when, how often, or in what form the disciples will fast after "the bridegroom will be taken from them"; it says only that "then they will fast." Any rule about Christian fasting goes beyond this verse.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Pick a connection and defend it: name its type and label, and point to the words in both texts that support that grade. The five connections attached to this lesson sit at different levels on purpose:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hosea 6:4-6 | quotation | explicit | Jesus introduces a saying to be learned and quotes Hosea 6:6a word for word in the BSB; Matthew's Greek matches the Septuagint clause printed at ellopos.net |
| Mark 2:1-12 | parallel | strong | 9:6 and Mark 2:10 are identical; real differences in place, roof, address, and the scribes' and crowd's words |
| Luke 5:27-39 | parallel | strong | Same three-part sequence and shared sayings; "Matthew"/"Levi," Hosea quotation only in Matthew, "both are preserved" only in Matthew, "The old is better" only in Luke |
| Hosea 2:16-20 | allusion | plausible | God as Israel's husband; one named scholar (Nosgen, reported secondhand by the Pulpit Commentary) reads 9:15 as a claim to be Israel's Bridegroom; no shared word "bridegroom" |
| Daniel 7:13-14 | allusion | plausible | "Son of Man" and authority; the Pulpit Commentary says Jesus "seems to point them to Daniel 7:13"; two shared terms, no quotation |

Choose one row and defend it in your own words, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`. Two candidates this lesson considered and did not author: Isaiah 62:5 ("as a bridegroom rejoices over his bride, so your God will rejoice over you," BSB) and Isaiah 54:5 ("For your husband is your Maker," BSB). Isaiah 62:5 actually shares the word "bridegroom" with Matthew 9:15, which Hosea 2 does not. It was not authored because none of the sources read for this lesson connects Matthew 9:15 to it; the Pulpit Commentary's citations go to Hosea, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel. That is a `no_warrant_yet`: a real verbal overlap waiting for a named reader, not a refuted link. Matthew's second quotation of Hosea 6:6 at 12:7 is a third candidate. It is a checkable recurrence inside one Gospel and is noted in Context, not graded as its own connection.
