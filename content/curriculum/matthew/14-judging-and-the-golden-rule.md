Matthew 7:1-12 closes the long middle section of the Sermon on the Mount with five short, loosely linked units: a command not to judge, grounded in a measure-for-measure warning (7:1-2); the speck-and-beam image illustrating it (7:3-5); a harder saying about withholding what is holy from dogs and pearls from pigs (7:6); an invitation to ask, seek, and knock (7:7-11); and the Golden Rule (7:12), which the text's own wording ties back to "the Law and the Prophets" -- close to, but not identical to, the phrase "the Law or the Prophets" that opened this whole discourse at 5:17. Two lines in this short span are among the most over-quoted in English-speaking popular culture: "do not judge" is routinely proof-texted to mean that no evaluation or disapproval of anyone is ever warranted, and the Golden Rule is often treated as if it were Jesus's own, unprecedented invention. Both popular readings run into real, checkable difficulty on inspection -- the first from this same Gospel's own later verses, the second from a documented, pre-Christian Jewish source. Every quotation below was drafted directly from this app's own shipped `web/public/bible/BSB/40.json` file, not from memory of the KJV's "Judge not, that ye be not judged" or a modern paraphrase's "or you too will be judged" -- neither of which is this app's own wording, as the Context section below states line by line.

## Context

**What the text says, in order (7:1-12), checked directly against this app's own BSB wording.** "Do not judge, or you will be judged. For with the same judgment you pronounce, you will be judged; and with the measure you use, it will be measured to you" (7:1-2, BSB). This differs in small but real ways from the wording most readers already carry in memory: the BSB's own text has no "that" (as in the KJV's "that ye be not judged") and no "too" (as in some modern paraphrases' "or you too will be judged"), and its second verse specifies "the **same** judgment you pronounce" -- a word some popular renderings of this verse drop. The speck-and-beam image follows: "Why do you look at the speck in your brother's eye, but fail to notice the beam in your own eye? ... You hypocrite! First take the beam out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to remove the speck from your brother's eye" (7:3-5, BSB). The BSB's own word is "beam," not "plank" -- a modern-sounding substitute some readers expect from a paraphrase they have heard elsewhere; "speck" is the BSB's own word for the smaller object, not the KJV's "mote." Verse 6 reads: "Do not give dogs what is holy; do not throw your pearls before swine. If you do, they may trample them under their feet, and then turn and tear you to pieces" (7:6, BSB) -- note the BSB's own word order, "give dogs what is holy," not "give what is holy to dogs." The ask/seek/knock unit follows: "Ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and the door will be opened to you. For everyone who asks receives; he who seeks finds; and to him who knocks, the door will be opened" (7:7-8, BSB), then the father-and-son analogy: "Which of you, if his son asks for bread, will give him a stone? Or if he asks for a fish, will give him a snake? So if you who are evil know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who ask Him!" (7:9-11, BSB). The unit closes: "In everything, then, do to others as you would have them do to you. For this is the essence of the Law and the Prophets" (7:12, BSB).

**A real, checkable wording distinction inside 7:11 itself.** The BSB's own text uses two different phrases in the same verse: evil human fathers give "good **gifts**" to their children, but the heavenly Father gives "good **things**" to those who ask Him (7:11, BSB). A reader working from memory of a translation that uses "good gifts" in both halves of this verse -- as several widely used English translations do -- would misquote this app's own wording at the second half without realizing it. This is exactly the kind of over-familiar-text error this lesson's own drafting process was built to catch, and the second automated check described in Sources and Limits below exists specifically to catch it.

**A real, checkable echo of 5:17, with one wording difference.** Matthew 5:17 reads, "Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law **or** the Prophets" (BSB; quoted directly from this app's own corpus, not assumed from the earlier Antitheses lesson in this curriculum). Matthew 7:12 closes this unit with, "For this is the essence of the Law **and** the Prophets" (BSB) -- the same two-term phrase, but with "or" at 5:17 and "and" at 7:12, and with "the essence of" added at 7:12 that 5:17 does not carry. Both are checkable facts about where this phrase recurs and how its wording shifts; this lesson does not resolve whether the shift from "or" to "and" is a meaningful compositional choice or an incidental variation in how Matthew phrases the same two-word pairing twice (see Questions to Carry below).

**7:15-20, just past this unit's own boundary, is quoted here only because Positions below needs it directly.** "Beware of false prophets. They come to you in sheep's clothing, but inwardly they are ravenous wolves. By their fruit you will recognize them... So then, by their fruit you will recognize them" (7:15-16, 20, BSB). This verse range sits outside 7:1-12, the passage range this lesson actually covers; it is quoted here, directly from the same BSB file, only because the Positions section below reports a real dispute over how this later command to recognize false prophets by their fruit relates to 7:1's "do not judge."

**The unclean-animal background behind "dogs" and "swine" (7:6).** The Wikipedia article on Matthew 7:6 used here reports that "Dogs were part of society, but were half wild and roamed the region in packs that were sometimes dangerous to humans," while "Pigs were the quintessential unclean animal and were closely associated with the Gentile communities" of the period. This is reported here as checkable background on what these two images would have called to mind for a first-century Jewish audience, not as a settled claim about what 7:6 as a whole instructs -- that question, including a real named scholarly disagreement over whether 7:6 even continues the "do not judge" topic at all, is reported fully in Positions below.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 7:1-12 is built, not on what that construction is meant to teach.

**A reciprocal-treatment bracket, stated plainly in the text's own repeated language.** The unit opens with a measure-for-measure warning -- "with the measure you use, it will be measured to you" (7:2, BSB) -- and closes with a reciprocal-treatment command -- "do to others as you would have them do to you" (7:12, BSB). Both state a principle of matching treatment back to the one who gives it; both ground that principle in something beyond the hearer's own preference, 7:2 in the judgment the hearer will himself receive and 7:12 in "the Law and the Prophets." This is a checkable fact about where these two phrases sit in the unit's own order -- the first and next-to-last sentences Jesus speaks in this short span -- not a claim about Matthew's own compositional intent, and not a claim that the passage forms a chiasm in the technical sense; no source consulted for this lesson states that it does, and this lesson does not assert a structural label stronger than what a plain reading of the bracket itself supports.

**A triad repeated verbatim, then repeated again in reverse order.** "Ask... seek... knock" (7:7, BSB) names three actions in one order; the next verse restates the same three actions and their three results in the identical order -- "everyone who asks receives; he who seeks finds; and to him who knocks, the door will be opened" (7:8, BSB). This is a directly checkable repetition, not an inference.

**Two paired questions building a single lesser-to-greater argument.** 7:9-10 asks two structurally identical questions (a son asking for bread and receiving a stone instead; a son asking for a fish and receiving a snake instead), both expecting the answer "no parent would do this." 7:11 then draws one explicit conclusion from both together: "if you who are evil know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who ask Him" (BSB). The argument's own shape -- two parallel negative cases, one "how much more" conclusion -- is checkable directly from the text's own wording.

**Whether 7:6 continues the same topic as 7:1-5, or opens a new one, is itself a live question this lesson does not resolve on its own.** Read one way, all five pieces of 7:1-12 form one continuous unit on judgment and reciprocal treatment. Read another way -- reported by name in Positions below -- 7:6 either extends the judging material in a specific, narrower direction, or breaks from it entirely toward the "God and Mammon" theme Matthew 6:24 raised two units earlier. This lesson's own Literary Design observations above describe the bracket and the two internal structures without deciding which reading of 7:6's placement is correct; that dispute belongs to Positions, not here.

## Positions

Two disputes attach to how this unit's own content coheres internally; a third is a historical comparison this lesson reports rather than a live disagreement about what the text itself says.

**What does "do not judge" (7:1) forbid, given this same Gospel's own commands to assess who receives "what is holy" (7:6) and to recognize false prophets "by their fruit" (7:15-20)?** This tension is not invented by this lesson; it is a long-recognized question with named participants on record.

Jerome raised the question directly, centuries ago, by pointing to apostolic practice elsewhere in the New Testament: "But if He forbids us to judge, how then does Paul judge the Corinthian who had committed uncleanness? Or Peter convict Ananias and Sapphira of falsehood?" -- a question reported through the Wikipedia article on Matthew 7:1 used here. Leon Morris, reported in the same article, reads 7:1 as a narrower warning than a blanket rule against evaluation: "it is an attack on the hasty and unfair judgments, and as the further verses show it is also an attack on the hypocrites who criticize others while ignoring their own faults." Pseudo-Chrysostom, also reported there, reads the verse as targeting a specific motive rather than the act of correction itself: Christ "did not herein forbid Christians to reprove others out of goodwill, but only intended that Christians should not despise Christians by making a show of their own righteousness, hating others often on suspicion alone, condemning them, and pursuing private grudges under the show of piety." The same article states its own synthesis of this reading, building on a claim it attributes to "most scholars" without naming them individually: "Obeying Christ's commands in this chapter does not preclude assessing another person's basic character -- whether one is a dog (v. 6) or a false prophet (v. 15), or whether one's life shows fruit (v. 16) -- since Scripture repeatedly exhorts believers to evaluate carefully." This lesson reports that synthesis as the article's own stated position, not as a claim independently verified against "most scholars"' own individual writings, which were not read for this lesson.

What these three readings share, and what distinguishes them from a reading that would treat 7:1 as forbidding all evaluation outright, is a proposed narrowing: each locates the thing 7:1 actually forbids somewhere more specific than "noticing anything about another person at all" -- a particular manner of judging (hasty, unfair, hypocritical, self-righteous) rather than the act of discernment itself. None of the sources consulted here states the opposite position in comparably named, sourced terms -- that is, no named scholar surveyed for this lesson argues 7:1 really does forbid the kind of discernment 7:6, 7:15, and 7:16 go on to require, leaving those later verses in open contradiction with 7:1 rather than in some form of coherence with it. This lesson could not locate a comparably substantive defense of that stronger reading and does not invent one to balance the position above; it reports this as a real limit on what this lesson found, not as evidence the stronger reading has no defenders anywhere.

**What does 7:6 instruct, and how does it relate to the judge-not command that opens this same short unit?** A second, separate dispute, this one over what 7:6 itself means and whether it continues 7:1-5's topic at all.

One reading, reported through the Wikipedia article on Matthew 7:6 used here, connects 7:6 directly back to 7:1-5: "this verse in light of the ones immediately preceding it (7:1-5) where instruction is given to not judge a brother... In this interpretation, the 'holy things' and the 'pearls' are the 'brother' who might be cast amongst the 'dogs' and 'swine.'" On this reading, 7:6 continues the judging material by naming a specific risk: exposing a brother's faults to those who will only trample and attack him for it, rather than correct him. A second, historical reading, also reported in the same article, connects the verse instead to the Eucharist: "a common view was that this verse refers to the Eucharist, as exemplified in the Didache, which teaches that only baptized individuals ought to receive the Eucharist" -- a reading this lesson reports from the Wikipedia article's own characterization of the Didache, not from an independent reading of the Didache's own text, which was not fetched for this lesson. A third reading, attributed by name to John Nolland in the same article, rejects the connection to judging (and to the Eucharist) altogether: "This verse is not an attack on any particular group, but rather a continuation of the theme of God and Mammon begun at Matthew 6:24." On Nolland's reading, 7:6 is not part of the judging material this unit opens with at all; it belongs instead to a different theme two units earlier in the Sermon.

These three readings disagree about something checkable and consequential: whether 7:6 is part of the same topic as 7:1-5 (the first and second readings, in different ways) or a different topic placed next to it without a topical connection (Nolland's reading). This lesson does not resolve which is correct. The unclean-animal background reported in Context above (dogs as half-wild scavengers, pigs as "the quintessential unclean animal... closely associated with the Gentile communities") is common ground to all three readings; what divides them is what that image is doing in this specific place in the text.

**How does Jesus's Golden Rule (7:12) compare to Rabbi Hillel's earlier negative formulation of the same principle?** This is a historical and comparative-religion question, not a live disagreement about what Matthew 7:12 itself says -- but the sourcing behind it is real and substantive enough to report by name, as the comparison is often repeated at a meme-level of accuracy that this lesson checked directly rather than assumed.

Hillel's own saying is preserved in the Babylonian Talmud, tractate Shabbat 31a, in a story about two gentiles who separately asked to be converted "on condition that you teach me the entire Torah while I am standing on one foot." Read directly from Sefaria's own text of the passage (the William Davidson Talmud translation, not a secondhand report of it), Shammai "pushed him away with the builder's cubit in his hand," but "the same gentile came before Hillel. He converted him and said to him: That which is hateful to you do not do to another; that is the entire Torah, and the rest is its interpretation. Go study." Two things are checkable directly from this primary text: Hillel's saying is stated in the negative ("do not do" what is "hateful to you"), where Matthew 7:12 states Jesus's saying in the positive ("do to others as you would have them do to you," BSB); and both sayings explicitly ground themselves in the same authority -- Hillel's in "the entire Torah," Jesus's in "the Law and the Prophets" (BSB) -- a real, checkable parallel in form, not only in content.

Named scholars disagree about how much the positive/negative difference actually matters. R.T. France, reported through the Wikipedia article on Matthew 7:12 used here, identifies the negative form (sometimes called the "Silver Rule") as itself "appear[ing] in several works of Greek philosophy and also in earlier Jewish writings" -- that is, Hillel's formulation was not unique to him even within his own era, which this lesson reports as a caution against treating the Hillel/Jesus comparison as a two-party contest rather than one instance of a wider ancient pattern. Ulrich Luz, reported in the same article, states that "some scholars see the positive version as being very important because it instructs all disciples to work actively for the good of others, not simply to desist passively from doing harm" -- a real claim that the positive form asks more of a hearer than the negative one does. Against this, the same article reports that "early Christian writers saw little difference between the two versions, and several paraphrased this verse with the negative form" -- that is, among readers closest to the text historically, the distinction Luz's scholars draw was not treated as decisive. Israel Abrahams, in an essay read directly for this lesson, reports a further, internal rabbinic question about Hillel's own choice of words: Rabbi Samuel Edels (known as the Maharsha) is quoted asking "Why did not Hillel say to him, 'Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself'?" -- that is, even within the Jewish tradition that preserves Hillel's saying, a later named rabbi questioned why Hillel reached for a negative formulation of his own instead of simply quoting Leviticus 19:18's positive command directly. This lesson reports Edels's question as a real, named instance of internal reflection on the same positive/negative distinction, without resolving it.

This lesson does not treat Hillel's saying as the single source Jesus's own saying depends on, and no source consulted here makes that stronger claim either; France's report that the negative form recurs elsewhere in both Greek and Jewish writing argues against treating this as a two-party comparison at all. What this lesson does report as checkable is narrower and, this lesson judges, still substantial: a real, datable, pre-Christian or near-contemporary Jewish saying exists, preserved in Judaism's own central legal text, that grounds a reciprocal ethical principle in "the entire Torah" using close to the same rhetorical move Matthew 7:12 uses to ground its own version in "the Law and the Prophets" -- and named scholars disagree, on the record, about how much weight the positive-versus-negative difference between the two sayings should carry.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage states in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. This is a model of the shape, not an answer key -- the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 7:3-5: "Why do you look at the speck in your brother's eye, but fail to notice the beam in your own eye? ... You hypocrite! First take the beam out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to remove the speck from your brother's eye" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the image deliberately exaggerates scale -- a "speck" against a "beam," the kind of timber that would make the comparison physically absurd if taken literally, since no one could function with a beam lodged in their own eye at all. Inference: the exaggeration itself is doing the persuasive work; a first-century hearer would recognize this as pointed, even uncomfortable humor rather than a literal diagnostic claim about relative fault sizes, aimed at making the self-appointed corrector feel the absurdity of their own position before they feel entitled to correct anyone else's. This inference goes one step beyond the Context section's plain quotation of the verse and is labeled here as this lesson's own step.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Before attempting to correct a specific, named fault in someone else, a person has real reason to ask whether a comparable or larger fault of their own is currently unexamined -- not because self-examination earns the right to correct others, but because the text's own image suggests an unexamined "beam" actively distorts the corrector's ability to "see clearly."

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the specific sequence the text states -- self-examination before correction, not correction withheld forever -- and the warning against correcting from a position of unacknowledged, comparable fault. Not carried forward: a claim that only a flawless person may ever name a specific wrong in someone else, which is not what 7:5 itself says ("first take the beam out... **and then** you will see clearly to remove the speck," BSB -- the beam's removal is a precondition for clear correction, not a reason correction never happens).

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a command carried by direct analogy from the passage's own stated sequence ("first... and then"), not an inference stretched from a narrative example. Promise scope: none claimed; 7:3-5 states a warning and a sequence, not a promise about what will happen once self-examination is attempted.

**Modern domain and situation.** Workplace management. A team lead has scheduled a one-on-one to address a direct report's repeated lateness to morning stand-ups. The night before, the team lead realizes they have missed the deadline on their own last two deliverables to that same report, without having said anything about it.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: naming one's own comparable shortcoming honestly before raising the other person's, not abandoning the conversation about lateness altogether and not using a confession of one's own fault as a way to avoid the original topic. One faithful response: the team lead opens the one-on-one by naming their own missed deadlines first, specifically and without excuse, then raises the lateness pattern as a separate, still-real issue that needs addressing -- treating the sequence (self-examination first) as a matter of order and credibility, not as a reason the lateness goes unaddressed.

**Cautions.** This does not mean every act of correction must be preceded by a full public confession of a comparable fault -- some situations (a safety violation, a harassment complaint) require immediate correction regardless of the corrector's own unrelated shortcomings, and treating 7:3-5 as a universal precondition for ever raising a concern would turn a warning about hypocrisy into a tool for avoiding accountability altogether. It also does not mean the two faults named in any given situation must be of comparable size or kind -- the text's own exaggeration (a speck against a beam) argues against drawing a precise equivalence between any two specific faults in any specific case. The passage states a sequence and a warning; the bridge above is an analogy the learner is testing, not a general rule that applies identically to every situation involving correction.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. 7:7-11's promise -- "Ask, and it will be given to you... how much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who ask Him" (BSB) -- reads, on its own, close to unconditional. Elsewhere in the New Testament, 1 John 5:14 adds an explicit qualifier ("if we ask anything according to His will, He hears us," BSB) and James 4:3 names a reason asking can fail ("you ask with wrong motives," BSB). What would you need to decide about how 7:7-11's own promise relates to these later qualifiers -- does 7:7-11 simply assume conditions 1 John and James later state explicitly, or does reading those conditions back into Matthew 7 soften a promise the text itself states without qualification?
2. The connection to Luke 11:9-13 above shows Matthew's conclusion naming "good things" (general) where Luke's names "the Holy Spirit" (specific) as what the Father gives to those who ask. What would you need to know about how Matthew's and Luke's Gospels relate to each other -- which one preserves an earlier form of this saying, and why the other might have generalized or specified it -- before deciding whether these are two records of one saying, with one Gospel narrowing or widening the other's scope, or two separately remembered occasions?
3. Matthew 5:17 grounds Jesus's own relationship to Scripture in "the Law **or** the Prophets" (BSB); Matthew 7:12 grounds the Golden Rule in "the essence of the Law **and** the Prophets" (BSB) -- a real wording shift this lesson's Context section states but does not explain. What would you need to know about Matthew's own habits of phrasing elsewhere in this Gospel to judge whether this "or"/"and" shift carries real weight, or whether Matthew simply varies stock phrasing without intending a distinction a careful reader should notice?
4. Positions above reports three different readings of what 7:6 instructs and whether it continues 7:1-5's topic at all -- a reading connecting it to the "brother" of 7:1-5, a historical reading connecting it to the Eucharist via the Didache, and Nolland's reading connecting it instead to the Mammon theme of 6:24. What kind of evidence (a grammatical link to the surrounding verses, a documented first-century use of "dogs and pigs" language in a context closer to Matthew's own, something else) would actually settle which reading is right, rather than each reader preferring whichever one fits an assumption they already hold about how tightly the Sermon on the Mount's short units connect to each other?

## Sources and Limits

- **The claim attributed to "most scholars" in the Wikipedia article on Matthew 7:1 (that 7:1 does not forbid the discernment 7:6, 7:15, and 7:16 require) is the article's own unattributed synthesis, not a claim this lesson independently verified against named scholars' own writing.** This lesson could not locate a comparably substantive, named defense of the opposite reading (that 7:1 and 7:6/7:15/7:16 remain in real, unresolved tension) and does not invent one to balance the Positions section above; this is reported as a real limit on what this lesson found, not as proof no such defense exists.
- **The Didache's own text was not independently read for this lesson.** Its role in one of the three readings of Matthew 7:6 reported in Positions above (the Eucharist-restriction reading) is reported entirely through the Wikipedia article on Matthew 7:6's own characterization of it.
- **R.T. France's, Eduard Schweizer's, Ulrich Luz's, Harold Fowler's, Leon Morris's, Pseudo-Chrysostom's, John Nolland's, and Jerome's own commentaries and writings are reported here only through Wikipedia's tertiary-source summaries of them, not through their own primary texts, which were not independently located and read for this lesson.** Verify any of them directly before quoting further.
- **J.B. Lightfoot's and James Hastings's positions, cited by the Wikipedia article on "The Mote and the Beam," were not quoted directly in what this lesson's own research returned and are not asserted beyond naming that the article cites them.**
- **A web search surfaced a claim attributed to Gerhard Kittel (that "the Jewish negative formulation is... something other than the positive putting of it by Jesus") from a search-result summary rather than a direct fetch of a primary or citable secondary source.** This lesson could not verify that attribution against a source it actually read and has deliberately left it out of Positions above rather than report an unverified quotation as Kittel's own words.
- **Israel Abrahams's own specific reference to Paul's letters (characterized in this lesson's Positions section as giving "love its negative application") was not pinned down to a specific chapter and verse by this lesson.** Abrahams's essay was read directly, but the exact Pauline passage he has in mind was not independently identified or verified here.
- **The William Davidson Talmud translation's exact licence terms were not independently re-verified beyond Sefaria's own public API access.** This lesson quotes Hillel's saying directly from that translation via Sefaria's text API (not from a secondary report of it), but a full reading of Sefaria's own licensing terms for that specific translation was not separately carried out.
- **Whether 5:17's "the Law or the Prophets" and 7:12's "the essence of the Law and the Prophets" reflect a deliberate compositional choice by Matthew, or an incidental variation in stock phrasing, was not researched for this lesson and is left open at Questions to Carry above rather than asserted either way.**
- **This lesson's own BSB-quotation discipline: every quotation attributed to the BSB above was drafted only after a direct Node.js read of `web/public/bible/BSB/40.json` (and `42.json`, `45.json`, `59.json`, `62.json` for the Luke, Romans, James, and 1 John quotations used in Context, Positions, and the connection files), and a second, separate automated script then re-loaded the same files and confirmed every BSB-attributed quotation in this lesson and its four connection files is a literal, verbatim substring of the real verse text before this lesson was finalized** (see this lesson's handback report for the exact pass/fail count). This discipline caught, and corrected before any draft was written, several places where commonly remembered English wording (from the KJV or a popular modern paraphrase) differs from this app's own BSB text -- most notably at 7:1 ("or you will be judged," not "or you too will be judged"), 7:3-5 ("beam," not "plank"), 7:6 ("do not give dogs what is holy," not "do not give what is sacred to dogs"), 7:11 ("good things" for what the Father gives, distinct from "good gifts" for what human fathers give), and 7:12 ("the essence of the Law and the Prophets," not simply "the Law and the Prophets").
