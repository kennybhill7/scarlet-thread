Matthew 9 ended with Jesus telling his disciples to "Ask the Lord of the harvest, therefore, to send out workers into His harvest" (9:38, BSB). Chapter 10 opens with the answer: "calling His twelve disciples to Him, Jesus gave them authority" (10:1), and a few verses later "These twelve Jesus sent out" (10:5). This lesson covers the first part of the sending: the names of the Twelve (10:2-4), where they are to go and where not (10:5-6), what they are to say and do (10:7-8), what they may not take (10:9-10), and how they are to treat a house or town that receives them or refuses them (10:11-15). Two study skills are practiced here. The first is comparison. Four New Testament lists name the Twelve, and three Gospels give the equipment rules, so a reader can lay the texts side by side and see exactly where they agree and differ before turning to anyone's explanation. The second is holding a tension open. Jesus' order "Do not go onto the road of the Gentiles" (10:5) sits in the same Gospel as "make disciples of all nations" (28:19). Readers in several traditions have explained that tension, and this lesson reports them without choosing. Because these verses have at times been read as a verdict on whole peoples, the lesson also states what the text itself says and does not say.

## Context

**What the text says, in order (10:1-15).** Jesus gives "His twelve disciples" "authority over unclean spirits, so that they could drive them out and heal every disease and sickness" (10:1, BSB). Matthew then lists "the names of the twelve apostles" (10:2-4). Jesus sends them with instructions: "Do not go onto the road of the Gentiles or enter any town of the Samaritans. Go rather to the lost sheep of Israel" (10:5-6). They are to preach "The kingdom of heaven is near" (10:7) and to "Heal the sick, raise the dead, cleanse the lepers, drive out demons. Freely you have received; freely give" (10:8). They are not to "carry any gold or silver or copper in your belts", nor take "bag for the road, or second tunic, or sandals, or staff; for the worker is worthy of his provisions" (10:9-10). In each town they are to "find out who is worthy there and stay at his house until you move on" (10:11), greet the home, and "If the home is worthy, let your peace rest on it; but if it is not, let your peace return to you" (10:13). Where they are not welcomed: "shake the dust off your feet when you leave that home or town. Truly I tell you, it will be more bearable for Sodom and Gomorrah on the day of judgment than for that town" (10:14-15).

**The same work, handed on.** The phrase "every disease and sickness" appears in this app's BSB in exactly three verses: Matthew 4:23 and 9:35, the two summaries of Jesus' own work, and 10:1, where he gives the Twelve authority to do it. Their message in 10:7, "The kingdom of heaven is near", is word for word the message of John the Baptist (3:2) and of Jesus (4:17) without the opening "Repent". The four works in 10:8 match kinds of story Matthew has just told: a leper cleansed (8:2-3), demons driven out (8:28-32; 9:32-33), the sick healed (8:14-16), and a dead girl raised (9:25). These are this lesson's own observations, checked by script against the BSB. They are not findings attributed to any scholar.

**The four lists of the Twelve.** The New Testament names the Twelve four times: Matthew 10:2-4, Mark 3:16-19, Luke 6:14-16 and Acts 1:13 (which lists eleven, after Judas). Laid side by side in this app's BSB, they agree on more than the names. Simon Peter is always first, Philip always fifth, James son of Alphaeus always ninth, and in each Gospel list Judas Iscariot is last. The lists differ on one name. Matthew 10:3 and Mark 3:18 have "Thaddaeus". Luke 6:16 and Acts 1:13 have "Judas son of James", a name that appears in no other verse of this app's BSB. (John 14:22 mentions "Judas (not Iscariot)" among the disciples without saying whose son he was.) The two names are commonly harmonized as one man. As the Catena Aurea is reproduced in the Wikipedia article on Matthew 10:3, Remigius writes that "Thaddæus is the same whom Luke calls Jude of James", and Augustine, on manuscripts that read "Lebbaeus" here, asks, "whoever prevented the same man from having two, or even three different names?" The Wikipedia article on Jude the Apostle reports that this harmonization goes back to "many Christians since early times", and that "Some Biblical scholars reject this theory." It names three alternatives those scholars propose: "an unrecorded replacement of one for the other during the ministry of Jesus because of apostasy or death; the possibility that 'twelve' was a symbolic number and an estimation; or simply that the names were not recorded perfectly by the early church." Wikipedia does not name those scholars, and this lesson did not find them in their own words, so the alternatives are reported here only as Wikipedia gives them. The same Jude article notes a manuscript difference at 10:3. Some manuscripts, followed by the King James Version, read "Lebbaeus, whose surname was Thaddaeus". This app's BSB reads simply "Thaddaeus".

**"Matthew the tax collector."** Only Matthew's list adds a description to Matthew's name. A script over this app's BSB finds "Matthew the tax collector" only at 10:3; Mark 3:18, Luke 6:15 and Acts 1:13 have plain "Matthew". Matthew's Gospel is also the one that names the tax collector called at the booth "Matthew" (9:9), where Mark 2:14 and Luke 5:27 call him "Levi". Jerome, as the Catena is reproduced on Wikipedia, read the epithet as the author speaking of himself. The other Gospels "do not add, the Publican, that they should not seem to throw scorn upon the Evangelist by bringing to mind his former life. But writing of himself he both puts Thomas first in the pair, and styles himself the Publican." The word order Jerome notices can be checked in the BSB: "Thomas and Matthew" in Matthew 10:3, against "Matthew, Thomas" in Mark 3:18 and "Matthew and Thomas" in Luke 6:15. His reading assumes that the apostle wrote the Gospel, and that is itself disputed. The Wikipedia article on Matthew the Apostle reports that "Early Church tradition holds that the Gospel of Matthew was written by the apostle Matthew," that "Most scholars hold that the Gospel of Matthew was written anonymously, though the traditional authorship 'still has its defenders'," and that "The author is not named within the text." So "Matthew calls himself the tax collector" is one reading of 10:3, not a fact the verse states.

**"Apostles," once.** In this app's BSB, the word "apostle" appears in Matthew only at 10:2. Matthew calls the group "His twelve disciples" in 10:1 and "These twelve" in 10:5. The Wikipedia article on Matthew 10 notes the same shift in the Greek, from *tous dōdeka mathētas autou* to *tōn dōdeka apostolōn*, and that the verb in 10:5 is *apesteilen*, "sent forth". This lesson reports the Greek through that tertiary source and did not check it against a critical edition.

**"The lost sheep of Israel."** This app's BSB has "the lost sheep of Israel" at 10:6 and "the lost sheep of the house of Israel" at 15:24. The Greek of the two verses is the same: *ta probata ta apolōlota oikou Israēl* (Nestle 1904, via Biblehub). The shorter English at 10:6 is a translation choice; it does not reflect a different Greek text. Three things about the phrase can be checked. First, it recurs once in Matthew. At 15:24 Jesus uses it of his own mission, "I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel", when a Canaanite woman asks for help; that story belongs to a later lesson. Second, it echoes the Old Testament. "Lost sheep" occurs in only five verses of this app's BSB (Psalm 119:176, Jeremiah 50:6, Matthew 10:6 and 15:24, Luke 15:6). Jeremiah 50:6 is the one where a whole people is meant: "My people are lost sheep; their shepherds have led them astray" (BSB). Its Septuagint form (where it is numbered 27:6) uses the same two Greek words as Matthew, *probata apolōlota* (via Blue Letter Bible). The Pulpit Commentary says "The words recall Jeremiah 50:6 (27:6, LXX.)", and the Expositor's Greek Testament calls "the lost sheep" "an expression consecrated by prophetic use." (The connection attached to this lesson grades that link.) The previous lesson already traced Matthew 9:36's "sheep without a shepherd" to Ezekiel 34, whose shepherd promises "I will seek the lost" (34:16). That connection is not repeated here. Third, there is the question of what "lost" implies. The Expositor's Greek Testament says the word "was used by Jesus not in blame but in pity", meaning "neglected" rather than "finally and hopelessly given over to perdition". John Chrysostom (*Homily 32 on Matthew*, read directly) says Jesus "calls them lost, not stray, sheep, in every way contriving how to excuse them, and winning their mind to himself." In 10:6 the people called "lost sheep" are the ones the Twelve are sent to, and the Twelve themselves are Jews. The text does not use the phrase to condemn.

**What the Twelve may not take, in three Gospels.** The rules differ, and the differences can be checked in this app's BSB. Matthew: no "gold or silver or copper", no "bag for the road, or second tunic, or sandals, or staff" (10:9-10). Mark: "take nothing but a staff for the journey—no bread, no bag, no money in their belts—and to wear sandals, but not a second tunic" (6:8-9). Luke: "no staff, no bag, no bread, no money, no second tunic" (9:3). On the staff, Matthew and Luke forbid it and Mark allows it. On sandals, Matthew forbids them and Mark allows them, and Luke 9 does not mention them (Luke's later sending of the seventy-two says "Carry no purse or bag or sandals," 10:4). How readers have handled this is set out in Positions. Two smaller points: the BSB's "Do not carry" at 10:9 translates a Greek verb that commentators gloss as "acquire" or "provide" (see Positions), and the reason Matthew gives is "the worker is worthy of his provisions" (10:10). Luke 10:7 has "the worker is worthy of his wages", and 1 Timothy 5:18 quotes that saying in Luke's wording, introduced by "the Scripture says". Bengel notes that Matthew's Greek word, *trophēs*, means "food". Lesson 13 of this course used 10:9-10 as a picture of the Twelve's economic situation. That remains a description of one mission, and Luke records Jesus later reversing it: "Now, however... the one with a purse should take it" (Luke 22:36, BSB).

**Hospitality, and the dust.** The instructions assume that some households will take the messengers in. They are to stay with someone "worthy" (10:11) and leave their "peace" on the home (10:13). Where they are refused, they shake off the dust (10:14). In Acts the same gesture is recorded once, by Paul and Barnabas at Pisidian Antioch: "they shook the dust off their feet in protest against them" (Acts 13:51, BSB). At Corinth Paul "shook out his garments" (Acts 18:6). Nineteenth-century commentators explained the gesture from a Jewish purity rule about soil outside the land of Israel. Barnes: "The Jews taught uniformly that the dust of the Gentiles was impure, and was to be shaken off." The Pulpit Commentary cites Rashi on the Babylonian Talmud, *Shabbat* 15b, and quotes Edersheim. Two cautions apply. First, the rabbinic texts cited come from centuries after the Gospels. This lesson read them only as these commentators quote them, so their bearing on Jesus' saying is the commentators' inference. Second, the commentators drew from this background that the town "was to be considered and treated as if it were heathen" (Edersheim, quoted by the Pulpit Commentary). That reading carries a judgment on Gentiles as well as on the town. Matthew 10:14 itself says nothing about Gentiles or about defilement. It says only "shake the dust off your feet when you leave that home or town". Mark 6:11 and Luke 9:5 add that it is done "as a testimony against them".

**Sodom and Gomorrah.** The comparison in 10:15 names the two cities of Genesis 19, where "the LORD rained down sulfur and fire on Sodom and Gomorrah" (Genesis 19:24, BSB). In this app's BSB, "Gomorrah" appears in no other Gospel. Luke's parallel names only Sodom (10:12), and Mark 6:11 has no comparison. John Gill notes that the Sodom clause found in some copies of Mark 6:11 (and in the King James Version there) "is omitted in some copies, and so it is in the Vulgate Latin version", and suggests it "may perhaps be transcribed from" Matthew 10:15. What the saying compares is narrow. It compares the day of judgment for those cities with the day of judgment for a town that will not "welcome you or heed your words". Ellicott reads the point as responsibility measured by what a place received: the cities "were yet less guilty (as sinning less against light and knowledge)". Matthew 11:23-24 makes a similar comparison for Capernaum and gives its reason in Jesus' own words: "if the miracles that were performed in you had been performed in Sodom, it would have remained to this day." What Sodom's sin was is a separate question, handled in this course's Genesis 19 lesson. Nothing in Matthew 10:15 depends on answering it.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 10:1-15 is built. These are observations a reader can check, not claims about what Matthew meant by them.

**The harvest prayer, then the sending.** 9:37-38 ends with "Ask the Lord of the harvest... to send out workers", and 10:1 and 10:5 follow with Jesus calling and sending the Twelve. The disciples told to pray for workers become the workers (see the Practice Bridge of the previous lesson).

**Names in pairs.** Matthew's list is set out in twos joined by "and": "Simon, called Peter, and his brother Andrew; James son of Zebedee, and his brother John; Philip and Bartholomew; Thomas and Matthew the tax collector; James son of Alphaeus, and Thaddaeus; Simon the Zealot, and Judas Iscariot" (10:2-4, BSB). Mark's list is not paired this way, but Mark's sending has Jesus "send them out two by two" (6:7), a detail Matthew does not give. Whether the pairs in Matthew's list reflect that practice is not stated in the text.

**A narrowing sequence of places.** The instructions move from region ("the road of the Gentiles", "any town of the Samaritans", 10:5) to town ("Whatever town or village", 10:11) to house ("stay at his house", 10:11; "As you enter the home", 10:12), and then back out ("that home or town", 10:14; "that town", 10:15). Bengel noted the same three terms in Greek: "Ὁδὸν—πόλιν—οἴκον, way—city—house".

**A discourse with a closing formula.** The instructions continue through 10:42 and end with "After Jesus had finished instructing His twelve disciples" (11:1, BSB). That is close to the formula that ends the Sermon on the Mount ("When Jesus had finished saying these things," 7:28) and recurs at 13:53, 19:1 and 26:1. This lesson takes 10:1-15 only. 10:16-42 widens to persecution and is left to a later lesson.

## Positions

Two questions in this passage draw named, published disagreement. The first has the higher stakes and gets the fuller treatment. Neither is settled here.

**1. "Do not go onto the road of the Gentiles" (10:5) and "make disciples of all nations" (28:19): how do they fit?** The tension is in the text itself. In this app's BSB, 10:5 is the only verse in Matthew that mentions Samaritans. At the end of the Gospel, Jesus says, "Therefore go and make disciples of all nations" (28:19). Matthew has also already told of a Gentile centurion's faith (8:5-13) and of "many" who "will come from the east and the west" (8:11). Three readings are reported here, each in its own words.

*A temporary order, "to the Jew first."* Jerome, as the Catena Aurea is reproduced on Wikipedia, writes: "This passage does not contradict the command which He gave afterwards, Go and teach all nations; for this was before His resurrection, that was after." John Chrysostom (*Homily 32 on Matthew*, read directly) gives a pastoral reason for the order: "think not at all, says He, because they insult me, and call me demoniac, that I hate them and turn away from them. Nay, as I sought earnestly to amend them in the first place, so keeping you away from all the rest, to them do I send you as teachers and physicians." Ellicott, in the nineteenth century, gives three reasons why the order does not contradict 8:11: "(1) that the limitation was confined to the mission on which they were now sent; (2) that it did but recognise a divine order, the priority of Israel in God's dealing with mankind, 'to the Jew first, and also to the Gentile;' and (3) that the disciples themselves were as yet unfitted to enter on a work which required wider thoughts and hopes than they had yet attained." The phrase Ellicott quotes is Paul's in Romans 1:16, which this app's BSB renders "first to the Jew, then to the Greek." On this reading, 10:5-6 and 28:19 are two stages of one plan.

*An extension, not a replacement.* Amy-Jill Levine ("Matthew and Anti-Judaism," *Currents in Theology and Mission*, 2007, read directly) reads the two commands together from 28:19 back to 10:6: "The Great Commission is an extension of the mission, not an end to the old. Matthew 10:6 (see 15:24) insists that the mission is to 'the lost sheep of the house of Israel.' The resurrected Jesus does not say 'Make disciples of all the Gentiles and forget those Israelite sheep.'" She sets this inside a wider argument against reading Matthew as hostile to Jews. On 15:24 she objects to interpreters who "highlight Jesus' Jewish ethnocentrism", and to "A few critics" who "suggest that Matt 10:5b-6 and 15:24 are from a 'Jewish-Christian' source; they cannot imagine either Jesus or a 'full' Christian behaving this way." On this reading, 28:19 adds the nations without cancelling 10:6.

*Matthew's own shaping, and his community's concerns.* John Squires, writing with Elizabeth Raine (*Insights*, Uniting Church in Australia, 2023, read directly; a church-magazine piece, not a peer-reviewed article), reads the verses through how Matthew edited Mark. "The statement about going 'only to the lost sheep of Israel' (10:5–6), in the mission directives to the twelve disciples, is clearly an addition to the original Markan passage," and the same is said of 15:24. On 28:19: "This command correlates with nothing at all in the body of the Gospel, during the earthly period of Jesus' life. The mission to the Gentiles is an entirely post-resurrection phenomenon." They place the Gospel in a community that "was still immersed within its Jewish tradition," and conclude that "Remembering that Jesus said 'Go nowhere among the Gentiles' (10:5) makes perfect sense in this context." They add, as their own view, that the Gospel "might more accurately reflect the activity of the historical Jesus," for whom "Staying away from Gentiles and Samaritans would be a perfectly respectable course of action." On this reading, the tension tells us about Matthew's setting and sources as much as about a plan in two stages.

The three readings overlap more than their labels suggest. All three take the restriction in 10:5-6 as real and not something to explain away, and all three treat 28:19 as coming after the resurrection. They differ on what joins the two verses. For Jerome, Chrysostom and Ellicott, a divine order joins them, the Jews first and then the nations. For Levine, 28:19 extends a mission to Israel that remains in force. For Squires and Raine, the join is Matthew's editing of Mark for a community still inside Judaism. This lesson reports these readings and does not rank them.

**A caution on how these verses have been used.** Some readings of this chapter have turned it into a verdict on the Jewish people as a whole. One example comes from a few chapters later. On Matthew 19:28, John Gill (via the Biblehub aggregator) reads the apostles "judging the twelve tribes of Israel" as condemning the Jews "for their unbelief" and turning "from them to the Gentiles, under which judgment they continue to this day." Levine's 2007 article, cited above, is a sustained critique of that kind of reading of Matthew. The opposite slide also happens. The commentators' explanation of the dust in 10:14 treats Gentile land as defiling and so carries a judgment on Gentiles (see Context). What the text of 10:1-15 itself says can be checked. The Twelve are Jews, sent to Jewish towns. "Lost sheep" names those they are sent to help. The refusal in 10:14-15 is that of "anyone", a "home or town", not of a people. And Matthew's Gospel ends by sending the same disciples to "all nations" (28:19). Nothing in 10:1-15 states a judgment on Jews or Gentiles as peoples.

**2. The staff and the sandals: one instruction or two?** Matthew and Luke forbid a staff; Mark allows "a staff" and says to "wear sandals" (Mark 6:8-9), where Matthew forbids sandals (Context). Three named lines of reading are reported here.

Augustine (*Harmony of the Gospels* 2.30, read directly) names the question himself: "How comes it that Matthew and Luke have stated that the Lord said to His disciples that they were not to take a staff with them, whereas Mark puts the matter in this way: And He commanded them that they should take nothing for their journey, save a staff only". His answer is that "the staff which, according to Mark, was to be taken, bears one sense, and that the staff which, according to Matthew and Luke, was not to be taken with them, is to be interpreted in a different sense". The forbidden staff, "neither a staff", is "equivalent to not even the smallest things". The permitted staff stands for "that power which they received from the Lord", by which "even those things which were not carried with them would not be wanting to them." So, he concludes, "Both these counsels, therefore, must be accepted as having been spoken by the Lord to the apostles." He reads Mark's sandals in the same symbolic way: "the foot is to be neither covered, nor yet left bare to the ground".

A second line rests on the verb. Matthew 10:9 opens with *mē ktēsēsthe*, which the BSB renders "Do not carry". Bengel glosses it "do not procure", adding: "They were permitted to use what they already possessed, but not to procure any thing new." The Pulpit Commentary: "There is no connotation of foresight in the word itself, but only of acquisition. Observe that the apostles are not forbidden to take what they already have." On this reading, Matthew forbids acquiring a staff and sandals for the trip, while Mark permits the ones already in hand. Ellicott puts it this way: "They were to have none of the reserved comforts of common travellers, no second staff in case the first should break, no second pair of shoes". Barnes says of Mark's sandals: "Between him and Matthew there is an apparent contradiction, but there is really no difference."

A third line names the difference and does not resolve it. Heinrich Meyer calls Matthew's "nor a staff" "an unimportant variation from Mark 6:8." The Expositor's Greek Testament says of the rules generally: "Possibly we ought not to take these instructions too literally, but in their spirit."

What the readers share is the text's own reason, "the worker is worthy of his provisions" (10:10). Augustine leans on it, and Bengel and Barnes read the rules as teaching trust for this journey. They differ on whether the words themselves agree. Augustine says they agree once the two senses of "staff" are seen. The "acquire" readers say they agree once the verb is read closely. Meyer calls it a minor variation. It is worth noticing that this app's BSB translation ("Do not carry") hides the verb on which the second reading depends. This lesson reports the readings and does not rank them.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key; the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step. It is drawn from the hosts' side of the passage, not the messengers', so that no one reads it as a rule about owning or giving up possessions.

**Passage element.** Matthew 10:11-13: "Whatever town or village you enter, find out who is worthy there and stay at his house until you move on. As you enter the home, greet its occupants. If the home is worthy, let your peace rest on it; but if it is not, let your peace return to you" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the messengers carry nothing (10:9-10), so the plan depends on a household in each town taking them in. They are to stay in one house rather than move about, and they bring something to it, a greeting and "peace". Inference: for first hearers, receiving these travellers was a real cost to a household and a real act of welcome, and the instructions treat it as an exchange rather than a transaction, since the messengers were told "Freely you have received; freely give" (10:8). This step goes beyond the text's wording and is this lesson's own.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Welcoming someone who comes to serve, and receiving what they bring, is itself a part in the work, not just a background to it. And someone who comes to serve should not turn the welcome into a price.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: welcome given without being bought, and service given without being sold. Not carried forward: the specific equipment rules (which Luke 22:36 shows were not permanent even for the Twelve), the dust gesture, or any claim about who is "worthy" in a given case.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: instructions addressed to the Twelve for one mission, carried to a later reader by analogy and from the hosts' side, not addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none in 10:11-13. Later in the same discourse, Jesus says that whoever receives "a prophet because he is a prophet will receive a prophet's reward" (10:41). That reward is not specified, and this lesson does not read it as a promise of money, health, or any particular return for hosting.

**Modern domain and situation.** Church and community. A congregation is asked to house a small visiting team of volunteers who are coming for a week to help rebuild homes after a storm. A family with a spare room is weighing whether to host two of them.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: hospitality offered within real limits. One faithful response: the family agrees to host, sets out plainly what they can provide (a room, breakfast, a key), receives the volunteers' help and company as a gift rather than an obligation, and lets the church know honestly if it becomes too much.

**Cautions.** This passage gives no instruction to give up possessions, take a vow of poverty, or give money in order to receive a blessing, and nothing in this lesson should be read that way. If anyone presenting themselves as a messenger asks for money in exchange for prayer, healing, or blessing, 10:8 ("freely give") is a reason for caution, and a pastor is the right person to talk to. Hospitality is not owed at the cost of safety. A household should not host anyone it has reason to be unsafe with, and a person who needs shelter for their own safety, including from abuse, needs a local shelter, a hotline, or a pastor, not a study note. If hosting would strain a household's finances, the faithful response may be a smaller offer, or none.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Matthew and Mark have "Thaddaeus" where Luke and Acts have "Judas son of James". The one-man harmonization and the alternatives Wikipedia reports (a replacement, a symbolic "twelve", imperfect memory) are all possible. What kind of evidence, inside or outside the New Testament, would make one of them more likely than the others?
2. Jerome and Ellicott read 10:5-6 and 28:19 as stages. Levine reads 28:19 as an extension that does not cancel 10:6. Squires and Raine read the tension through Matthew's editing of Mark. Read 8:5-13, 15:21-28 and 28:16-20 in order. Which reading best accounts for all three passages, and which details does each reading leave hardest to explain?
3. Augustine resolves the staff difference by reading "staff" in two senses, and Bengel by reading the verb as "acquire". Meyer calls it an "unimportant variation". What would it take to show that a harmonization is the most natural reading of the texts, rather than a way of protecting a conclusion reached beforehand? And would a minor variation in wording matter to the point of the instructions?
4. Matthew joins the Twelve to "the twelve tribes of Israel" at 19:28, and Revelation 21:12-14 puts both sets of names on one city. This course's lens offers twelve tribes and twelve apostles as a parallel to test, not a settled claim. What would a reader need to show to move from "Matthew pairs the numbers" to a conclusion about how Israel and the church relate? Which texts would that reader have to deal with (Romans 9-11 is one)?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 10:1-15 in order: authority, names, where to go, what to say and do, what not to take, houses, and refusal. (b) Name the one apostle whose name differs between Matthew's list and Luke's, and give both names. (c) State one equipment difference between Matthew 10:10 and Mark 6:8-9.

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the sending, as the answer to 9:38, and the authority given in the same words as 4:23 and 9:35 (Context; Literary Design); (1:00-2:00) the four lists, the one difference in names, the harmonization and its alternatives, and "Matthew the tax collector" with Jerome's reading and the authorship caveat (Context); (2:00-3:30) 10:5-6 against 28:19, with at least two of the three readings in their own words, and what the text does and does not say about Jews and Gentiles (Positions 1); (3:30-4:30) the staff and sandals in three Gospels and two named ways of reading them (Positions 2); (4:30-5:00) the dust and Sodom, what the comparison does and does not say, ending on one open question.

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: "Reading 10:5-6 as Matthew's editing of Mark, as Squires and Raine do, makes Jesus' words into the evangelist's opinion." Before answering, ask whether a claim about how Matthew edited Mark is the same as a claim about whether Jesus said something, and note that Squires and Raine themselves suggest the restriction may reflect the historical Jesus. A second candidate, running the other way: "The 'Jews first' reading is a polite way of saying the Jews had their chance." Ask whether Jerome's, Chrysostom's or Ellicott's words, as quoted, say that, and set Levine's reading beside them.

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 10:5-6 restricts one mission, during Jesus' ministry. By itself it does not settle whether that restriction ends at 28:19, continues alongside it, or reflects Matthew's setting; that is the work of the positions above. (b) Matthew 10:15 compares the judgment of a refusing town with that of Sodom and Gomorrah. It does not say what Sodom's sin was. (c) Matthew 10:9-10 gives equipment rules for this journey. It does not set a rule for all believers about money or possessions, and Luke 22:36 records a later instruction that reverses it for the Twelve themselves.

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words in both texts:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mark 6:7-13 | parallel | strong | Same sending, same dust gesture; Mark allows a staff and sandals that Matthew forbids, and lacks the limit to Israel |
| Luke 6:13-16 | parallel | strong | Eleven names match; Luke has "Judas son of James" where Matthew has "Thaddaeus", and no "tax collector" |
| Jeremiah 50:6 | allusion | plausible | Same two Greek words as the Septuagint ("lost sheep" of a whole people), named by two commentaries; no citation, and Ezekiel 34 competes |
| Genesis 49:28 | motif | strong | Matthew pairs the Twelve with "the twelve tribes of Israel" at 19:28, and Sanders treats the call of the twelve as evidence of restoration hopes; what the pairing means is left open |
| Genesis 19:24-25 | explicit_reference | explicit | Jesus names "Sodom and Gomorrah"; the saying compares two judgments and does not describe Sodom's sin |

Or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`. Two candidates were considered and declined. Romans 1:16's "first to the Jew, then to the Greek" is often read beside 10:5-6, and Ellicott quotes it. But Paul does not cite Matthew, and authoring the link as a connection would build one of the three positions above into the thread itself. It is reported inside that position instead. Ezekiel 34:16 ("I will seek the lost") fits "lost sheep" in theme, but the previous lesson already carries an Ezekiel 34 connection from 9:36, and its rationale already names 10:6. A second row would duplicate the argument without new evidence. Say what new evidence would justify authoring either one.

## Sources and Limits

- **Every BSB quotation in this lesson and its five connection files** was printed from this app's own corpus (`web/public/bible/BSB/*.json`) before drafting and checked afterward by a script asserting that each quoted fragment is a literal substring of the cited verse. Quotations from other sources were checked the same way against saved copies of the pages read. The handback report gives the counts. Counts and "only" claims (the five "lost sheep" verses, "every disease and sickness" in three verses, "apostle" in Matthew only at 10:2, "Matthew the tax collector" only at 10:3, "Gomorrah" in no other Gospel, "Samaritan" in Matthew only at 10:5) were made by script over the same corpus and apply to this app's English text, not to the Greek.
- **Read directly:** Augustine's *Harmony of the Gospels* 2.30 and Chrysostom's *Homily 32 on Matthew* (New Advent, public-domain translations); Levine's 2007 article (text extracted from the hosted PDF); Squires and Raine's 2023 *Insights* article.
- **Read through an aggregator:** Ellicott, Barnes, Meyer, Bengel, Gill, Poole, the Pulpit Commentary and the Expositor's Greek Testament were read through Biblehub's commentary pages, saved and searched as text, not checked against printed editions. Edersheim, Rashi and the Talmud passage are known here only as the Pulpit Commentary and Gill quote them.
- **Read through Wikipedia (a tertiary source):** Jerome, Remigius and Augustine's Lebbaeus remark (as the Catena Aurea is reproduced on Wikipedia's articles on Matthew 10:3 and 10:5); the Thaddaeus harmonization and its alternatives (Wikipedia, "Jude the Apostle", which does not name the scholars who reject the harmonization); the authorship of Matthew (Wikipedia, "Matthew the Apostle"); the Greek terms in 10:1-5 (Wikipedia, "Matthew 10").
- **E. P. Sanders** is quoted from Early Christian Writings' reproduction of his list in *Jesus and Judaism* (1985), pp. 326-327, not from the book. The list names "the call of the twelve" and "the twelve tribes"; his fuller argument about the number twelve was not read.
- **Squires and Raine's article** contains one evident slip. It describes the material in chapters 5-9 as introducing a mission that "is to the Gentiles only (10:5)", where the surrounding paragraphs make clear the opposite is meant. Nothing in this lesson relies on that sentence. The article is a church-magazine reprint of a blog post, not peer-reviewed scholarship; a future pass should report the community/redaction reading from a peer-reviewed source as well. David C. Sim, "The Gospel of Matthew and the Gentiles," *JSNT* 17.57 (1995), is a likely candidate; its publisher page returned HTTP 403 and it is not cited.
- **The Greek and Septuagint observations** rest on Biblehub's Nestle 1904 pages for Matthew 10:6 and 15:24, and on Blue Letter Bible's Septuagint text of Jeremiah 50:6 (LXX 27:6), with Brenton's English checked on Biblehub. No critical edition was consulted. A first attempt to read LXX Jeremiah 27 on Biblehub's Swete page returned an incomplete chapter and was discarded. The Expositor's Greek Testament's reference reads "Jeremiah 50:6, Swete's ed., Matthew 27:6", evidently meaning LXX Jeremiah 27:6.
- **Not treated:** 10:16-42 (persecution, and the reward sayings of 10:40-42, apart from one mention in the Practice Bridge); the identity of Bartholomew, Simon "the Zealot", and James son of Alphaeus beyond their place in the lists; and whether the Israel-and-church question raised by 19:28 has a resolution. The lens this course uses deliberately does not decide that question, and neither does this lesson.
