Six days after Jesus told His disciples that some of them would "not taste death before they see the Son of Man coming in His kingdom" (16:28, BSB), He takes three of them up a mountain. "There He was transfigured before them. His face shone like the sun, and His clothes became as white as the light" (17:2). Moses and Elijah appear. A bright cloud covers them, and a voice says what it said at the Jordan, with three words added: "Listen to Him!" (17:5). On the way down, Jesus orders silence until He is raised, and the disciples ask about Elijah. The scene reaches back into the Old Testament in several ways, not equally strong, and this lesson grades each on its own evidence: a phrase that matches Deuteronomy 18:15, details that may recall Moses on Sinai in Exodus 24, and a single Greek verb in 17:11 that named readers trace to the Greek Malachi. It reports what Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant sources say about the light, the two visitors and the mountain, each in its own words. It does not choose among them. Every BSB quotation below was checked by script against this app's own Bible files.

## Context

**What happens, in order.** Jesus takes Peter, James and John "up a high mountain by themselves" (17:1) and is transfigured (17:2). "Suddenly Moses and Elijah appeared before them, talking with Jesus" (17:3). Peter offers to "put up three shelters" (17:4). A bright cloud covers them and the voice speaks (17:5). The disciples fall "facedown in terror"; Jesus touches them: "Get up ... Do not be afraid" (17:6-7). At the end "they saw no one except Jesus" (17:8). Coming down, He commands silence "until the Son of Man has been raised from the dead" (17:9). The disciples ask, "Why then do the scribes say that Elijah must come first?" (17:10). Jesus answers that Elijah "will restore all things," that "Elijah has already come," and that "In the same way, the Son of Man will suffer at their hands" (17:11-12). "Then the disciples understood that He was speaking to them about John the Baptist" (17:13).

**Where this sits.** Lesson 35 ("Peter's Confession; the Way of the Cross") ends at 16:28 and reports its readings without choosing; one of them, Chrysostom's, takes 16:28 to point to this scene. That question is not reopened here. Gregory Palamas, quoted in Positions, reads 16:28 the same way: the light is what Jesus there "terms the Glory of His Father and of His Kingdom." The three disciples are the three Jesus later takes aside in Gethsemane, "Peter and the two sons of Zebedee" (26:37), as the Catholic NABRE notes observe.

**What only Matthew has.** Compared in this app's BSB with Mark 9:2-13 and Luke 9:28-36, only Matthew says "His face shone like the sun" (Luke: "the appearance of His face changed," 9:29); only Matthew calls the cloud "bright"; only Matthew's voice includes "in whom I am well pleased" (Mark 9:7: "This is My beloved Son. Listen to Him!"; Luke 9:35: "This is My Son, whom I have chosen. Listen to Him!"); only Matthew has the disciples fall and Jesus touch them (17:6-7); and only Matthew calls it a "vision" (17:9). Matthew names "Moses and Elijah"; Mark has "Elijah and Moses" (9:4). The NABRE notes call the face clause and 17:6-7 "a Matthean addition" and say of "vision," "Matthew alone uses this word." Dale Allison, as Andrei Orlov quotes him, reads these changes together: "Matthew rescripted Mark in order to push thoughts towards Moses." One item on Allison's list does not hold in the editions checked here (see "Listen to Him!" below).

**"Transfigured" and "six days."** "Transfigured" occurs in the BSB only at Matthew 17:2 and Mark 9:2 (by script); the Greek is *metemorphōthē*. Ellicott notes that elsewhere in the New Testament the word "is used only in its spiritual sense, and is there rendered 'transformed'" (Romans 12:2; 2 Corinthians 3:18, "being transformed into His image"). "After six days" occurs in the BSB only at Matthew 17:1 and Mark 9:2; Luke has "About eight days after Jesus had said these things" (9:28). Calvin's harmonizing is typical: Matthew and Mark "reckon six entire days," while Luke includes "both the day on which Christ spake these words, and the day on which he was transfigured." Palamas reads the difference as meaningful: the vision "is the mystery of the Eighth Day, i.e., of the future age."

**Moses on Sinai: Exodus 24 and 34.** In Exodus 24, "the glory of the LORD settled on Mount Sinai. For six days the cloud covered it, and on the seventh day the LORD called to Moses from within the cloud" (24:16, BSB). In Exodus 34, Moses came down "unaware that his face had become radiant from speaking with the LORD," and the people "were afraid to approach him" (34:29-30).

- *Exodus 24: six days, mountain, cloud, voice.* The NABRE notes say the mountain is "possibly recalling the revelation to Moses on Mount Sinai" (Exodus 24:12-18). Orlov quotes Davies and Allison listing seven parallels, among them "a voice comes from the cloud (Exod 24.16; Mark 9.7)" and "the event takes place 'after six days' (Exod 24.16; Mark 9.2)," and Craig Evans: "the chronological notation 'after six days' recalls Exod 24:16." Orlov also reports, from Adela Yarbro Collins's survey, other explanations of the six days: Wellhausen's (the time between Jesus' death and His appearance in Galilee), a Sabbath reading, and Foster McCurley's "a Semitic idiom." He points to John McGuckin for "criticism of this hypothesis." None of the commentaries on 17:1 collected on Bible Hub mentions Exodus 24. The connection below grades this allusion and plausible.
- *Exodus 34: the face.* Barnes: "A similar appearance is described respecting Moses when he came down from the mount, Exodus 34:29-30." Meyer says the analogy "comes short": Moses' brightness "was the result of God's having appeared before him," while Christ's "proceeded from His own divine nature and life." Palamas makes a similar contrast (Positions 2). The BSB wording differs ("shone like the sun"; "radiant"); within Matthew the nearer echo is 13:43, "the righteous will shine like the sun." This one is left as a discussed candidate (Teach-Back 5).

**The shelters and the cloud.** Peter's "shelters" (17:4) are *skēnai*, "tents." The NABRE notes say the word "is used in the LXX for the booths" of Tabernacles and that "some scholars have suggested that there is an allusion here to that liturgical custom." The OCA's *The Orthodox Faith* goes further: "There is little doubt that Christ's transfiguration took place at the time of the Festival of Booths." For the cloud, Benson points to Exodus 40:34: "Then the cloud covered the Tent of Meeting, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle" (BSB). Chrysostom contrasts Sinai: "when He threatens, He shows a dark cloud—as on Mount Sinai ... so here, because it was His desire not to alarm, but to teach, it is a bright cloud."

**The voice: the baptism repeated.** "This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased" stands word for word at 3:17 and 17:5 in the BSB, and the Greek clause is the same in both verses in all nine editions Bible Hub lists (a script comparison). "Beloved Son" occurs in Matthew only in these two verses; outside them, "in whom I am well pleased" occurs in the BSB only at 2 Peter 1:17 (by script). Lesson 6 ("The Baptism of Jesus") weighs Psalm 2:7 and Isaiah 42:1 behind these words; that is not repeated here.

**"Listen to Him!" and Deuteronomy 18:15.** Moses says, "The LORD your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among your brothers. You must listen to him" (Deuteronomy 18:15, BSB). Acts 3:22 quotes it with a formula, "For Moses said," and applies it to Jesus: "You must listen to Him in everything He tells you." Matthew has no formula, and "listen to Him" occurs in twelve BSB verses (by script). The Greek differs twice. Matthew's verb is a present imperative (*akouete*); the Septuagint has a future, *autou akousesthe*, "him you shall hear," which Acts 3:22 keeps. And the word order splits by edition: the SBL, Nestle 1904, Westcott-Hort and Tischendorf texts read *akouete autou*, while the Byzantine, Greek Orthodox and both Textus Receptus texts read *autou akouete*, the Septuagint's order. Mark 9:7 splits the same way. Allison, as Orlov quotes him, says Matthew reversed Mark ("the order of akouete autou has been reversed") and that "the change to autou akouete strengthens the allusion to LXX Deut 18:15." In the nine editions checked here, Matthew and Mark agree in order within each edition, and Bible Hub's Westcott-Hort line marked with NA27 variants shows none at this point, so the reversal could not be confirmed; the edition Allison used was not identified. The allusion does not depend on it. The NABRE notes call the command "a reference to Dt 18:15," and Meyer, Benson and the Pulpit Commentary name it too.

**Elijah must come first (17:10-13).** The question rests on Malachi: "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and awesome Day of the LORD. And he will turn the hearts of the fathers to their children" (Malachi 4:5-6, BSB). Watch the numbering. These are 3:23-24 in the Hebrew (as Sefaria prints it), which is why the NABRE notes cite "Mal 3:23–24." In Swete's Septuagint on Bible Hub they are 4:4-5, with the reminder about Moses (BSB 4:4) moved after them. The BSB's "he will turn" hides a link the Greek shows. The Septuagint reads *hos apokatastēsei kardian patros pros huion*, "who will restore the heart of a father to a son," and Jesus' "he will restore all things" (17:11) is *apokatastēsei panta*, the same verb and form, in all nine editions on Bible Hub. Meyer calls it "an expression taken from the rendering of Malachi 4:6 by the LXX"; Bengel: "The same verb is used by the LXX. in Mal. 3:24." The Hebrew verb is *heshiv*, "turn back." In 17:12, "they did not recognize him, but have done to him whatever they wished"; Meyer reads the subject as the scribes of 17:10 ("The subject is the γραμματεῖς").

Lesson 22 ("John's Question from Prison") covers the first identification of John with Elijah (11:14) and the tension with John 1:21, and its connection (conn-matt11-elijah-malachi4) already notes the identification's return at 17:12-13. Neither is re-argued here. New in this passage: Elijah's suffering is set beside the Son of Man's ("In the same way," 17:12), and the disciples now understand (17:13). The NABRE notes suggest the exchange "may reflect later controversy with Jews who objected to the Christian claims for Jesus that Elijah had not yet come."

**2 Peter remembers the mountain.** The writer says, "we were eyewitnesses of His majesty. For He received honor and glory from God the Father when the voice came to Him from the Majestic Glory, saying, 'This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.' And we ourselves heard this voice from heaven when we were with Him on the holy mountain" (2 Peter 1:16-18, BSB). The Catechism cites "Mt 17:1-8 and parallels; 2 Pt 1:16-18" together (§554, note 292). "Holy mountain" occurs in the New Testament only there (by script). Who wrote 2 Peter is disputed; the Pulpit Commentary cites Edersheim: "even if this Epistle is not St. Peter's, it still would represent the most ancient tradition." That question is not taken up here.

**The mountain is not named.** Matthew says only "a high mountain" (17:1), as of the temptation at 4:8 ("a very high mountain"). This app's Places data has entries for Mount Tabor and Mount Hermon from their Old Testament mentions; neither is linked to Matthew 17, and this lesson adds none.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 17:1-13 is built, not on what the arrangement is meant to teach.

**Up and down.** The scene opens with Jesus leading the three "up a high mountain" (17:1) and turns at "As they were coming down the mountain" (17:9). What happens on the mountain is mostly seen and heard. What happens on the way down is talk: a command, a question, an answer.

**Three, then one.** Moses and Elijah appear "talking with Jesus" (17:3); Peter offers three shelters (17:4); at the end the disciples "saw no one except Jesus" (17:8). Chrysostom reads this as showing the words were said "concerning Christ only"; Calvin, that "the Law and the Prophets had a temporary glory." Those readings are theirs. The text reports only that the two were gone.

**Interrupted speech.** The voice comes "While Peter was still speaking" (17:5). Mark and Luke add a comment on Peter's confusion (Mark 9:6; Luke 9:33); Matthew has none.

**Glory between predictions of suffering.** The scene stands between the first prediction of the passion (16:21) and the second (17:22-23), and its last saying puts suffering back in view (17:12).

## Positions

Five questions get named, sourced answers: why Moses and Elijah appear, what the light was, which mountain it was, whether 17:11 speaks of John or of a future Elijah, and why Jesus orders silence. This lesson reports them and does not choose.

**1. Why Moses and Elijah?** Readers give several reasons, and many give more than one. The three most common:

*They stand for the Law and the Prophets.* Meyer: they, "as forerunners of the Messiah, represented the law and the prophets," citing Schoettgen and Wetstein; the Cambridge Bible agrees. Calvin's own reason is that the scene was "intended to demonstrate that Christ alone is the end of the Law and of the Prophets," though he has "no objection" to the common reason that Elijah stands for "all the Prophets." The Catechism says that "the Law and the Prophets had announced the Messiah's sufferings" (§555). The Orthodox Church in America's *The Orthodox Faith*: "These two figures actually stand for the Old Testament itself: Moses for the Law and Elijah for the Prophets."

*They are the two who met God on the mountain.* Cyril of Jerusalem (*Catechetical Lecture* 12.16) calls them "two witnesses, those who stood before Lord on Mount Sinai" (so the New Advent text reads): "Moses was in a cleft of the rock," citing Exodus 33:22, "and Elias was once in a cleft of the rock," citing 1 Kings 19:8. Bengel: each was "remarkable for revelations vouchsafed to him on Mount Sinai and Horeb." The Catechism: "Moses and Elijah had seen God's glory on the Mountain" (§555). Davies and Allison, as Orlov quotes them, call the two "the only OT figures of whom it is related that they spoke with God on Mount Sinai." In the BSB, Moses asks, "Please show me Your glory" (Exodus 33:18), and Elijah, at "Horeb, the mountain of God," hears "a still, small voice" (1 Kings 19:8, 12). *The Orthodox Faith* lists these passages as the feast's Vespers readings (Exodus 24:12-18; 33:11-34:8; 1 Kings 19:3-16).

*Their departures were unusual.* Ellicott: "The close of the ministry of each was not after the 'common death of all men.' No man knew of the sepulchre of Moses (Deuteronomy 34:6), and Elijah had passed away in the chariots and horses of fire (2Kings 2:11)." In the BSB, "no one to this day knows the location of his grave" (Deuteronomy 34:6), and Elijah "went up into heaven in a whirlwind" (2 Kings 2:11). Chrysostom: Christ shows that "He has power both of death and life," bringing forward "both him that had died, and him that never yet suffered this." *The Orthodox Faith*: the two "stand for the living and dead."

Chrysostom gives five reasons in all (Homily 56), including "the difference ... between the servants and the Lord," since some took Jesus for Elijah (16:14), and "To show the glory of the cross." Bengel adds that "Both of them are mentioned together in Malachi 4:4-5." These reasons are not rivals for most who give them; Calvin, Chrysostom and the Catechism each hold more than one.

**2. What was the light?** The sources below, from all three traditions, speak of the light as a showing of divine glory. They describe it in different terms.

*Eastern Orthodox.* The Feast of the Transfiguration is kept on 6 August, the Orthodox Church in America's "Second 'Feast of the Savior' in August." For its life of the feast, the OCA prints a discourse of St. Gregory Palamas (14th century). "This Light was the Light of the Divine Nature, and as such, it was Uncreated and Divine." Christ was transfigured, Palamas writes, "not taking upon Himself something new nor being changed into something new, nor something which formerly He did not possess. Rather, it was to show His disciples that which He already was, opening their eyes and bringing them from blindness to sight." Of Moses' shining face he writes that Moses "underwent a transfiguration. However, our Lord Jesus Christ possessed that Light Himself." The disciples saw it because they "passed beyond mere flesh into spirit through a transformation of their senses." He argues against unnamed opponents who reduce it to "something 'created,'" and ties it to prayer: it "is granted to all who, with constant exercise in efforts of virtue and prayer, strive with their mind towards God." The feast's Kontakion, as the OCA gives it, says the disciples saw His glory "so that when they would behold Thee crucified, they would understand that Thy suffering was voluntary."

*Roman Catholic.* The Catechism: "For a moment Jesus discloses his divine glory, confirming Peter's confession. He also reveals that he will have to go by the way of the cross at Jerusalem in order to 'enter into his glory'" (§555). It quotes Aquinas, "The whole Trinity appeared: the Father in the voice; the Son in the man; the Spirit in the shining cloud," and the same Byzantine Kontakion in its own translation: "so that when they should see you crucified they would understand that your Passion was voluntary" (§555, note 299). With Aquinas it calls the Transfiguration "the sacrament of the second regeneration," that is, "our own Resurrection," and "a foretaste of Christ's glorious coming"; with Augustine it answers Peter's wish to stay: "Go down to toil on earth, to serve on earth, to be scorned and crucified on earth" (§556).

*Protestant.* Calvin: the transfiguration "gave them a taste of his boundless glory, such as they were able to comprehend." It was "not a complete exhibition of the heavenly glory of Christ," but "under symbols which were adapted to the capacity of the flesh, he enabled them to taste in part what could not be fully comprehended." Its purpose was to show "that he was not dragged unwillingly to death, but that he came forward of his own accord." Calvin sets aside the reason that it was "to fortify them against the trial ... arising from his death"; Matthew Henry gives that very reason: "It was intended to support their faith, when they would have to witness his crucifixion." Meyer (Lutheran) holds that Christ's brightness "proceeded from His own divine nature and life, the δόξα of which radiated from within," and, citing Calovius, calls the change one of appearance, not substance ("non substantialis, sed accidentalis fuit transformatio").

Chrysostom, before all three, writes that Christ "discovered so much only of His brightness as they were able to bear." Where these statements agree and where they part is left to the learner (Questions 2).

**3. Which mountain?** Matthew does not say.

*Mount Tabor.* Cyril of Jerusalem, in the fourth century, speaks of Moses and Elias "being present with Him at His Transfiguration on Mount Tabor." Benson reports that Jerome knew "a tradition in his days, handed down from the times of the apostles, that this was mount Tabor," and the Pulpit Commentary says the tradition was then "generally adopted and upheld till the sixteenth century." Palamas speaks of "the Light of Tabor," and *The Orthodox Faith* names the mountain "by tradition Mount Tabor."

*A height of Hermon, near Caesarea Philippi* (16:13, the last place Matthew names). Meyer: "we may, with some probability, suppose that the height in question was one of the peaks of Hermon." Ellicott, the Cambridge Bible and the Pulpit Commentary agree, adding that Tabor's summit was "at this period occupied by a fortress" (Cambridge). The Pulpit Commentary still concludes that "more precise identification is impossible."

*No particular mountain intended.* The Catholic NABRE notes: "this has been identified with Tabor or Hermon, but probably no specific mountain was intended by the evangelist or by his Marcan source"; its meaning is "theological rather than geographical." 2 Peter calls it only "the holy mountain" (1:18).

**4. Does 17:11 speak of John, or of an Elijah still to come?** Jesus says, "Elijah does indeed come, and he will restore all things" (17:11), then, "But I tell you that Elijah has already come" (17:12). The present and future tenses of 17:11 are the crux.

*Fulfilled in John.* Calvin paraphrases Jesus' answer: "The promise ... that Eliah would come was true, and has been already fulfilled." He adds that "will restore all things" does "not mean that John the Baptist restored them perfectly, but that he conveyed and handed them over to Christ." Barnes: "He did not mean by this that Elijah was yet to come, for he tells them immediately Matthew 17:12 that he had come." Ellicott grants that it "would hardly be right to reject" the future reading "merely on the ground of its literalism," but holds that 17:12 "ought, it is believed, to be decisive," and reads 17:11's tenses as pointing to a lasting "Elijah ministry ... throughout the ages." Bengel reads the present tense as "midway between prediction and fulfilment."

*John for the first coming, Elijah himself before the second.* Chrysostom: "Elias indeed comes then, before my second advent; and now too is Elias come; so calling John." He points to Malachi's "Elias the Tishbite" and its warning of judgment as belonging to the second coming. Meyer lists Hilary, Augustine, Theophylact, Euthymius Zigabenus and "the majority of the older Catholic expositors" as holding this view. The Pulpit Commentary: "Christ is here alluding to his own second coming, which shall be preceded by the appearance of Elijah in person."

*Objections each reading faces.* Against the first, the Pulpit Commentary: "To regard ver. 12 as simply a correction of ver. 10 is to do violence to language, and to leave one half of Malachi's prediction unexplained." Against the second, Meyer holds that a coming of "the real Elias ... before the second advent" "is taught by Jesus neither here nor elsewhere," and Ellicott, as above, takes 17:12 as decisive. The tension with John 1:21 belongs to lesson 22.

**5. Why "Do not tell anyone ... until the Son of Man has been raised" (17:9)?**

*Because it would not be believed or understood until then.* Calvin: "the disciples would not have believed it, if Christ had not given a more striking proof of his glory in his resurrection." Bengel: "The glory of the resurrection rendered this previous manifestation more credible." The Catholic NABRE notes: "only in the light of Jesus' resurrection can the meaning of his life and mission be truly understood; until then no testimony to the vision will lead people to faith." Benson quotes Macknight: if published before the resurrection, "it might have appeared incredible."

*To avoid provoking opposition, or offense at the cross.* Barnes: telling it then "would have provoked the Jews and endangered his life. His time was not yet come." The Pulpit Commentary: the people "would have stumbled at the cross and Passion, which would seem no fitting sequel."

*A literary motif rather than a historical command.* In 1901 William Wrede drew attention to the commands to silence in Mark, among them Mark 9:9, the parallel to this verse. He proposed, as Wikipedia summarizes him (a tertiary source), that the theme "was not historical but was an addition by the author of Mark." Meyer had earlier noted a "mythical view" of 17:9. In Matthew, "tell anyone" commands occur at 8:4, 16:20 and 17:9 (by script).

*Objections each reading faces.* The first two readings take the command as historical, which is what Wrede denied. Wrede's reading meets its critics: the same article reports that William Sanday and Albert Schweitzer "reacted negatively," quotes G. E. Ladd ("a clever theory, but utterly lacking in evidence"), and says that "By the mid-1970s the Messianic Secret theory was no longer credited by scholars in the form that Wrede had proposed it." Calvin objects to a related reason, that the vision was to fortify the disciples before the cross: "why does he expressly forbid them to make known what they had seen till after his resurrection, but because the result of the vision would be later than his death?"

This lesson does not choose among these readings.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key. Your own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 17:4-5. Peter proposes a plan: "If You wish, I will put up three shelters." "While Peter was still speaking," the voice says, "This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased. Listen to Him!" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the voice interrupts Peter's plan with a command to listen. Six days earlier Peter had answered the prediction of suffering with "This shall never happen to You!" (16:22). Inference, the lesson's own: for a reader coming from 16:21-23, the voice turns Peter from planning to hearing, and what Jesus says next concerns His suffering (17:9, 12). Chrysostom draws a similar line: "Hear ye Him. So that although He choose to be crucified, you are not to oppose Him."

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When something weighty happens, the first need may be to hear what is being said before acting on one's own plan, especially when it is hard to hear.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: hearing before building; willingness to hear an unwelcome word. Not carried forward: the vision, the shelters, or any claim that a modern person hears a voice from a cloud. "Listen to Him" is said of Jesus alone; it transfers no authority to anyone who claims to speak for Him.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a command to the three disciples about Jesus, carried over by analogy to a habit of attention. Promise scope: none; the passage promises no reward or outcome for listening.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family. A grown son learns that his father's illness is serious. At the first family meeting he arrives with a full plan for care, housing and money, and starts presenting it before his father has spoken.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: attentive restraint. One faithful response: he sets the plan aside, asks his father what he wants and fears, hears the answers he does not want too, and brings the plan back later as a draft, not a decision.

**Cautions.** This is not medical, legal or financial advice; decisions about serious illness and care belong with the patient's own wishes, his doctors, and, where needed, a lawyer or financial counselor. "Listen" here does not mean doing whatever one is told. Someone being pressured, threatened or harmed by a family member is not asked by this passage to stay silent or comply.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. The connection to Deuteronomy 18:15 is graded strong, and the connection to Exodus 24 plausible. Both rest partly on Moses' presence in the scene. What, in the words of each text, makes the difference between the two grades? Would you grade either differently?
2. Palamas calls the light "Uncreated and Divine"; Calvin speaks of "symbols which were adapted to the capacity of the flesh"; the Catechism says Jesus "discloses his divine glory." What do these statements share, and where exactly do they part? Is anything in 17:1-8 itself able to decide between them?
3. In 17:12 Jesus sets John's fate beside His own: "In the same way, the Son of Man will suffer." How does that line change what the disciples have just seen on the mountain?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 17:1-13 in order, from the climb to 17:13. (b) Quote the voice of 17:5 in the BSB's own words, and say which part repeats 3:17. (c) Name three details only Matthew has, compared with Mark and Luke.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) the scene and what only Matthew has; (1:00-2:00) Moses and Elijah, two reasons with a named source each; (2:00-3:00) the voice: 3:17 repeated, and "Listen to Him" with Deuteronomy 18:15 and the edition split; (3:00-4:00) the light in Orthodox, Catholic and Protestant words, and the mountain; (4:00-5:00) Elijah, the verb from Malachi, and the two readings of 17:11.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: "Matthew is obviously presenting Jesus as a new Moses; every detail points to Sinai." Before answering, list which details have a named reader behind them. A second, running the other way: "The Moses links are modern inventions." Before answering, look at Cyril of Jerusalem and Bengel on the mountain, and Meyer and Benson on Deuteronomy 18:15.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 17:1-13 does not name the mountain; Tabor, Hermon and "no specific mountain" are readers' answers. (b) The passage does not say what the light was made of, or whether it was seen with ordinary eyes; the text says His face "shone like the sun" and calls the event a "vision" (17:9). (c) The passage does not say what Moses and Elijah talked about. Luke says they "spoke about His departure" (Luke 9:31); Matthew and Mark do not report it.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words of both texts. The five attached to this lesson:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matthew 3:17 | motif | strong | The voice repeats "This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased" word for word, in the BSB and in all nine Greek editions |
| Deuteronomy 18:15 | allusion | strong | "Listen to Him" matches "You must listen to him"; Moses is present; Acts 3:22 applies the verse to Jesus; named Catholic and Protestant readers; no formula, and the Greek verb differs |
| 2 Peter 1:16-18 | explicit_reference | explicit | Quotes the voice and places the writer "with Him on the holy mountain" |
| Exodus 24:15-18 | allusion | plausible | Six days, mountain, cloud and voice; argued by Davies and Allison and by Evans (secondhand), questioned by others; no older commentary on Bible Hub mentions it |
| Malachi 4:6 | allusion | strong | "restore" in 17:11 is the Septuagint's verb for Elijah's work (apokatastēsei); Meyer and Bengel name the source |

The Deuteronomy, Exodus and Malachi rows are this lesson's thread connections to the Old Testament; none has a citation formula, so none is graded quotation. Two candidates were not authored. **Exodus 34:29-35** (Moses' face) is named by Barnes and, secondhand, by Allison, but the BSB wording differs ("radiant" against "shone like the sun"), and Meyer and Palamas each stress a difference in kind; it is left as a candidate. **Leviticus 23:39-43** (Booths behind the "shelters") is suggested by unnamed scholars in the NABRE notes and asserted by *The Orthodox Faith*; no named scholar's argument was read, so it is `no_warrant_yet`.

## Sources and Limits

- **Read directly, with raw copies saved:** every source named here, except those marked secondhand below: the Bible Hub commentary pages on Matthew 17:1, 17:2, 17:3, 17:5, 17:9, 17:11 and 17:12 and 2 Peter 1:16; Calvin's Harmony on Matthew 17; Chrysostom's Homilies 56 and 57; Cyril's Lecture 12; the NABRE notes; Catechism §§554-556 with their vatican.va footnotes; the two OCA pages; the Bible Hub Greek and Septuagint pages; and Sefaria's Hebrew of Malachi 3:22-24.
- **Secondhand only.** Allison (*The New Moses*), Davies and Allison, Evans, Yarbro Collins, Wellhausen, McCurley and McGuckin are reported through Andrei Orlov's "Which Moses?"; the PDF does not print where it was published. Jerome and Macknight come through Benson, Schoettgen and Wetstein through Meyer, Edersheim through the Pulpit Commentary, Aquinas and Augustine through the Catechism. Wrede is reported only through Wikipedia (tertiary), whose article also contains an inconsistent sentence about Wrede that is not used here.
- **Orthodox sources.** Palamas is read in the OCA's English, whose translator is not named; the opponents he argues against, and the controversy behind the homily, were not researched. *The Orthodox Faith* page is unsigned. No Greek Orthodox Archdiocese page and no Orthodox commentary on Matthew (for example Theophylact's) was read. Critiques of each tradition's view of the light were not researched, so Positions 2 has no objections paragraph.
- **Editions.** Greek comparisons rest on the nine editions Bible Hub prints; NA28 was not checked directly.
- **Bible Hub reproduces public-domain commentaries without page numbers;** attributions follow its labels. Some older commentaries on this chapter use disparaging language about Judaism (the Pulpit Commentary's "Spurious, degraded Judaism") or about Catholics (Calvin); it is not reproduced. Matthew 17:12's "they" are, on Meyer's reading, the scribes of 17:10.
- **Not researched:** whether the event was a vision or an outward event (Meyer surveys Tertullian and others); Daniel 7 and 10 behind the clothes and the fear (cited in the NABRE notes); Luke's "departure" (9:31).
- **Counts and quotations.** Every count or "only" claim came from a case-insensitive, word-boundary Node script over this app's BSB files, with its output kept. Every BSB quotation in this lesson and its five connection files was checked by script against `web/public/bible/BSB/*.json`.
