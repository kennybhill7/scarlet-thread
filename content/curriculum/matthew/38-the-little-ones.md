Matthew 18 is a block of Jesus' teaching addressed to His disciples, and it begins with a question about rank: "Who then is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven?" (18:1, BSB). Jesus answers by placing "a little child" among the disciples (18:2) and saying, "unless you change and become like little children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven" (18:3). The rest of 18:1-14 follows from that child: welcoming such a child (18:5), a warning about causing "one of these little ones who believe in Me to stumble" (18:6), a woe on "the man through whom they come" (18:7), cutting off hand, foot and eye (18:8-9), "their angels in heaven" (18:10), and a man who leaves ninety-nine sheep to look for one (18:12-14). This lesson tracks the repeated words, checks an empty verse in the app's Bible, compares Mark, Luke and Matthew's own Sermon on the Mount, and reports four questions on which named readers disagree. Every BSB quotation below was checked by script against the app's own Bible files.

*If this passage touches your own life.* Matthew 18:6 is often quoted about harm done to children, including harm done inside churches. If a child is in danger now, call 911 or your local emergency number. In the United States and Canada, the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline answers at any hour: call 1-800-422-4453, text "GO" to the same number, or chat at childhelphotline.org. Childhelp describes it as "a safe, nonjudgmental space for anyone concerned about or affected by child abuse," and says plainly that "It is not a reporting line and is not connected to 911 or emergency services"; its site lists each state's reporting contacts. In Canada, contact your provincial or territorial child-protection service. Elsewhere, contact local emergency or child-protection services. A study note is not the place to work out what to do about harm; a trained person is.

## Context

**What happens, in order (18:1-14).** The disciples ask who is greatest (18:1). Jesus has "a little child" stand among them (18:2) and answers with three sayings about the child: change and become like children (18:3), "whoever humbles himself like this little child is the greatest" (18:4), and "whoever welcomes a little child like this in My name welcomes Me" (18:5). Then come the warnings: the millstone (18:6), the woe (18:7), hand, foot and eye (18:8-9). "See that you do not look down on any of these little ones" (18:10) leads to the parable of the stray sheep (18:12-13), which ends, "your Father in heaven is not willing that any of these little ones should perish" (18:14). The next unit, on a brother who sins (18:15-35), belongs to this curriculum's next lesson.

**Where the passage sits.** Jesus has just foretold His death again (17:22-23; compare 16:21), and the disciples "were deeply grieved." Calvin notes the timing: after that prediction, "they immediately enter into a dispute about the highest rank." Mark tells the same scene with more detail and with John's report about another exorcist between the child and the millstone (Mark 9:33-48; see Teach-Back 5). Luke has the millstone and the woe in another setting (Luke 17:1-2) and the stray sheep in a third (Luke 15:3-7).

**"Little child," "little ones."** In Matthew's BSB "little child" or "little children" occurs in seven verses: 11:25, 18:2-5 and 19:13-14 (a case-insensitive script search). "Little ones" occurs in Matthew only at 10:42, 18:6, 18:10 and 18:14. The Greek words differ: the child of 18:2-5 is *paidion*; "these little ones" in 18:6, 10 and 14 is *ton mikron touton*, "these small ones" (all nine Greek editions on Bible Hub). Matthew 10:42 already used the phrase of disciples: "one of these little ones because he is My disciple." Whether the "little ones" of chapter 18 are the child, people like the child, or both is Positions 2.

**One root behind four English words (18:6-9).** The Greek root *skandal-* occurs six times in these four verses: the verb *skandalizo* in 18:6, 8 and 9, and the noun *skandalon* three times in 18:7 (SBL and Byzantine texts, by script). Rabanus, quoted in the Catena Aurea, describes a *skandalon* as "a stumbling-block, or a fall, or hitting of the foot." The BSB renders the six words as "stumble" (18:6), "causes of sin" and "stumbling blocks" (18:7), "they" (the third noun, "through whom they come"), and "causes you to sin" (18:8, 9). In English, "stumble" occurs only once in Matthew 18. A reader of the BSB alone would not see that the hand and eye sayings continue the word of 18:6.

**The millstone (18:6).** The Greek is *mylos onikos*, a "donkey millstone"; the BSB has "large millstone." The Expositor's Greek Testament: "a millstone driven by an ass, i.e., a large one, as distinct from smaller-sized ones driven by the hand." In the BSB "large millstone" occurs only in Matthew 18:6 and Mark 9:42, and "millstone" in twelve verses in all (script searches). Readers differ on where the image comes from. Jerome, in the Catena Aurea, says Jesus "speaks according to the custom of the province; for among the Jews this was the punishment of the greater criminals." Gill answers, "Jerom thinks, that this was a sort of punishment in use among the Jews ... but this does not appear," and lists the four capital punishments of the Mishnah. Meyer writes that drowning "was not a Jewish method of putting to death" but "belonged to the Greeks, Romans, Syrians, and Phoenicians"; Ellicott that it "was not recognised in the Jewish law" but had been inflicted by Augustus, adding that "it is not improbable that the Romans had inflicted it" in Galilee, as Jerome said. Calvin calls it "the punishment then reckoned the most appalling, and which was inflicted on the most atrocious malefactors." The saying itself is a comparison: "it would be better for him" (18:6). Chrysostom reads the comparison as pointing past the image: Jesus "said not, A millstone shall be hanged about his neck, but, It would be better for him to undergo this; implying that another evil, more grievous than this, awaits him."

**Who has quoted 18:6 about harm to children.** Two examples, from sources read for this lesson. Pope Francis, in a 2014 homily at a Mass with people abused by clergy: "What Jesus says about those who cause scandal applies to all of us: the millstone and the sea (cf. Mt 18:6)." He quoted 18:6-7 again in his closing address to the 2019 Vatican meeting on the protection of minors. The Southern Baptist Convention's 2013 resolution "On Sexual Abuse Of Children" cites Matthew 18:3-6 and Mark 10:13-14 for the statement that Jesus "demonstrated His love for children by encouraging them to follow Him, correcting those who hindered them, and condemning those who harmed them." This lesson reports that the verse is quoted this way. It does not assess how any church has handled abuse.

**The woe (18:7).** "Woe to the world for the causes of sin. These stumbling blocks must come, but woe to the man through whom they come!" Calvin reports two ways to take "Woe to the world": as a curse on "the authors of offenses," or as Jesus lamenting "the evils which he perceives to be rapidly coming on the world"; he prefers the second. Chrysostom, as the Catena Aurea excerpts his Homily 59, answers the objection that "must come" removes choice: Jesus "foreshews what must come to pass," and "it is not done because He foretold it."

*A misuse to name.* One early reading, preserved in the Catena Aurea, turns these verses against the Jewish people. Hilary of Poitiers reads the millstone saying as aimed at Jews who offend the apostles, and takes "the man through whom they come" (18:7) to mean the Jewish people, as those who brought about the offence of Christ's passion. The speaker, His disciples and the first hearers were all Jews. In the same Catena, Jerome applies the woe in particular to Judas. The Second Vatican Council answered this charge directly: "what happened in His passion cannot be charged against all the Jews, without distinction, then alive, nor against the Jews of today." It continued: "Although the Church is the new people of God, the Jews should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God, as if this followed from the Holy Scriptures" (*Nostra Aetate* 4, 1965). Nothing in 18:6-7 names a people.

**Hand, foot and eye (18:8-9).** These verses repeat Matthew 5:29-30 with changes: 18:8 adds the foot, "right" drops out of both sayings, and the setting moves from lust (5:28) to causing others to stumble (see the connection to Matthew 5). How readers take the sayings is Positions 4. The BSB footnote to 18:9 notes that "the fire of hell" is, in Greek, "the Gehenna of fire."

**An empty verse (18:11).** In the app's BSB file, Matthew 18:11 is an empty string; the text goes from 18:10 to 18:12. A script over the whole file finds sixteen such empty verses, among them Matthew 17:21 and 23:14. On Bible Hub's parallel page, 18:11 has text in four editions and none in five. The Byzantine Majority Text (2005), the Greek Orthodox text (1904) and both Textus Receptus editions (Scrivener 1894, Stephanus 1550) read *elthen gar ho huios tou anthropou sosai to apololos*, "For the Son of Man came to save the lost." The SBL, Nestle 1904, Westcott-Hort, Westcott-Hort/NA27 and Tischendorf texts have nothing. None of the nine has the "seek and" of Luke 19:10 ("the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost," BSB). The BSB's own footnote on Bible Hub, printed at 18:10, reads: "BYZ and TR include 11For the Son of Man came to save the lost; see Luke 19:10." The app's shipped file stores verse text only, with no footnotes, so a learner sees an empty verse with no explanation.

Commentators read on Bible Hub take both sides. The Expositor's Greek Testament calls the verse "an interpolation from Luke 19:10," and Meyer omits it as "not genuine." The Cambridge Bible notes that it "is omitted in the Sinaitic and the Vatican MSS." but adds that "it falls in precisely with the train of thought, and is almost required to connect Matthew 18:10; Matthew 18:12." The Pulpit Commentary lists the omitting witnesses and editors, then notes the verse "is retained in many good uncials, nearly all the cursives, the Vulgate, Syriac, etc.," and objects to the interpolation theory: "one does not see why, if this is the case, the inter-polater should have left out the striking verb 'to seek,' which would naturally have coincided with 'seeketh' in ver. 12." It concludes: "For expository use, at any rate, we may consider the verse as genuine." Chrysostom's Homily 59 quotes the verse and comments on it ("He signifies even the cross"), so his text had it. No critical apparatus was read for this lesson.

**The stray sheep (18:12-14).** The parable is told to disciples about "these little ones." In Greek the sheep "goes astray" (*planethe*) and the man seeks "the one going astray" (*to planomenon*); the BSB renders the second as "the one that is lost." "Astray" occurs in Matthew's BSB only in 18:12-13 (script search). Only at the end does Matthew use the "perish" root that Luke uses for the sheep throughout: "not willing that any of these little ones should perish" (*apoletai*, 18:14). The editions split on one word in 18:14: the SBL, Nestle, Byzantine, Greek Orthodox, Tischendorf and Textus Receptus texts read "your Father," Westcott-Hort reads "My Father," and Bible Hub's Westcott-Hort/NA27 line prints both; the BSB has "your Father." Jerome, in the Catena, reads 18:14 back to 18:10: "this parable was set forth to enforce that same saying." Luke's version and the Ezekiel 34 background are graded in the connections (Teach-Back 5).

## Literary Design

Notes on how 18:1-14 is built, not on what it is meant to teach.

**"One of these little ones" as a frame.** The phrase opens the warning (18:6), returns at 18:10, and closes the parable (18:14). In each, the SBL text uses the number "one" (*hena*, *henos*, *hen*), and the parable is about one sheep out of a hundred. Bengel, as the Expositor's Greek Testament quotes him: "frequens unius in hoc capite mentio," the frequent mention of "one" in this chapter.

**A child at the start, a sheep at the end.** The unit opens with a child set "among them" (18:2) and ends with ninety-nine sheep left "on the hills" while the man searches for one (18:12). Between them the sayings turn from the child as a model (18:3-4), to the child as someone to welcome (18:5), to the little ones as people who can be harmed (18:6-10). Whether that turn means the "little ones" are the child, or the childlike, is the subject of Positions 2.

## Positions

Four questions in this passage draw named, published disagreement. None is settled here. Each reading is given in its holders' own words where a source was read directly, and marked as secondhand where it was not.

**1. What about a child is held up as the model (18:3-4)?** Matthew's text gives one clue: "whoever humbles himself like this little child" (18:4). Readers fill it in differently, and many combine several qualities.

*Freedom from rank and ambition.* Calvin: "as children know nothing about being preferred to each other, or about contending for the highest rank, Christ desires that their example should banish from the minds of his followers those eager longings after distinction." Chrysostom (Homily 58): the child is "pure" from envy, "from vainglory, and from longing for the first place." The Expositor's Greek Testament: "like the children, in unpretentiousness. A king's child has no more thought of greatness than a beggar's."

*Innocence.* Jerome, in the Catena Aurea: "He does not enjoin on the Apostles the age, but the innocence of infants." Ellicott: the disciples should "regain, in this respect, the relative blamelessness of children."

*Trust and dependence.* Matthew Henry lists several marks, among them that children "are teachable, and willingly dependent on their parents." Hilary, in the Catena: children "trust what is told them, and believe what they hear to be true."

*Low status.* Some modern readers point to the child's place in society. Precept Austin, quoting R. T. France, says children were not taken seriously "except as a responsibility, one to be looked after, not one to be looked up to," and quotes France that "humbles himself" means "the acceptance of an inferior position." D. A. Carson, as the same page quotes him: "The child is held up as an ideal, not of innocence, purity, or faith, but of humility and unconcern for social status." Alan Jacobs, in his essay "A Bible Fit for Children" (quoted by *Today in the Word*, via Precept Austin): "in most societies children do not have the full rights and privileges of adults; they are not free agents, they are under the authority of their elders."

**Objections each reading faces.** Against innocence, Calvin: "We know that in children there are many things faulty," and Barnes says Jesus "did not intend to express any opinion about the native moral character of children." Against trust or faith as the point, Carson's (via Precept Austin) sentence above excludes "faith" by name. Against a purely inward humility, France's (via Precept Austin) "acceptance of an inferior position" points to a social place, not only a feeling. Against the low-status reading, Meyer describes the condition as "to acquire a moral disposition similar to the nature of little children," a change of character, not of standing. Calvin's own caution applies to every reading: "comparisons must not be too closely or too exactly carried out, so as to apply at all points." This lesson does not choose.

**2. Who are "these little ones" (18:6, 10, 14)?**

*Disciples of any age who are lowly.* Meyer: "not to be understood, any more than παιδίον τοιοῦτο, Matthew 18:5, of literal children," but as "a way of designating modest, simple-minded, unassuming believers." Gill: "Not in age, but are little and mean in their own eyes, and contemptible in the esteem of the world." Chrysostom, on 18:5: "by a little child, here, He means the men that are thus simple and lowly, and abject and contemptible in the judgment of the common sort."

*Children and the childlike together.* Ellicott on 18:10: the words "include among the 'little ones,' the child-like as well as children." The Pulpit Commentary on 18:6: "Whether child or adult, a pure, simple soul." Bengel: "Children are more easily impressible; therefore they are more easily injured," and "Jesus paid great attention to little children, and endued them with faith."

*New or weak believers.* Origen, in the Catena Aurea: "The little ones are those that are but lately born in Christ, or those who abide without advance, as though lately born."

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the first, Ellicott notes that at 18:10 "The child was still there, perhaps still folded in the arms of Jesus," and Bengel's comment on "who believe" ties the phrase to children. Against the second, Gill argues that "which believe in me ... cannot be said of infants," and Meyer warns against using the verse "as proof of the faith of little children." Against the third, Origen reports the reading that the little one is "a perfect Christian," though he finds it does not fit 18:6 ("this exposition does not seem to agree with that which was said"). Chrysostom's "abject and contemptible" names status, not newness of faith. This lesson does not choose. The church statements quoted in Context apply 18:6 to harm done to children. This lesson does not say how each reading bears on that use.

**3. What are "their angels" who "always see the face of My Father" (18:10)?** "Their angels" occurs only here in the BSB, and "face of My Father" only here (script searches). In Matthew's BSB "angel" or "angels" occurs in nineteen verses, but nowhere else are angels said to belong to human beings.

*Each person has a guardian angel.* The Catechism of the Catholic Church (336): "From its beginning until death, human life is surrounded by their watchful care and intercession. 'Beside each believer stands an angel as protector and shepherd leading him to life.'" Jerome, in the Catena: "High dignity of souls, that each from its birth has an Angel set in charge over it!" Origen, in the same place, reports two views: an angel given from baptism, or, for "those who are foreknown of God," from birth. Among Protestant commentators, Meyer holds that "The belief that every individual has a guardian angel ... is here confirmed by Jesus," and the Pulpit Commentary calls it the "doctrine ... that each soul has assigned to it by God a special angel."

*Angels guard believers together, not one each.* Calvin affirms "angels, who are ministers of their salvation," but says: "The interpretation given to this passage by some commentators, as if God assigned to each believer his own angel, does not rest on solid grounds," since Scripture "declares that the angels encamp around (Psalm 34:7) the godly, and that not one angel only, but many, have been commissioned to guard every one of the faithful." Poole: "Our Saviour doth not say their several and respective angels, but their angels"; the individual view "hath no patronage from this text." Barnes: "He does not mean, I suppose, to state that every good man has his guardian angel, as many of the Jews believed; but that the angels were, in general, the guards of his followers."

*Jewish background.* Both sides point to Jewish belief about angels; the texts below can be read directly, though the Talmud was compiled centuries after Matthew, and the Targum cited below is also later than Matthew. In Shabbat 119b, Rabbi Yosei bar Yehuda says: "Two ministering angels accompany a person on Shabbat evening from the synagogue to his home, one good angel and one evil angel." In Chagigah 16a, Rabbi Zerika says that "The two ministering angels who accompany him, i.e., each individual," testify against him, citing Psalm 91:11 ("For He will command His angels concerning you to guard you in all your ways," BSB). Ludwig Blau and Kaufmann Kohler, in *The Jewish Encyclopedia* (1901-1906), write that in the Hebrew Bible "Guardian angels are mentioned, but not, as was later the case, as guardian spirits of individuals and nations," and later that "Every man has a special guardian angel, according to Targ. Yer. Gen. xxxiii. 10." They connect these spirits with Matthew 18:10 and Acts 12:15, and suggest they "resemble the Persian fravashis, and were probably modeled after them," while noting that "The accompanying angels are probably not identical with the guardian spirits." Several commentators read "see the face" as court language for servants admitted to a king; Meyer cites 2 Kings 25:19 and Tobit 12:15.

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the one-angel-each reading, Calvin's and Poole's objections are quoted above. Against the collective reading, Meyer holds that the individual belief "is here confirmed by Jesus," and the Pulpit Commentary holds the individual doctrine "grounded on this" verse. Gregory the Great, quoted in the Catena, answers a question both readings face, how angels can both see God and serve people: "by a spiritual presence they come forth to us, and yet by internal contemplation ... keep themselves there whence they come forth." This lesson does not choose, and the Eastern Orthodox teaching on angels was not located in a source read here.

**4. How do readers take cutting off hand, foot and eye (18:8-9)?**

*The person's own impulses or senses.* Calvin takes the verses "as added for the purpose of amplification": "we would rather choose to pluck out our eyes, or cut off our hands, than give encouragement to offenses." Meyer: "the σκάνδαλα occasioned from without operate through the senses, and thereby seduce into evil." Barnes says the maimed body of 18:8 is said "for the purpose of carrying out or making complete the figure."

*Relationships that lead into sin.* Chrysostom (Homily 59): "not saying these things of limbs; far from it; but of friends, of relations, whom we regard in the rank of necessary members." Jerome, in the Catena: "all affection, our whole kindred, are severed from us; lest under cover of duty any believer should be exposed to offence." The Pulpit Commentary names both: "Literally, the hand or foot leads into sin," while "Metaphorically, the expression signifies all that is as dear and as necessary as these important members," including persons.

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the first, Chrysostom's "not saying these things of limbs" excludes the body as the point. Against the second, Meyer reads the warning as about temptations that come "through the senses," and Calvin applies it to one's own eyes and hands. All the readers quoted here take the words as figures; none reads them as a command to injure the body. This lesson does not choose.

*A safety note.* One misuse is documented. A 2012 medical review (Schwerkoske, Caplan and Benford, *Psychosomatics*) found sixteen published cases of self-injury prompted by Bible verses; its abstract says the four verses involved are "all from the Gospel of Matthew" (the abstract does not name them) and that "All patients presented with a diagnosis of psychosis at the time of the event." If you, or someone you know, feel that a verse is telling you to harm your body, treat it as an emergency: in the US call or text 988 (the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), which counsels "anytime, day or night," or call your local emergency number. Any reading of these sayings, about one's own impulses or about relationships, can be pressed on someone by another person. If anyone is using these verses to tell you to harm yourself or to cut you off from family or outside help, a counselor or pastor outside that situation can help. A reader working any of this through for their own life needs a pastor or counselor, not a study note.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, a model of the shape and not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 18:10: "See that you do not look down on any of these little ones. For I tell you that their angels in heaven always see the face of My Father in heaven." (BSB)

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the warning comes to disciples who have just asked who is greatest (18:1), and it gives a reason from heaven: the little ones have angels who see the Father's face. Inference (this lesson's): for these disciples, the people they were tempted to rank as least were the ones the Father was watching over.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** How a group treats the person with the least standing in it is a test of whether it has stopped asking who is greatest.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: noticing who is overlooked, and refusing to treat low standing as low worth. Not carried forward: a doctrine of angels (Positions 3 is open), or a claim that any particular person is protected from harm.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a command ("See that you do not look down") with a stated reason. Promise scope: it promises nothing about outcomes for any reader; it tells disciples how to regard "these little ones."

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. On a construction crew, a new laborer who speaks little English is given the worst tasks and left out of the morning safety briefing, which is held quickly in English. The foreman's crew jokes that he "doesn't need to know."

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: regard turned into practice. One faithful response: a crew member asks the foreman to slow the briefing, finds a coworker who can translate, and makes sure the new man knows the site's hazards and whom to call.

**Cautions.** This is not legal or workplace-safety advice; follow the employer's safety rules and, in the US, OSHA requirements. The verse is not a reason for anyone to accept mistreatment quietly in the name of being "little." If a child, or anyone, is being harmed, the referrals near the top of this lesson apply. This bridge is a worked example for the learner to test, not counsel for any particular person.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose.

1. Matthew 18:4 says "humbles himself like this little child." Is the child humble, or simply low? Does your answer change what 18:3 asks of an adult?
2. The child of 18:2-5 is *paidion*; "these little ones" in 18:6-14 is *mikroi*. If Matthew meant only children, why change words? If he meant only disciples, why keep the child in view?
3. The BSB renders one Greek root four ways in 18:6-9. What does the reader of English gain and lose?
4. Matthew 18:11 is empty in the app's Bible. How should a reader treat a verse that some editions have and others lack? Does it matter that the verse's content appears in Luke 19:10?
5. In Matthew's Greek the sheep "goes astray"; in Luke it is "lost." Does the change of verb fit the change of audience?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 18:1-14 in order, from the question to "should perish." (b) Name the Greek root that runs through 18:6-9 and how many times it occurs. (c) Say why 18:11 is empty in the app.

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) the question and the child, with Positions 1; (1:00-2:00) "little ones" and the millstone, with Positions 2 and the safety referral; (2:00-3:00) the woe, the misuse, and hand and eye beside 5:29-30; (3:00-4:00) "their angels" and Positions 3; (4:00-5:00) 18:11 and the stray sheep beside Luke 15.

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own and answer it. Form your own first. If you are stuck, here is one: "If the little ones are believers rather than literal children (Meyer, Gill), how, if at all, does 18:6 bear on harm done to a child?" (Check: what Ellicott, the Pulpit Commentary and Bengel say, who is standing among the disciples in 18:2-5, and how the church statements in Context use the verse.) A second runs the other way: "Since the little ones are children, 18:6-14 says nothing to adults about how they treat one another." (Check: 10:42, 18:4, and Chrysostom's "the men that are thus simple and lowly.")

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 18:10 does not say whether each person has one angel; it says "their angels." (b) The passage does not name a people as "the man through whom they come" (18:7). (c) The passage does not settle whether 18:11 belongs to Matthew's text; the editions divide.

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words in both texts:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mark 9:33-48 | parallel | strong | The same sequence; "large millstone" and "who believe in Me" occur only in Matthew 18:6 and Mark 9:42 in the BSB |
| Matthew 5:29-30 | motif | strong | The same eye and hand sayings, repeated within Matthew, with the foot added and the setting changed |
| Luke 15:3-7 | parallel | strong | The same parable in the same order, told to a different audience with a different verb |
| Ezekiel 34:11-16 | allusion | plausible | The Septuagint of 34:16 puts "seek," "the lost" and "the straying" together; no citation formula |

The Ezekiel 34 row is this lesson's Old Testament thread, and it is only plausible: Matthew quotes no clause of Ezekiel. Defend one row, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`. Three candidates were considered and not authored. Luke 17:1-2 shares the woe and the millstone but sits in another setting, and the Mark row already carries that saying. Psalm 91:11, which Chagigah 16a cites for accompanying angels, is not quoted or echoed in Matthew 18:10's wording. Revelation 18:21, where an angel casts "a stone the size of a great millstone" into the sea against Babylon, shares an image but not a saying.
