John the Baptist last appeared in Matthew at the river, announcing someone stronger who would come after him (3:11). In chapter 11 he is in prison, and he sends a question: "Are You the One who was to come, or should we look for someone else?" (11:3, BSB). Jesus answers with a list of what is happening, in words that sound like Isaiah. Then he turns to the crowd and says who John is, first with a quotation ("it is written," 11:10) and then with a name ("he is the Elijah who was to come," 11:14). So the passage shows three ways of pointing back to the prophets: an echo without a citation, a quotation that blends two texts, and a name. Each is graded differently below and checked in the Greek, because here the English sometimes hides a connection and sometimes invents one. Two hard questions, 11:12 on violence and the Elijah saying against John 1:21, are reported as open, with named readers on each side.

## Context

**What the text says, in order (11:1-19).** After Jesus "had finished instructing His twelve disciples, He went on from there to teach and preach in their cities" (11:1, BSB). "Meanwhile John heard in prison about the works of Christ, and he sent his disciples to ask Him, 'Are You the One who was to come, or should we look for someone else?'" (11:2-3). Jesus replies: "Go back and report to John what you hear and see: The blind receive sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the good news is preached to the poor. Blessed is the one who does not fall away on account of Me" (11:4-6). As the messengers leave, he asks the crowds three times what they went into the wilderness to see, ending "A prophet? Yes, I tell you, and more than a prophet" (11:7-9). He quotes Scripture about John (11:10), calls him the greatest "among those born of women" (11:11), speaks of the kingdom and violence (11:12-13), and names him "the Elijah who was to come" (11:14). He ends with "children sitting in the marketplaces" who complain, "We played the flute for you, and you did not dance; we sang a dirge, and you did not mourn" (11:16-17). John was said to have "a demon"; of the Son of Man they said, "Look at this glutton and drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and sinners!" The last line is: "But wisdom is vindicated by her actions" (11:18-19).

**Where John is.** Matthew has said only that John "had been imprisoned" (4:12, BSB); the reason, Herodias, comes later (14:3-4).

**"The One who was to come."** John's phrase is *ho erchomenos*, "the coming one," the same participle he used at the river: *ho de opiso mou erchomenos*, "the one coming after me" (3:11). The BSB's "after me will come One more powerful than I" hides the echo. What John meant by asking is taken up in Positions 1.

**The answer and Isaiah (11:5).** Jesus does not say "it is written" here. He reports six things happening, and several of them use Isaiah's language. The match is closer in the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Old Testament, than in English, and it is spread across more than one Isaiah passage.

- *Isaiah 35:5-6.* "Then the eyes of the blind will be opened and the ears of the deaf unstopped. Then the lame will leap like a deer" (BSB). The Septuagint has *typhlon* (blind), *kophon* (deaf), *cholos* (lame), against Matthew's *typhloi*, *kophoi*, *choloi*. The deaf clause matches in verb as well as noun: Septuagint *ota kophon akousontai*, "ears of the deaf will hear," against Matthew's *kophoi akouousin*, "the deaf hear." The verbs for the blind and the lame are different words. The Cambridge Bible notes that the Isaiah 35 passage "describes the work of God, who 'will come and save you.'" That is the language of 35:4: "your God will come ... He will come to save you" (BSB).
- *Isaiah 61:1.* "the LORD has anointed Me to preach good news to the poor" (BSB). The Septuagint has *euangelisasthai ptochois*, and Matthew has *ptochoi euangelizontai*: the same verb root and the same noun. The Septuagint of 61:1 also contains *typhlois anablepsin*, "recovery of sight to the blind," a phrase not in the Hebrew text the BSB translates (the BSB has "freedom to the prisoners"). Matthew's first clause, *typhloi anablepousin*, uses that same root. The Pulpit Commentary (via the Biblehub aggregator) puts it this way: "the words are taken from the LXX." Luke 4:17-21 quotes this verse with a formula. Jesus "found the place where it was written" and reads it, including "recovery of sight to the blind" (Luke 4:18, BSB), and says, "Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing" (4:21). Isaiah 61:1 also promises "to proclaim liberty to the captives" (BSB), which Jesus's answer to a prisoner does not include.
- *Isaiah 29:18-19 and 26:19.* The Septuagint of 29:18-19 matches about as closely as 35:5-6. It has the deaf, the blind and the poor (*akousontai ... kophoi*, *ophthalmoi typhlon opsontai*, *ptochoi*), with *kophoi* in the same form as Matthew's and the same verb, "hear." In the BSB, "the deaf will hear the words of the scroll, and out of the deep darkness the eyes of the blind will see" (29:18). Isaiah 26:19 ("Your dead will live; their bodies will rise," BSB) shares only Matthew's "dead ... raised": the Septuagint has *nekroi* and *egerthesontai*, Matthew *nekroi egeirontai*.
- *Lepers.* No verse in this app's BSB Isaiah names lepers or leprosy (a script search). Whether 35:8 ("The unclean will not travel it," BSB) or 53:4 lies behind that item is not settled here. At 53:4 the Latin Vulgate reads *putavimus eum quasi leprosum*, "we thought him as a leper," where the BSB has "we considered Him stricken by God."

Older commentators name these sources without a formula to go on. Meyer (via the Biblehub aggregator) calls Jesus's words "an echo of Isaiah 35:5 f., 8, Isaiah 61:1 ff." The Expositor's Greek Testament notes that *kophoi* means "deaf" here, "though in Matthew 9:32-33, it means dumb, showing that the prophecy, Isaiah 35:5, is in the speaker's thoughts."

**A parallel outside the Bible.** One Dead Sea Scroll, 4Q521, also pairs raising the dead with good news for the poor. In the translation Wikipedia credits to Gaye Strathearn (2011): "he will make the dead live, he will proclaim good news to the poor." The article reports "a striking similarity between Luke 7:22," the parallel to Matthew 11:5, "and Line 12 of this fragment" (its citation markup there is malformed). This is a tertiary report; see Sources and Limits.

**The quotation about the messenger (11:10).** Here Jesus does use a formula: "This is the one about whom it is written: 'Behold, I will send My messenger ahead of You, who will prepare Your way before You'" (BSB). Set the three related texts side by side in this app's BSB:

- Malachi 3:1: "Behold, I will send My messenger, who will prepare the way before Me."
- Exodus 23:20: "Behold, I am sending an angel before you to protect you along the way and to bring you to the place I have prepared."
- Matthew 11:10: "Behold, I will send My messenger ahead of You, who will prepare Your way before You."

In English, Matthew looks like Malachi with "before Me" (Hebrew *lefanai*) turned to "before You." The Greek changes the picture. Matthew's first clause, *Idou ego apostello ton angelon mou pro prosopou sou*, matches the Septuagint of Exodus 23:20 word for word after its opening "and." The Septuagint of Malachi 3:1 has a different verb (*exapostello*) and "before My face" (*pro prosopou mou*). Craig Blomberg (*Criswell Theological Review*, 1987) states it directly: "The first clause of this quotation parallels the LXX of Exod 23:20 exactly, but the second finds no equivalent there." Blomberg counts "eight consecutive paralleled words" for the Synoptic clause, treating *ego* as uncertain. With *ego*, as every edition of Matthew 11:10 on Biblehub has it, the run is nine.

Matthew's second clause ("who will prepare Your way") follows Malachi's sense. Ellicott calls the quotation "a free translation from the Hebrew," and Meyer calls it "a free rendering of the Hebrew and not from the LXX." Neither of their notes on 11:10 mentions Exodus. Luke 7:27 has the same quotation in the same BSB words. Mark 1:2 has a shorter form, introduced by "As it is written in Isaiah the prophet." In the critical editions Biblehub lists, Mark 1:2 lacks *ego*; the Byzantine and Received Text editions include it. Why the pronoun changed is Positions 4. (Lesson 5 deferred the Malachi 3:1 connection to this verse.)

**"Least."** Ellicott and Meyer both note that the Greek behind the BSB's "the least" (11:11), *mikroteros*, is a comparative, "lesser," a point that matters in Positions 2.

**"The Prophets and the Law."** In this app's whole BSB, that order occurs only at 11:13, against five occurrences of "the Law and the Prophets" (a script search). Luke has a related saying elsewhere: "The Law and the Prophets were proclaimed until John. Since that time, the gospel of the kingdom of God is being preached, and everyone is forcing his way into it" (Luke 16:16, BSB). The Pulpit Commentary calls it "curious" that Luke's version of this speech (7:24-35) should "omit our vers. 12-14"; Luke 7 never names Elijah.

**Elijah (11:14).** Malachi ends with "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and awesome Day of the LORD. And he will turn the hearts of the fathers to their children, and the hearts of the children to their fathers" (Malachi 4:5-6, BSB). The Septuagint of that verse says "Elijah the Tishbite" (*Elian ton Thesbiten*), naming the historical prophet. How widespread a pre-Christian belief in Elijah as the Messiah's forerunner was is itself debated. Blomberg reports recent studies "which take their starting point from the claim that no unambiguous evidence exists for a pre-Christian Jewish belief in Elijah as a Messianic forerunner." He points especially to M. M. Faierstein (*Journal of Biblical Literature*, 1981) and cites J. A. Fitzmyer (1985); neither article was read for this lesson. Within Matthew the identification comes back after the Transfiguration: "Elijah has already come ... Then the disciples understood that He was speaking to them about John the Baptist" (17:12-13, BSB). John's Elijah-like clothing (3:4) is treated in lesson 5.

**The children in the marketplace (11:16-19).** Luke 7:31-35 has the same picture with small, checkable differences of wording (for example "you did not weep" for "you did not mourn"). The largest is the last line. Matthew has "wisdom is vindicated by her actions," and Luke has "wisdom is vindicated by all her children" (Luke 7:35). Matthew's Greek editions are themselves divided here. Biblehub's parallel editions show *ergon*, "works," in Westcott and Hort and the Byzantine Majority Text, and *teknon*, "children," in Tischendorf and Stephanus's Textus Receptus. The Pulpit Commentary reports *ergon* "with the Sinaitic manuscript and the original hand of the Vatican" and says "The common reading, τέκνων, has come from Luke." The BSB follows "works" (rendered "actions").

**Who is "Wisdom"?** Older commentators differ. Gill offers several options, "or rather Christ himself, who is the wisdom of God." The Pulpit Commentary takes it as "the Divine wisdom, by which all creation was made (Proverbs 8:22-31; Wisd. 7:22)." Among recent scholars, Francois Viljoen (*In die Skriflig*, 2024), building on M. Jack Suggs (1970, not read here), argues that "The deeds of σοφία (Mt 11:19) are the deeds of Christ (Mt 11:2)." He also argues that "Matthew identifies Jesus not only as the child of Wisdom as Luke does, but as σοφία itself," and proposes that the saying "reflects Sirach 4:11 ... and Proverbs 8:32." Viljoen reports that Cotter and Beare treat the line as an independent proverb. He reports that Hagner calls it that too, yet also writes that "Jesus' deeds, like the deeds of Wisdom, are right and good and will ultimately vindicate him" (both via Viljoen). Other readers he reports are listed in Teach-Back 5.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 11:1-19 is built. These are claims about the text's shape, not about what that shape is meant to teach.

**"Works" at both ends.** John hears about "the works of Christ" (11:2), and the passage ends with wisdom "vindicated by her actions" (11:19). The BSB uses two English words, but in the reading the BSB follows the Greek noun is the same: *ta erga tou Christou* at 11:2 and *apo ton ergon autes* at 11:19 (Tischendorf and the Textus Receptus have *teknon* at 11:19 instead). Viljoen draws on the repetition (see Context); what it means is the open question.

**An echo that is only in English.** The BSB uses "who was to come" twice in this passage: John asks "Are You the One who was to come" (11:3), and Jesus calls John "the Elijah who was to come" (11:14). A script search finds that phrase nowhere else in Matthew's BSB text. The Greek differs, though: *ho erchomenos* at 11:3, and *ho mellon erchesthai*, "the one about to come," at 11:14. The repetition belongs to the translation.

**Three questions, then a turn.** "What did you go out ... to see?" comes three times (11:7, 8, 9; a script count). The first two expect "no"; the third gets "Yes, I tell you, and more than a prophet." The quotation in 11:10 then explains the "more."

## Positions

Five questions in this passage draw named, published disagreement. None is settled here. Each reading is given in its holders' words where a source was read directly, and marked as secondhand where it was not.

**1. Why did John send the question (11:2-3)?** Meyer's commentary sorts the main readings into three, and the sources read for this lesson fit them. (Meyer names a further view that he rejects, and the Expositor's Greek Testament reports another, "incipient faith.")

*John himself was in real doubt.* Meyer holds that "the Baptist's question proceeded from real doubt as to whether Jesus was the ἐρχόμενος, yea or nay." The doubt, in his view, "cannot be regarded as showing a want of spirituality," but "only as a temporary eclipse of his settled conviction, which, owing to human infirmity, had yielded to the influence of despondency." The Pulpit Commentary: "The question was brought from John; the answer is sent back to him (ver. 4). This points to the cause of the question lying ultimately, not with his disciples, but with himself." Ellicott, on 11:6, says Jesus's closing words "confirm the view that the question which the messengers had brought came from the Baptist himself." The Expositor's Greek Testament calls this view "common among modern commentators" and says it "has everything in its favour."

*John asked for his disciples' sake.* John Chrysostom (Homily 36 on Matthew, read directly) holds that "neither did he send as being himself in doubt, nor did he ask in ignorance." His reason is that "John's disciples were starting aside from Jesus," so John sent them to see for themselves. The Expositor's Greek Testament calls this view "universal with the fathers," Tertullian excepted. Meyer lists Origen, Augustine, Jerome, Hilary, Luther, Calvin and Bengel among its holders (secondhand).

*John was urging Jesus to act.* Meyer reports a third reading, which sees the question as "an expression of impatience, and an indirect challenge to the Messiah to establish His kingdom without delay." He names Lightfoot, Michaelis, Kuinoel, Fritzsche and Hase as its holders (secondhand).

Each reading can point to something in the text. The first can point to the answer being sent back to John (11:4). The second can point to "what you hear and see" (11:4): the messengers witness for themselves. The third can point to the setting, a prisoner waiting. The text records the question and the answer; it does not record John's response. This lesson does not decide between the readings.

A note for any reader carrying a question like John's for themselves: this lesson reports how commentators have read one man's question. It cannot weigh a reader's own season of doubt, and it is not meant to. Someone working through a crisis of faith should talk with a pastor or another trusted person in their church, not rely on a study note.

**2. Who is "the least in the kingdom of heaven" (11:11)?** Four readings are on record.

*Jesus himself.* Chrysostom (Homily 37, read directly) holds that Jesus is speaking of himself: "when He is speaking of Himself, He naturally conceals His person." Meyer summarizes Chrysostom's sense in Greek: lesser in age and in the opinion of many (*kata ten helikian kai kata ten pollon doxan*), yet in fact greater. Meyer lists Hilary, Erasmus, Luther and Melanchthon among those who read it this way (secondhand). Ellicott answers that "It would be but a poor truism to have declared that the King was greater than the herald," and that "there is no example of our Lord's so speaking of Himself elsewhere."

*Any member of the kingdom.* Ellicott reads it of disciples, the "little ones" of Matthew 10:42. They are less than John "in fame, work, the rigour of ascetic holiness," yet "above him in the knowledge of the truth." The Pulpit Commentary: "The weakest Christian is greater in privileges than the greatest of the Old Testament saints."

*The apostles and preachers.* Gill reads it "of the apostles of Christ, and the least among them," who could preach a death and resurrection John could only foretell. Poole reports "Mr. Calvin and many others" reading "the least of those who shall preach the gospel after my resurrection." Chrysostom, who holds the first reading, answers this one: "what sort of connection has it, to speak either of angels or of apostles? And besides, if He were speaking of the apostles, what hindered his bringing them forward by name?"

*One lower in the coming kingdom compared with John's place in the old order.* Meyer takes the comparative at face value: "he who stands lower in the kingdom of the Messiah, stands (according to the divine standard) higher than he." He insists this does not shut John out of the kingdom: "I confess that I am at a loss to comprehend how one can understand ver. 11 in such a way as to exclude ... the Baptist from the kingdom of heaven."

**3. What does 11:12 mean?** The BSB reads: "the kingdom of heaven has been subject to violence, and the violent lay claim to it." The Greek verb *biazetai* can be passive ("is treated with violence, is forced") or middle ("forces its way"), and the noun *biastai* means "violent people." Meyer, the Expositor's Greek Testament and Ellicott all note both grammatical options. Three readings are on record, and Meyer names the holders of each.

*Eager people press in.* The kingdom is "taken by storm" by people who want it badly. Meyer: "that eager, irresistible striving and struggling after the approaching Messianic kingdom," with "those who use violent efforts" dragging it "to themselves." Chrysostom (Homily 37, read directly): "who has so taken it? Tell me. All who approach it with earnestness of mind." Ellicott: "There is no thought of hostile purpose in the words." Jerome, in Aquinas's *Catena Aurea* as reproduced by Wikipedia (tertiary), reads it the same way: "great indeed is the violence, when we who are born of earth, seek an abode in heaven." This reading can point to Luke 16:16, "everyone is forcing his way into it" (BSB), which Meyer and the Pulpit Commentary both cite. Some holders name who the eager are; see Sources and Limits.

*Opponents attack it.* Meyer reports that Lightfoot, Schneckenburger and Hilgenfeld read the verse as "a hostile violence with which the Messianic kingdom is persecuted," with *harpazousin* meaning that opponents "snatch it away from men" (secondhand). Gill lists this as a live option: "the Gospel's suffering violence by the persecutions of its enemies." This reading can point to the setting, with John in prison (11:2) and "this generation" rejecting both men (11:16-19); that observation is this lesson's own. Meyer, who holds the first reading, calls this one "partly an anachronism."

*The kingdom forces its way forward.* Bengel takes the verb as middle: the kingdom "pushes itself forward as it were by violence." Meyer lists Melanchthon, Baur and Zyro alongside him (secondhand). Bengel writes that "There is no complaint here of hostile force, for the complaint begins at Matthew 11:16." The Expositor's Greek Testament grants that the verb "might be middle voice," but holds that "the passive is demanded by the noun following."

This lesson reports all three and ranks none.

**4. "Before You" in place of "before Me" (11:10): why the change?** In Malachi 3:1 the messenger prepares the way "before Me," that is, before God, who is speaking. In Matthew 11:10 he goes "ahead of You" and prepares "Your way." The readers below agree that the change is real. They disagree about what accounts for it.

*A deliberate christological application.* Bengel: "It is one of the strongest arguments for the divinity of Christ, that those things which are said of Christ in the New Testament are quoted from the Old Testament, where they are predicated as exclusively belonging to God." Ellicott: "In the original it is Jehovah Himself who speaks of His own coming ... In the Evangelist's paraphrase it is Jehovah who speaks to the Christ."

*The wording of Exodus 23:20.* In a footnote, Blomberg holds that the Exodus phrasing, "rather than any innovative, christological interpretation of Mal 3:1 most naturally accounts for the shift in person of the pronouns." He offers two ways the texts came together. "Malachi's prophecy may deliberately echo the Pentateuchal text," or a later rabbinic pairing of the two texts may show that the combination was already traditional (citing W. L. Lane, secondhand). He adds that interpreters "should therefore not read too much into this reminiscence of Exodus." In the next sentence, though, he allows that the wording "may suggest that the gospels' wording was designed to call to mind the remote setting of the Israelites in Exodus."

*A free application with no special design.* Meyer: "A free application without any substantial change in the contents of the passage, also without any special design in view."

The second reading is about where the wording came from, the first about what the change was for, and Blomberg presents his explanation as an alternative ("rather than") to the christological one. This lesson does not decide between them.

**5. "He is the Elijah" (11:14) and "I am not" (John 1:21).** In John's Gospel, priests and Levites ask John, "Are you Elijah?" He said, "I am not" (John 1:21, BSB). Matthew 11:14 has Jesus say "he is the Elijah who was to come." Readers have handled the difference in three main ways.

*Different senses of "Elijah."* Augustine (Tractate 4 on John, read directly) raises the problem himself: "it is to be feared lest men, insufficiently understanding, think that John contradicted what Christ said." His answer: "Rightly therefore, speaking literally, did John reply. For the Lord spoke figuratively." He adds a two-advents reading: "what John was to the first advent, that will Elias be to the second advent." Gill: "John's answer is to the intention of their question," which was whether he was "Elijah the prophet that lived in Ahab's time." Meyer: John "could only suppose his interrogators were thinking of the literal, not of the antitypical Elijah." These readers lean on Luke 1:17, where the angel says John will go "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (BSB). Blomberg reports this as "The most common" harmonization but questions it: "It might be asked if this would not have been self-evident to John's inquirers." He notes a third option, that John "did not genuinely know he was fulfilling the function of an Elijah." He leans toward M. de Jonge's reading: "Perhaps a better approach is to side with M. de Jonge" (de Jonge secondhand).

*Two traditions that differ.* What follows is Blomberg's summary; he argues against it, and no holder of this view was read for this lesson. He reports that "Most modern scholarship affirms that this is just another Johannine inaccuracy, where theology has outrun history." He describes a "popular theory" that the Fourth Gospel's author "deliberately modified the more authentic tradition reflected in the Synoptics" in response to followers of the Baptist "who perhaps even saw him as a Messiah." For a detailed statement he points to Walter Wink (*John the Baptist in the Gospel Tradition*, 1968). Meyer's commentary on John 1:21 argues against Hilgenfeld, which indicates Hilgenfeld held the texts in tension (secondhand).

*The Fourth Gospel as the earlier picture.* Blomberg reports that J. A. T. Robinson ("Elijah, John and Jesus," *New Testament Studies*, 1957-58) "stood this approach on its head." On Robinson's reading the Fourth Gospel "is more historically accurate than the Synoptics" here, and John's status was raised only later (secondhand).

The first group reads the two texts as compatible; the second and third read them as reflecting different stages or emphases of the tradition. Blomberg's own principle is that "objective historiography demands that a viable, harmonistic solution be preferred to one which requires that the gospels contradict themselves." This lesson reports the readings and does not rank them.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, a model of the shape and not an answer key; the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 11:2-6: "Meanwhile John heard in prison about the works of Christ, and he sent his disciples to ask Him, 'Are You the One who was to come, or should we look for someone else?' Jesus replied, 'Go back and report to John what you hear and see ... Blessed is the one who does not fall away on account of Me'" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the answer is not a yes or a no but a report of what is happening, in Isaiah's language, followed by a blessing on the one who does not "fall away." Jesus answers without an explicit rebuke; readers differ on whether 11:6 carries a warning (Ellicott: "A warning was needed, but it was given in the form of a beatitude"). In 11:7-11 he praises John publicly. Inference (this lesson's): a sincere question about Jesus could be brought to him and answered with evidence and Scripture. That holds on every reading in Positions 1, since on each someone asked and was answered.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** An honest question can be brought into the open and tested against what can be seen and what is written, rather than hidden or treated as disloyalty.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: bringing the question openly, and looking again at evidence. Not carried forward: John's prison, any claim that a modern reader's questions will be answered the same way or on any timeline, and any verdict on why John asked.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a narrated example, carried by analogy; nothing in 11:2-6 is a command to the reader. Promise scope: the blessing of 11:6 is on "the one who does not fall away on account of Me"; the passage does not promise that a reader's doubts will be resolved, or how or when.

**Modern domain and situation.** Church life. After a hard year, a long-serving small-group leader finds that things she once taught confidently now feel uncertain. She has told no one, afraid of unsettling the group.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: honest inquiry, not concealment and not a public announcement. One faithful response: she names the question privately to her pastor or a trusted friend in the church, rereads the passages she questions with someone, and steps back from teaching for a season if that seems wise.

**Cautions.** This example is about one kind of question and is not counsel for anyone's particular situation. A crisis of faith is a matter for a pastor, not a study note. If doubt comes with persistent hopelessness, loss of sleep or appetite, or any thought of self-harm, the right next step is a doctor or a mental-health professional. In the United States the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988) is available at any hour. The bridge above is an analogy the learner is testing, not a prescription.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Jesus's answer in 11:5 leaves out "liberty to the captives" while speaking to a prisoner, and lists lepers, whom no verse in this app's Isaiah names. What would you need to know to decide whether the list was shaped to fit Isaiah, shaped to fit what had happened in chapters 8-9, or both?
2. Matthew 11:10's first clause matches the Septuagint of Exodus 23:20 word for word, but the formula ("it is written") seems to point to Malachi. Blomberg both cautions against reading "too much into" the Exodus wording and allows that it may "call to mind the remote setting of the Israelites in Exodus." What would count as evidence either way?
3. The BSB's "who was to come" at 11:3 and 11:14 looks like a deliberate echo but is not one in Greek, while "works" (11:2) and "actions" (11:19) look unrelated but are the same Greek noun in the reading the BSB follows. What does that suggest about how far a reader should trust English repetitions when grading connections?
4. Blomberg's preference for harmonizing John 1:21 with Matthew 11:14 rests on a stated principle ("objective historiography demands that a viable, harmonistic solution be preferred"), and the readings he reports on the other side rest on a view of how the Fourth Gospel used its tradition. Can either side be argued from the two texts alone, or does every answer bring in a prior view? Which one do you bring?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 11:1-19 in order, from John's question to the closing line about wisdom. (b) Quote John's question as the BSB has it. (c) Name the three different ways this passage points back to the Old Testament (11:5, 11:10, 11:14), and say which one uses "it is written."

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) the sequence; (1:00-2:00) 11:5 and its Isaiah sources, with the Septuagint matches; (2:00-3:00) 11:10, Malachi 3:1, the Greek of Exodus 23:20, and the pronoun question (Positions 4); (3:00-4:00) Elijah, Malachi 4:5 and John 1:21 (Positions 5); (4:00-5:00) 11:11 or 11:12 with at least two named readings, ending on an open question.

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: "Calling Matthew 11:5 an 'allusion' undersells it; Jesus is plainly claiming to fulfil Isaiah, so it should be graded like a quotation." (Checkable: no formula, no full clause quoted, wording spread across several Isaiah passages.) A second runs the other way: "Grading 11:5 'strong' oversells it; 4Q521 shows a similar list outside the Gospels, so the list may be a common pattern rather than a pointer to Isaiah." (Ask what the Septuagint matches add that a general healing list would not.)

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) The passage does not say why John asked, or how he responded; nothing from John follows the answer (11:4-7). (b) The passage does not say who "the violent" are in 11:12 or whether the "violence" is hostile; the grammar allows more than one reading (Positions 3). (c) Matthew 11:14 does not say John is Elijah returned in person; it says "if you are willing to accept it, he is the Elijah who was to come," and readers differ on the sense (Positions 5).

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words in both texts:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Isaiah 35:5-6 | allusion | strong | Blind, deaf and lame together; the deaf clause matches the Septuagint in noun and verb; no formula |
| Isaiah 61:1 | allusion | strong | "Good news" and "the poor" share root and noun with the Septuagint, which also has "recovery of sight to the blind"; Luke 4:18 quotes the verse with a formula, Matthew 11:5 does not |
| Malachi 3:1 | quotation | explicit | "It is written" plus Malachi's messenger who prepares the way; "before Me" becomes "before You" |
| Exodus 23:20 | allusion | strong | The first clause is nine consecutive words of the Septuagint of Exodus 23:20; graded below explicit because the formula points to Malachi and the weight of the match is disputed |
| Malachi 4:5-6 | explicit_reference | explicit | Jesus names "the Elijah who was to come"; Malachi 4:5 is where Elijah's coming is promised; no quotation of Malachi's words |

Or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`. The candidate not authored is 11:19 to Proverbs 8 (or Sirach 4:11), with Jesus as personified Wisdom. Viljoen argues for it, and Proverbs 8:32 ("my sons ... blessed are those who keep my ways," BSB) gives Wisdom children, as Luke 7:35 does. Matthew's "her actions" lacks those children, though, and Viljoen reports dissent: Luz and Davies and Allison "dismiss the stance that Matthew offers a Wisdom Christology," and Gathercole (2003) takes the "deeds" as "the ministries of John and Jesus" (all via Viljoen). If authored, this would be a `motif` at `plausible`, and a reviewer may reasonably decide it should be. Isaiah 29:18-19 matches about as closely as 35:5-6 (deaf, blind and poor, with *kophoi* and the same verb). It is folded into the Isaiah 35 row rather than authored separately, to avoid splitting one allusion into several rows. Isaiah 26:19 shares only "dead ... raised."

- **Every BSB quotation in this lesson and its five connection files was checked by script against this app's own corpus** (`web/public/bible/BSB/*.json`), before and after drafting. The check corrected three wordings that circulate elsewhere: 11:11 "has risen," 11:12 "has been subject to violence," 11:19 "by her actions."

- **Not developed: outsiders taking the kingdom.** Some readers of 11:12 have outsiders (publicans, sinners, Gentiles) take the kingdom. Gill holds it in a mild form ("publicans, and harlots, and Gentile sinners; who might be thought to be a sort of intruders"). So does the Expositor's Greek Testament, which imagines the view of "those who deemed themselves the rightful citizens of the kingdom": "What a rabble! thought Scribes and Pharisees." Gregory the Great (via the Catena on Wikipedia) has repentant sinners "enter as sinners into the place of another." Hilary holds it in a supersessionist form ("the glory of Israel ... is entered and held by force by the might of the Gentiles," via the Catena on Wikipedia), as does Cornelius a Lapide (also via Wikipedia). That form is not developed here. Readings that cast Israel as the loser of the kingdom have been used against Jewish people, and a fair entry would name that use; this lesson's sourcing does not support such an entry.
