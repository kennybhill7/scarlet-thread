John the Baptist last appeared in Matthew at the river, announcing someone stronger who would come after him (3:11). In chapter 11 he is in prison, and he sends a question: "Are You the One who was to come, or should we look for someone else?" (11:3, BSB). Jesus answers with a list of what is happening, in words that sound like Isaiah, and then turns to the crowd and says who John is, first with a quotation ("it is written," 11:10) and then with a name ("he is the Elijah who was to come," 11:14). That makes this passage a good place to practice a distinction this course keeps returning to. An answer can echo the prophets without citing them. A quotation can carry a formula and still blend more than one text. A figure can be named outright. Each of the three is graded differently below, and each match is checked in the Greek, because in this passage the English sometimes hides a connection and sometimes invents one. The passage also contains one of the hardest sentences in the Gospels (11:12, on the kingdom and violence), and its Elijah saying stands in apparent tension with John's own words in another Gospel. Both are reported as open questions with named readers on each side.

## Context

**What the text says, in order (11:1-19).** After Jesus "had finished instructing His twelve disciples, He went on from there to teach and preach in their cities" (11:1, BSB). "Meanwhile John heard in prison about the works of Christ, and he sent his disciples to ask Him, 'Are You the One who was to come, or should we look for someone else?'" (11:2-3). Jesus replies: "Go back and report to John what you hear and see: The blind receive sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the good news is preached to the poor. Blessed is the one who does not fall away on account of Me" (11:4-6). As the messengers leave, he asks the crowds three times what they went into the wilderness to see: "A reed swaying in the wind?", "A man dressed in fine clothes?", "A prophet? Yes, I tell you, and more than a prophet" (11:7-9). He quotes Scripture about John (11:10), says "among those born of women there has risen no one greater than John the Baptist. Yet even the least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he" (11:11), speaks of the kingdom and violence (11:12), says "all the Prophets and the Law prophesied until John" (11:13), and adds, "if you are willing to accept it, he is the Elijah who was to come. He who has ears, let him hear" (11:14-15). He ends with a picture of "children sitting in the marketplaces," who complain, "We played the flute for you, and you did not dance; we sang a dirge, and you did not mourn" (11:16-17). John came "neither eating nor drinking," and they said "He has a demon!"; the Son of Man came "eating and drinking," and they said "Look at this glutton and drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and sinners!" The last line is: "But wisdom is vindicated by her actions" (11:18-19).

**Where John is.** Matthew has already said that John "had been imprisoned" (4:12, BSB), but he does not tell why until 14:3-4: "Herod had arrested John and bound him and put him in prison on account of Herodias, his brother Philip's wife, because John had been telling him, 'It is not lawful for you to have her.'" So a first-time reader of chapter 11 knows only that John is in prison, not the reason.

**"The One who was to come."** The phrase in John's question is, in Greek, *ho erchomenos*, "the coming one." It uses the same participle John himself used at the river: *ho de opiso mou erchomenos*, "the one coming after me" (3:11). The BSB renders 3:11 differently ("after me will come One more powerful than I"), so the echo is visible only in the Greek. Within Matthew, then, the question asks whether Jesus is the one John announced. That is an observation about the wording; what John meant by asking it is a separate question, taken up in Positions 1.

**The answer and Isaiah (11:5).** Jesus does not say "it is written" here. He reports six things happening, and several of them use Isaiah's language. The match is closer in the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Old Testament, than in English, and it is spread across more than one Isaiah passage:

- *Isaiah 35:5-6.* "Then the eyes of the blind will be opened and the ears of the deaf unstopped. Then the lame will leap like a deer" (BSB). The Septuagint has *typhlon* (blind), *kophon* (deaf), *cholos* (lame), against Matthew's *typhloi*, *kophoi*, *choloi*. The deaf clause matches in verb as well as noun: Septuagint *ota kophon akousontai*, "ears of the deaf will hear," against Matthew's *kophoi akouousin*, "the deaf hear." The verbs for the blind and the lame are different words. The Cambridge Bible notes that the Isaiah 35 passage "describes the work of God, who 'will come and save you.'" That is the language of 35:4: "your God will come ... He will come to save you" (BSB).
- *Isaiah 61:1.* "the LORD has anointed Me to preach good news to the poor" (BSB). The Septuagint has *euangelisasthai ptochois*, and Matthew has *ptochoi euangelizontai*: the same verb root and the same noun. The Septuagint of 61:1 also contains *typhlois anablepsin*, "recovery of sight to the blind," a phrase not in the Hebrew text the BSB translates (the BSB has "freedom to the prisoners"). Matthew's first clause, *typhloi anablepousin*, uses that same root. The Pulpit Commentary (via the Biblehub aggregator) puts it this way: "the words are taken from the LXX." Luke 4:17-21 is the place where this same verse is quoted with a formula. Jesus "found the place where it was written" and reads it, including "recovery of sight to the blind" (Luke 4:18, BSB), and says, "Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing" (4:21).
- *Isaiah 26:19 and 29:18.* "Your dead will live; their bodies will rise" (26:19, BSB); in the Septuagint, *anastesontai hoi nekroi, kai egerthesontai*, which shares Matthew's noun (*nekroi*) and his verb root (*egeirontai*, "are raised"). Isaiah 29:18 has "the deaf will hear the words of the scroll, and out of the deep darkness the eyes of the blind will see" (BSB).
- *Lepers.* A script search of this app's Isaiah finds no verse that mentions lepers or leprosy. That item in Jesus's list has no Isaiah counterpart.

Older commentators name these sources without a formula to go on. Meyer (via the Biblehub aggregator) calls Jesus's words "an echo of Isaiah 35:5 f., 8, Isaiah 61:1 ff." The Expositor's Greek Testament notes that *kophoi* means "deaf" here, "though in Matthew 9:32-33, it means dumb, showing that the prophecy, Isaiah 35:5, is in the speaker's thoughts." The Pulpit Commentary adds an observation of its own. In quoting Isaiah 61, Jesus "does not quote Isaiah's phrase, 'liberty to the captives,'" though it stands in the same verse and John is a prisoner. The Pulpit Commentary's explanation is that he wanted to turn John's attention "away from the more political part of Messiah's work." That is the commentary's reading, reported here, not something the text states.

**A parallel outside the Bible.** One Dead Sea Scroll, 4Q521 (sometimes called the Messianic Apocalypse), also puts raising the dead and good news for the poor side by side. In the translation the Wikipedia article credits to Gaye Strathearn (2011), the line reads: "for he will heal the badly wounded and he will make the dead live, he will proclaim good news to the poor." The same article reports "a striking similarity between Luke 7:22," the parallel to Matthew 11:5, "and Line 12 of this fragment." This is reported through a tertiary source. Strathearn's paper could not be retrieved for this lesson, and the scroll was not read in any edition.

**The quotation about the messenger (11:10).** Here Jesus does use a formula: "This is the one about whom it is written: 'Behold, I will send My messenger ahead of You, who will prepare Your way before You'" (BSB). Set the three related texts side by side in this app's BSB:

- Malachi 3:1: "Behold, I will send My messenger, who will prepare the way before Me."
- Exodus 23:20: "Behold, I am sending an angel before you to protect you along the way and to bring you to the place I have prepared."
- Matthew 11:10: "Behold, I will send My messenger ahead of You, who will prepare Your way before You."

In English, Matthew looks like Malachi with the pronouns changed. In Malachi it is God who speaks of a messenger who prepares the way "before Me" (Hebrew *lefanai*, "before My face"). In Matthew the way belongs to someone addressed as "You." In Greek the picture changes. Matthew's first clause, *Idou ego apostello ton angelon mou pro prosopou sou*, matches the Septuagint of Exodus 23:20 word for word after its opening "and." A word-by-word comparison finds nine identical words in a row. The Septuagint of Malachi 3:1 has a different verb (*exapostello*) and "before My face" (*pro prosopou mou*). Craig Blomberg (*Criswell Theological Review*, 1987) states it directly: "The first clause of this quotation parallels the LXX of Exod 23:20 exactly, but the second finds no equivalent there." Matthew's second clause ("who will prepare Your way") follows Malachi's sense. Ellicott calls the quotation "a free translation from the Hebrew," and Meyer calls it "a free rendering of the Hebrew and not from the LXX." Neither of their notes on 11:10 mentions Exodus. Luke 7:27 has the same quotation in the same BSB words. Mark 1:2 has a shorter form ("Behold, I will send My messenger ahead of You, who will prepare Your way") under the heading "As it is written in Isaiah the prophet." The Greek of Mark 1:2 lacks *ego*, so Mark's first clause shares eight consecutive words with Exodus. That matches Blomberg's own phrase, "eight consecutive paralleled words." Why the pronoun changed is reported in Positions 4. Lesson 5 of this course declined to author a Malachi 3:1 connection for Matthew 3 because Matthew quotes Malachi only here. This lesson is where that connection belongs.

**"Least" and the comparative.** In 11:11 the BSB has "the least in the kingdom of heaven." Ellicott and Meyer both note that the Greek word, *mikroteros*, is a comparative ("lesser"), not a superlative. That small grammatical point matters to one of the readings in Positions 2.

**"The Prophets and the Law."** Matthew 11:13 reads "all the Prophets and the Law prophesied until John" (BSB). A script search of this app's whole BSB finds the order "the Prophets and the Law" only in this verse, against five occurrences of "the Law and the Prophets." Luke has a related saying in a different setting: "The Law and the Prophets were proclaimed until John. Since that time, the gospel of the kingdom of God is being preached, and everyone is forcing his way into it" (Luke 16:16, BSB). The Pulpit Commentary notes that Luke "should omit our vers. 12-14" from his version of this speech (Luke 7:24-35) and places the related sayings at 16:16. In this app's text, Luke 7 never names Elijah.

**Elijah (11:14).** Malachi ends with "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and awesome Day of the LORD. And he will turn the hearts of the fathers to their children, and the hearts of the children to their fathers" (Malachi 4:5-6, BSB). The Septuagint of that verse says "Elijah the Tishbite" (*Elian ton Thesbiten*). The Pulpit Commentary (on John 1:21) says that rendering "had strengthened the common mistake of a metempsychosis, or such an abnormal manifestation before the coming of Messiah." How widespread a pre-Christian belief in Elijah as the Messiah's forerunner was is itself debated. Blomberg reports recent studies "which take their starting point from the claim that no unambiguous evidence exists for a pre-Christian Jewish belief in Elijah as a Messianic forerunner." He points especially to M. M. Faierstein (*Journal of Biblical Literature*, 1981) and cites J. A. Fitzmyer's 1985 reply. Neither article was read for this lesson. Within Matthew the identification comes back after the Transfiguration: "Elijah does indeed come, and he will restore all things. But I tell you that Elijah has already come ... Then the disciples understood that He was speaking to them about John the Baptist" (17:11-13, BSB). Lesson 5 already treats John's clothing (3:4) and its overlap with 2 Kings 1:8; this lesson does not repeat it.

**The children in the marketplace (11:16-19).** Luke 7:31-35 has the same picture, with small differences checkable in this app's BSB. Matthew has "marketplaces," Luke "marketplace." Matthew has "you did not mourn," Luke "you did not weep." Matthew has "neither eating nor drinking," Luke "neither eating bread nor drinking wine." Matthew has "they say," Luke "you say." The largest difference is in the last line: Matthew has "wisdom is vindicated by her actions," and Luke has "wisdom is vindicated by all her children" (Luke 7:35). The Greek manuscripts of Matthew are themselves divided here. Biblehub's parallel Greek editions show *ergon*, "works," in Westcott and Hort and in the Byzantine Majority Text, and *teknon*, "children," in Tischendorf and in Stephanus's Textus Receptus. The Pulpit Commentary reports *ergon* "with the Sinaitic manuscript and the original hand of the Vatican" and says "The common reading, τέκνων, has come from Luke." Bengel's editor lists Codex D, the Vulgate and the Received Text for "children." The King James Version's "Wisdom is justified of her children" follows the "children" reading. The BSB follows "works" (rendered "actions").

**Who is "Wisdom"?** Older commentators read the figure several ways. For Gill it is "either the wisdom of God, in making use of ministers of a different disposition," or the gospel, "or rather Christ himself, who is the wisdom of God." Bengel's editor writes, "No doubt Christ is the Wisdom meant." The Pulpit Commentary takes it as "the Divine wisdom, by which all creation was made (Proverbs 8:22-31; Wisd. 7:22)." Among recent scholars, Francois Viljoen (*In die Skriflig*, 2024) argues that "The deeds of σοφία (Mt 11:19) are the deeds of Christ (Mt 11:2)" and that "Matthew identifies Jesus not only as the child of Wisdom as Luke does, but as σοφία itself." He builds on M. Jack Suggs's *Wisdom, Christology, and Law in Matthew's Gospel* (1970), which this lesson did not read. Viljoen proposes that the saying "reflects Sirach 4:11 ... and Proverbs 8:32." In this app's BSB, Proverbs 8:32 reads: "Now therefore, my sons, listen to me, for blessed are those who keep my ways." Viljoen also reports other views. Cotter, Beare and Hagner regard the line "as an independent proverb with no clear connection with the preceding parable." Gathercole (2003) takes the "deeds" as "the ministries of John and Jesus." In a note on Matthew 23, he reports that Luz and Davies and Allison "dismiss the stance that Matthew offers a Wisdom Christology." All of these except Viljoen are reported secondhand through his article.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 11:1-19 is built. These are claims about the text's shape, not about what that shape is meant to teach.

**A seam.** "After Jesus had finished instructing His twelve disciples" (11:1) is one of five places in Matthew where the BSB has Jesus "had finished" a body of teaching: 7:28, 11:1, 13:53, 19:1 and 26:1 (a script count over this app's text). Each follows a long block of Jesus's speech. This lesson notes the pattern and does not build an argument on it.

**"Works" at both ends.** John hears about "the works of Christ" (11:2), and the passage ends with wisdom "vindicated by her actions" (11:19). The BSB uses two English words, but the Greek noun is the same: *ta erga tou Christou* at 11:2 and *apo ton ergon autes* at 11:19. A reader of the English alone would miss the repetition. Viljoen draws on exactly this repetition (see Context). Readers who take "wisdom" in some other way can still see the repetition in the text; what it means is the open question.

**An echo that is only in English.** The BSB uses "who was to come" twice in this passage: John asks "Are You the One who was to come" (11:3), and Jesus calls John "the Elijah who was to come" (11:14). A script search finds that phrase nowhere else in Matthew's BSB text. The Greek, however, differs: *ho erchomenos* at 11:3, and *ho mellon erchesthai*, "the one about to come," at 11:14. The repetition belongs to the translation. This is a useful case to keep in mind, because an English echo can look like design when it is not.

**Three questions, then a turn.** "What did you go out ... to see?" comes three times (11:7, 8, 9; a script count). The first two expect "no"; the third gets "Yes, I tell you, and more than a prophet." The quotation in 11:10 then explains the "more."
## Positions

Five questions in this passage draw named, published disagreement. None is settled here. Each reading is given in its holders' words where a source was read directly, and marked as secondhand where it was not.

**1. Why did John send the question (11:2-3)?** Meyer's commentary sorts the history of interpretation into three groups, and the sources read for this lesson fit them.

*John himself was in real doubt.* Meyer holds that "the Baptist's question proceeded from real doubt as to whether Jesus was the ἐρχόμενος, yea or nay." He adds that the doubt "cannot be regarded as showing a want of spirituality," but "only as a temporary eclipse of his settled conviction, which, owing to human infirmity, had yielded to the influence of despondency." He names Tertullian as an early holder of this view (secondhand).

*John asked for his disciples' sake.* John Chrysostom (Homily 36 on Matthew, read directly) holds that "neither did he send as being himself in doubt, nor did he ask in ignorance." His reason is that "John's disciples were starting aside from Jesus," so John sent them to see for themselves. Meyer lists Origen, Augustine, Jerome, Hilary, Luther, Calvin and Bengel among those who read it this way (secondhand).

*John was urging Jesus to act.* Meyer reports a third reading, which sees the question as "an expression of impatience, and an indirect challenge to the Messiah to establish His kingdom without delay." He names Lightfoot, Michaelis, Kuinoel, Fritzsche and Hase as its holders (secondhand).

Each reading can point to something in the text. The first can point to the plain wording of the question. The second can point to the fact that Jesus's answer goes to the messengers ("report to John what you hear and see," 11:4). The third can point to the setting, a prisoner waiting. The text records the question and the answer; it does not record John's response. This lesson does not decide between the readings.

A note for any reader carrying a question like John's for themselves: this lesson reports how commentators have read one man's question. It cannot weigh a reader's own season of doubt, and it is not meant to. Someone working through a crisis of faith should talk with a pastor or another trusted person in their church, not rely on a study note.

**2. Who is "the least in the kingdom of heaven" (11:11)?** Four readings are on record.

*Jesus himself.* Chrysostom (Homily 37, read directly) holds that Jesus is speaking of himself: "when He is speaking of Himself, He naturally conceals His person." Meyer summarizes Chrysostom's sense in Greek: lesser in age and in the opinion of many (*kata ten helikian kai kata ten pollon doxan*), yet in fact greater. Meyer lists Hilary, Erasmus, Luther and Melanchthon among those who read it this way (secondhand). Ellicott answers that "It would be but a poor truism to have declared that the King was greater than the herald," and that "there is no example of our Lord's so speaking of Himself elsewhere."

*Any member of the kingdom.* Ellicott reads it of disciples, the "little ones" of Matthew 10:42, who are less than John "in fame, work, the rigour of ascetic holiness," yet "above him in the knowledge of the truth." The Pulpit Commentary: "The weakest Christian is greater in privileges than the greatest of the Old Testament saints."

*The apostles and preachers.* Gill reads it "of the apostles of Christ, and the least among them," who could preach a death and resurrection John could only foretell. Poole reports "Mr. Calvin and many others" reading "the least of those who shall preach the gospel after my resurrection."

*One lower in the coming kingdom compared with John's place in the old order.* Meyer takes the comparative at face value: "he who stands lower in the kingdom of the Messiah, stands (according to the divine standard) higher than he." He insists this does not shut John out of the kingdom: "I confess that I am at a loss to comprehend how one can understand ver. 11 in such a way as to exclude ... the Baptist from the kingdom of heaven."

**3. What does 11:12 mean?** The BSB reads: "the kingdom of heaven has been subject to violence, and the violent lay claim to it." The Greek verb *biazetai* can be passive ("is treated with violence, is forced") or middle ("forces its way"). The noun *biastai* means "violent people." Meyer, the Expositor's Greek Testament and Ellicott all note both grammatical options. Three readings are on record, and Meyer names the holders of each.

*Eager people press in.* The kingdom is "taken by storm" by people who want it badly. Meyer: "that eager, irresistible striving and struggling after the approaching Messianic kingdom," with "those who use violent efforts" dragging it "to themselves." Chrysostom (Homily 37, read directly): "who has so taken it? Tell me. All who approach it with earnestness of mind." Ellicott: "There is no thought of hostile purpose in the words." Jerome and Gregory the Great are quoted to the same effect in Aquinas's *Catena Aurea*, as reproduced by Wikipedia (tertiary). This reading can point to Luke 16:16, "everyone is forcing his way into it" (BSB). Meyer cites the verb there for this reading; the Pulpit Commentary does the same.

*Opponents attack it.* Meyer reports that Lightfoot, Schneckenburger and Hilgenfeld read the verse as "a hostile violence with which the Messianic kingdom is persecuted," with *harpazousin* meaning that opponents "snatch it away from men" (secondhand). Gill lists this as a live option: "the Gospel's suffering violence by the persecutions of its enemies." This reading can point to the setting, with John in prison (11:2) and "this generation" rejecting both men (11:16-19); that observation is this lesson's own. Meyer, who holds the first reading, calls this one "partly an anachronism."

*The kingdom forces its way forward.* Bengel takes the verb as middle: the kingdom "pushes itself forward as it were by violence." Meyer lists Melanchthon, Baur and Zyro alongside him (secondhand). Bengel writes that "There is no complaint here of hostile force, for the complaint begins at Matthew 11:16." The Expositor's Greek Testament grants that the verb "might be middle voice," but holds that "the passive is demanded by the noun following."

This lesson reports all three and ranks none.

**4. "Before You" in place of "before Me" (11:10): why the change?** In Malachi 3:1 the messenger prepares the way "before Me," that is, before God, who is speaking. In Matthew 11:10 he goes "ahead of You" and prepares "Your way." All the readers below agree that the change is real. They disagree about what accounts for it.

*A deliberate christological application.* Bengel: "The advent of the Father and of the Son are the same, and so is the language which applies to them. It is one of the strongest arguments for the divinity of Christ, that those things which are said of Christ in the New Testament are quoted from the Old Testament, where they are predicated as exclusively belonging to God." The Pulpit Commentary: "Christ does not hesitate to apply to himself a prophecy of the coming of God." The Expositor's Greek Testament says the oracle is "altered so as to make the Messianic reference apparent," with *mou* ("my") changed to *sou* ("your"). Ellicott: "In the original it is Jehovah Himself who speaks of His own coming ... In the Evangelist's paraphrase it is Jehovah who speaks to the Christ."

*The wording of Exodus 23:20.* Blomberg holds that the pronoun comes with the Exodus phrasing: "This, rather than any innovative, christological interpretation of Mal 3:1 most naturally accounts for the shift in person of the pronouns." He also suggests the pairing may be older than the Gospels, since "a later rabbinic juxtaposition of these two texts may suggest that their combination was already traditional in Jesus' day." For that he cites W. L. Lane on *Exodus Rabbah* (secondhand). He adds: "Interpreters of the gospels should therefore not read too much into this reminiscence of Exodus."

*A free application with no special design.* Meyer: "A free application without any substantial change in the contents of the passage, also without any special design in view."

The second reading is about where the wording came from, the first about what the change was for, and Blomberg presents his explanation as an alternative ("rather than") to the christological one. This lesson does not decide between them.

**5. "He is the Elijah" (11:14) and "I am not" (John 1:21).** In John's Gospel, priests and Levites ask John, "Are you Elijah?" He said, "I am not" (John 1:21, BSB). Matthew 11:14 has Jesus say "he is the Elijah who was to come." Readers have handled the difference in three main ways.

*Different senses of "Elijah."* Augustine (Tractate 4 on John, read directly) raises the problem himself: "it is to be feared lest men, insufficiently understanding, think that John contradicted what Christ said." His answer: "Rightly therefore, speaking literally, did John reply. For the Lord spoke figuratively." He adds a two-advents reading: "what John was to the first advent, that will Elias be to the second advent." Gill: "John's answer is to the intention of their question," which was whether he was "Elijah the prophet that lived in Ahab's time." Meyer: John "could only suppose his interrogators were thinking of the literal, not of the antitypical Elijah." These readers lean on Luke 1:17, where the angel says John will go "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (BSB). Blomberg calls the literal-Elijah explanation "The most common" harmonization, and reports two others, from M. de Jonge and J. R. Michaels (secondhand). His own principle is that "objective historiography demands that a viable, harmonistic solution be preferred to one which requires that the gospels contradict themselves."

*Two traditions that differ.* Blomberg reports that "Most modern scholarship affirms that this is just another Johannine inaccuracy, where theology has outrun history." He describes a "popular theory" that the Fourth Gospel's author "deliberately modified the more authentic tradition reflected in the Synoptics" in response to followers of the Baptist "who perhaps even saw him as a Messiah." For a detailed statement he points to Walter Wink (*John the Baptist in the Gospel Tradition*, 1968), which this lesson did not read. Meyer's commentary on John 1:21 argues against Hilgenfeld, which indicates Hilgenfeld held the texts in tension (secondhand, and Hilgenfeld's own words were not read).

*The Fourth Gospel as the earlier picture.* Blomberg reports that J. A. T. Robinson ("Elijah, John and Jesus," *New Testament Studies*, 1957-58) "stood this approach on its head." On Robinson's reading the Fourth Gospel "is more historically accurate than the Synoptics" here, and John's status was raised only later (secondhand).

The first group reads the two texts as compatible, and the second and third read them as reflecting different stages or emphases of the tradition. This lesson reports the readings and does not rank them.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 11:2-6: "Meanwhile John heard in prison about the works of Christ, and he sent his disciples to ask Him, 'Are You the One who was to come, or should we look for someone else?' Jesus replied, 'Go back and report to John what you hear and see ... Blessed is the one who does not fall away on account of Me'" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: a question is sent, and the answer is not a yes or a no but a report of what is happening, worded in the language of Isaiah, followed by a blessing on the one who does not "fall away." Jesus does not rebuke the question, and in 11:7-11 he praises the questioner publicly. Inference: a first hearer could take from this that a sincere question about Jesus could be brought to him and answered by pointing to evidence and Scripture. This holds whichever reading in Positions 1 is right, since on every reading someone asked and was answered. The inference is this lesson's, and it is labeled as such.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** An honest question can be brought into the open and tested against what can be seen and what is written, rather than hidden or treated as disloyalty.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: bringing the question openly, and looking again at evidence. Not carried forward: John's prison, any claim that a modern reader's questions will be answered in the same way or on any timeline, and any verdict on why John asked.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a narrated example, carried to a later reader by analogy; nothing in 11:2-6 is addressed to the reader as a command. Promise scope: the blessing in 11:6 is spoken about "the one who does not fall away on account of Me." The passage does not promise that a reader's doubts will be resolved, or how or when.

**Modern domain and situation.** Church life. A long-serving small-group leader has found, over a hard year, that things she once taught confidently now feel uncertain. She has told no one, because she is afraid the group will be unsettled or that she will lose her place.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: honest inquiry, not concealment and not a public announcement. One faithful response: she names the question privately to her pastor or to a trusted, mature friend in the church. She rereads the Gospel accounts she has questions about, slowly, with someone. She steps back from teaching for a season if that seems wise, rather than teaching what she cannot currently say honestly.

**Cautions.** This example is about one kind of question and is not counsel for anyone's particular situation. A crisis of faith is a matter for a pastor, not a study note. If doubt comes with persistent hopelessness, loss of sleep or appetite, or any thought of self-harm, the right next step is a doctor or a mental-health professional, and in the United States the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988) is available at any hour. The bridge above is an analogy the learner is testing, not a prescription.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Jesus's answer in 11:5 draws on at least four Isaiah passages, lists one item (lepers) Isaiah never mentions, and leaves out "liberty to the captives" while speaking to a prisoner. What would you need to know to decide whether the list was shaped to fit Isaiah, shaped to fit what had actually happened in chapters 8-9, or both?
2. Matthew 11:10's first clause matches the Septuagint of Exodus 23:20 word for word, but the formula ("it is written") seems to point to Malachi. When a quotation blends two texts, should a reader hear the setting of both (the wilderness guide of Exodus, the coming to the temple in Malachi), or only the one the speaker seems to intend? Blomberg advises not to "read too much into" the Exodus wording. What would count as evidence either way?
3. The BSB's "who was to come" at 11:3 and 11:14 looks like a deliberate echo but is not one in Greek, while "works" (11:2) and "actions" (11:19) look unrelated but are the same Greek noun. What does that suggest about how far a reader should trust English repetitions when grading connections?
4. Blomberg's preference for harmonizing John 1:21 with Matthew 11:14 rests on a stated principle ("objective historiography demands that a viable, harmonistic solution be preferred"), and the readings he reports on the other side rest on a view of how the Fourth Gospel used its tradition. Can either side be argued from the two texts alone, or does every answer bring in a prior view? Which one do you bring?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 11:1-19 in order: John's question, Jesus's answer and blessing, the three questions to the crowd, the quotation, "no one greater," the violence saying, Elijah, the children's game, and the closing line about wisdom. (b) Quote John's question as the BSB has it. (c) Name the three different ways this passage points back to the Old Testament (11:5, 11:10, 11:14), and say which one uses "it is written."

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the sequence, and John in prison (Context); (1:00-2:00) the answer of 11:5 and its Isaiah sources, with the Septuagint matches and the missing lepers (Context; Teach-Back 5); (2:00-3:00) the 11:10 quotation, with Malachi 3:1, the Greek of Exodus 23:20, and the pronoun question (Context; Positions 4); (3:00-4:00) Elijah, Malachi 4:5, and John 1:21 (Positions 5); (4:00-5:00) one of the two cruxes, either 11:11 or 11:12, with at least two named readings, ending on an open question.

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: "Calling Matthew 11:5 an 'allusion' undersells it; Jesus is plainly claiming to fulfil Isaiah, so it should be graded like a quotation." Before answering, note what is checkable. There is no formula in 11:5, no full clause is quoted, and the words are spread across several Isaiah passages. A second candidate runs the other way: "Grading 11:5 'strong' oversells it; 4Q521 shows a similar list outside the Gospels, so the list may be a common pattern rather than a pointer to Isaiah." Ask what the Greek matches with the Septuagint of Isaiah 61:1 and 35:5 contribute that a general healing list would not.

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) The passage does not say why John asked, or how he responded to the answer; the text gives the question (11:3), the answer (11:4-6), and Jesus's speech after the messengers leave (11:7), and nothing from John afterward. (b) The passage does not say who "the violent" are in 11:12 or whether the "violence" is hostile; the grammar allows more than one reading (Positions 3). (c) Matthew 11:14 does not say John is Elijah returned in person; it says "if you are willing to accept it, he is the Elijah who was to come," and readers differ on the sense (Positions 5).

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words in both texts:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Isaiah 35:5-6 | allusion | strong | Blind, deaf and lame together; the deaf clause matches the Septuagint in noun and verb; no formula |
| Isaiah 61:1 | allusion | strong | "Good news" and "the poor" share root and noun with the Septuagint, which also has "recovery of sight to the blind"; Luke 4:18 quotes the verse with a formula, Matthew 11:5 does not |
| Malachi 3:1 | quotation | explicit | "It is written" plus Malachi's messenger who prepares the way; "before Me" becomes "before You" |
| Exodus 23:20 | allusion | strong | The first clause is nine consecutive words of the Septuagint of Exodus 23:20; graded below explicit because the formula points to Malachi and the weight of the match is disputed |
| Malachi 4:5-6 | explicit_reference | explicit | Jesus names "the Elijah who was to come"; Malachi 4:5 is where Elijah's coming is promised; no quotation of Malachi's words |

Or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`. The candidate this lesson considered and did not author is a connection from 11:19 to Proverbs 8 (or Sirach 4:11), reading "wisdom" as the personified Wisdom of Proverbs 8 and as Jesus himself. A named scholar argues for it (Viljoen, building on Suggs), and Proverbs 8:32 ("my sons ... blessed are those who keep my ways," BSB) gives Wisdom children, as Luke 7:35 does. Three things kept it from being authored. Matthew's own wording ("her actions") lacks the "children" that makes the verbal tie to Proverbs 8:32, and that tie belongs more naturally to Luke's form. The texts share no other wording beyond "wisdom." And named scholars are reported on the other side: Luz and Davies and Allison dismiss a Wisdom Christology in Matthew, and Cotter, Beare and Hagner treat the line as an independent proverb. All of them are reported only through Viljoen. If authored, this would be a `motif` at `plausible`, and a reviewer may reasonably decide it should be. Isaiah 26:19 and 29:18-19 were also not authored as separate rows; each shares less of Matthew's wording than 35:5-6 and 61:1, and both are named in the Isaiah 35 connection instead.
