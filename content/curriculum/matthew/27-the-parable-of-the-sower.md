Matthew 13 opens a new kind of teaching. Jesus sits in a boat, speaks to a crowd on the shore, and "He told them many things in parables" (13:3, BSB). The first is a farmer sowing seed on four kinds of ground. Between the story and its explanation the disciples ask a question that readers have argued over ever since: "Why do You speak to the people in parables?" (13:10). Jesus answers with words from Isaiah, introduced by Matthew's language of fulfillment: "In them the prophecy of Isaiah is fulfilled" (13:14). That quotation is the main thread this lesson follows. It can be checked word by word against Isaiah 6:9-10 in this app's BSB, in the Hebrew and in the Greek Septuagint, and the check turns up a real difference. The lesson then reports two disputes without settling them. The first is whether parables hide the truth as a judgment or open it to those willing to hear. The second is whether the rocky and thorny hearers were ever truly saved. Because some readers come away afraid that they are "bad soil," the lesson also reports what named readers say the parable is for. Every BSB quotation below was printed from the app's own Bible files before drafting and checked again by script afterward.

## Context

**What happens, in order (13:1-23).** From a boat, with "all the people" on the shore (13:2, BSB), Jesus tells the parable (13:3-9) and ends, "He who has ears, let him hear" (13:9). In Matthew's BSB that call occurs at 11:15, 13:9 and 13:43 (a script search). The disciples ask why He speaks in parables (13:10). He answers in four steps: knowledge of "the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven" has been given to them "but not to them" (13:11); a saying about having and not having (13:12); the reason for parables (13:13); and the Isaiah quotation (13:14-15). He then blesses the disciples' eyes and ears (13:16-17) and explains the parable soil by soil (13:18-23).

**Three Gospels, one parable.** Mark 4:1-20 and Luke 8:4-15 have the same parable, question, Isaiah words and explanation, in the same order. The differences are small, checkable in the BSB, and graded in the connections. Three matter here. First, the yields. Matthew runs "a hundredfold, sixtyfold, or thirtyfold" (13:8), Mark the other way, "thirtyfold, another sixtyfold, and another a hundredfold" (Mark 4:8), and Luke gives only "a hundredfold" (Luke 8:8). John T. Carroll (Working Preacher, 2023) notes that "Matthew reverses the sequence in Mark's rendition." Second, the enemy who takes the seed is "the evil one" in Matthew (13:19), "Satan" in Mark (4:15) and "the devil" in Luke (8:12). Third, Matthew's rocky-ground hearer "remains for only a season" (13:21), while Luke's hearers "believe for a season" (Luke 8:13), a difference that bears on the second dispute in Positions.

**"Whoever has will be given more" (13:12).** The saying returns almost word for word at 25:29, in the parable of the talents. Calvin says the later use has "a different sense," because there "the discourse relates to the lawful use of gifts."

**"This is why I speak to them in parables" (13:13): "because" or "so that."** The three Gospels connect the parables to the Isaiah words with different small words.

- Mark: "so that, 'they may be ever seeing but never perceiving, and ever hearing but never understanding; otherwise they might turn and be forgiven.'" (Mark 4:12, BSB).
- Luke: "so that, 'though seeing, they may not see; though hearing, they may not understand.'" (Luke 8:10, BSB).
- Matthew: "This is why I speak to them in parables: 'Though seeing, they do not see; though hearing, they do not hear or understand.'" (13:13, BSB).

In Greek, Mark and Luke have *hina*, the usual word for purpose ("in order that"), with verbs in the subjunctive. Matthew has *hoti*, usually "because," with verbs in the indicative. All seven editions Bible Hub lists have *hoti* at Matthew 13:13 and *hina* at Mark 4:12 and Luke 8:10. The BSB does not render Matthew's *hoti* as "because"; it sets the clause off as a quotation, as Chang-Wook Jung (*Journal of Biblical Text Research*, 2010) notes the NIV does. Jung also reports a textual question the editions above do not show: Old Latin ("Western"), Caesarean and Coptic witnesses read *hina* in Matthew too, while Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary* rates *hoti* almost certainly original, with a "B" grade (secondhand through Jung). Jung judges the external evidence "quite balanced." What the difference of conjunction amounts to is the first dispute in Positions.

**The quotation (13:14-15) beside Isaiah 6:9-10.** Set side by side in this app's BSB:

- Isaiah 6:9-10: "Go and tell this people, 'Be ever hearing, but never understanding; be ever seeing, but never perceiving.' Make the hearts of this people calloused; deafen their ears and close their eyes. Otherwise they might see with their eyes, hear with their ears, understand with their hearts, and turn and be healed."
- Matthew 13:14-15: "You will be ever hearing but never understanding; you will be ever seeing but never perceiving. For this people's heart has grown callous; they hardly hear with their ears, and they have closed their eyes. Otherwise they might see with their eyes, hear with their ears, understand with their hearts, and turn, and I would heal them."

The second halves match in the BSB; the first halves do not. Isaiah gives commands ("Be ever hearing," "Make the hearts... calloused," "deafen," "close"). Matthew describes ("You will be ever hearing," "this people's heart has grown callous"). Isaiah ends "and turn and be healed," Matthew "and I would heal them," and Matthew omits Isaiah's opening, "Go and tell this people."

The Hebrew and the Greek explain the difference. In Bible Hub's Hebrew interlinear, the verbs of Isaiah 6:9-10 are imperatives: *shim'u* ("hear"), *hashmen* ("make fat," that is, make dull), *hakbed* ("make heavy") and *hasha'* ("shut"). The verse ends *werapha lo*, rendered "and there be healing for it," with no "I." The Septuagint (Swete's text on Bible Hub) reads differently. It has a future indicative, *akoe akousete* ("you will hear with hearing"); a passive statement, *epachynthe gar he kardia tou laou toutou* ("for the heart of this people has grown fat"); and God speaking in the first person at the end, *kai iasomai autous* ("and I will heal them"). Matthew's Greek follows the Septuagint word for word in both verses with one small difference. In Swete's text the pronoun "their" goes with the ears (*tois osin auton*). In Matthew it goes with the eyes (*tous ophthalmous auton*). Jonathan Lo (2019), working from Rahlfs's edition of the Septuagint, reports only that Matthew "omits the possessive pronoun" from "their ears" and that "the rest of the quotation is reproduced verbatim." The two printed Septuagint editions apparently differ on the second pronoun; this lesson read only Swete's. Ellicott, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown and the Pulpit Commentary all name the Septuagint as the source; the Pulpit adds, "and so in Acts 28:26, 27. But John 12:40, on the contrary, is nearer the Hebrew." Gill notes that "in the prophecy of Isaiah, the words run in the imperative," and that Matthew's future is supported by the Septuagint, the Aramaic paraphrase (the Targum) and Jewish commentators.

Lo describes what the Septuagint's wording does: "In the LXX, this element is softened and downplayed; the prophet merely testifies to the people's rebellious condition that prevents repentance rather than causing it" (citing France and Hagner). Mark's ending, "and be forgiven" (Mark 4:12), differs from both the Hebrew and the Greek. Lo reports that "The Targum to Isaiah understands 'healing' to be a metaphor for forgiveness and renders the Aramaic translation as such," and Gill says the same of "the Chaldee paraphrase."

**Isaiah 6:9-10 elsewhere.** The words return in John 12:40 ("He has blinded their eyes and hardened their hearts," BSB) and in Acts 28:26-27, where Paul quotes them to Jewish leaders in Rome. In the BSB, Acts 28:27 is identical to Matthew 13:15 (a script comparison), and "ever hearing" occurs only at Isaiah 6:9, Matthew 13:14, Mark 4:12 and Acts 28:26. Only the Matthew-Isaiah row is authored here.

**An unusual formula.** Matthew's other fulfillment formulas speak of what was "spoken through the prophet" (for example 4:14, 8:17, 12:17 and 21:4). In this app's BSB, "the prophecy of Isaiah" occurs only at 13:14. The Greek verb is unusual too: *anaplerountai*, "is being filled up," a compound of Matthew's usual verb *pleroo*. Meyer says the compound occurs "nowhere else in Matthew"; Thayer's lexicon entry on Bible Hub cites only Matthew 13:14 for this use with a prophecy. Jung lists the features that set this quotation apart. Jesus speaks it, not the narrator. It follows the Septuagint. It largely repeats 13:13. Its two key words, the verb and "prophecy," occur nowhere else in Matthew. He reports that some scholars treat 13:14-15 as "a later interpolation, perhaps on the basis of Acts 28:26-7" (Graham Stanton, following Krister Stendahl). He also reports that Davies and Allison observe that "the gospel text runs smoothly if 13:14-15 is omitted," and that Donald Hagner holds that Matthew himself inserted the quotation (all secondhand through Jung). As Jung reports it, the interpolation case rests on these features of style. He cites no manuscript that lacks the verses, and every edition Bible Hub lists includes them.

**"Blessed are your eyes" (13:16-17).** "But blessed are your eyes because they see, and your ears because they hear. For truly I tell you, many prophets and righteous men longed to see what you see but did not see it, and to hear what you hear but did not hear it" (13:16-17, BSB). Luke has a close form in a different scene, after the seventy-two return: "many prophets and kings desired to see what you see" (Luke 10:24). The Greek differs at two points, *dikaioi* ("righteous") against *basileis* ("kings"), and *epethymesan* ("longed") against *ethelesan* ("wanted"). Calvin remarks that "Luke appears to represent this statement as having been spoken at another time." Chrysostom (Homily 45) draws attention to who receives the blessing: "For indeed these too were Jews, and brought up in the same circumstances."

**The explanation (13:18-23).** Each soil is a kind of hearer, and each explanation begins with hearing, from "When anyone hears the message of the kingdom" (13:19; a phrase found nowhere else in the BSB, by script search) to "the one who hears the word and understands it" (13:23). On the yields, Ellicott says Isaac's harvest in Genesis 26:12 shows a hundredfold "was not unheard of." Meyer adds that such details "should not be pressed, serving as they do merely to enliven and fill out the picture." That possible echo is weighed in Teach-Back 5.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 13:1-23 is built, not on what the construction is meant to teach.

**Parable, question, explanation.** Lo, citing R. T. France, lays the passage out as A-B-A': the parable in public (13:3-9), the question and answer (13:10-17), the explanation (13:18-23). Mark and Luke share the order; only Matthew's middle quotes Isaiah at full length and adds 13:16-17.

**Heart, ears, eyes, then eyes, ears, heart.** Matthew 13:15 names the organs in one order and then reverses it: "this people's heart has grown callous; they hardly hear with their ears, and they have closed their eyes. Otherwise they might see with their eyes, hear with their ears, understand with their hearts." Bengel noticed this pattern: "The heart is the first in the beginning, the last in the end." Lo finds the same mirror pattern in the Hebrew, the Greek and the Aramaic forms of Isaiah 6:10. The pattern is the prophet's, carried over whole.

**Three failures in 13:13.** Mark and Luke pair "seeing" with "hearing." Matthew's clause ends with three failures: "they do not see... they do not hear or understand" (13:13). Lo argues that Matthew made "understand" a separate verb to mirror Isaiah 6:10's own three-part list. He quotes Klyne Snodgrass's summary (secondhand): "Whereas Mark asks, 'Do you really hear Jesus' message?' Matthew asks, 'Do you really understand with your heart?'" "Understand" then recurs, from the path hearer who "does not understand" (13:19) to the good-soil hearer who "understands it" (13:23).

**"You" and "them."** The answer turns on two pronouns, from "given to you, but not to them" (13:11) to "But blessed are your eyes" (13:16). The text does not say whether a person can move from one group to the other; readers answer that differently in Positions.

## Positions

Two disputes attach to this passage. Each is reported in its holders' own words and not resolved. A misuse is then named, followed by a pastoral note.

**1. Why parables (13:10-15)? Do they conceal as judgment or reveal to the receptive?** Four named readings are reported below, then the commentators who argue over the conjunction. This curriculum's Matthew 11:20-30 lesson reported the same three church traditions (Reformed, Wesleyan, Catholic) on a related question.

*Chrysostom (Homily 45 on Matthew, read directly).* Chrysostom reads "it is given" as grace that does not cancel human choice: "this He said, not bringing in necessity, or any allotment made causelessly and at random, but implying them to be the authors of all their own evils, and wishing to represent that the thing is a gift, and a grace bestowed from above." On 13:13 he calls the hearers' condition "a voluntary and self-chosen blindness." He reads the parables themselves as an act of mercy: "For if it had not been His will that they should hear and be saved, He ought to have been silent, not to have spoken in parables; but now by this very thing He stirs them up, even by speaking under a veil."

*The Reformed tradition (John Calvin, Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists).* Calvin holds that the obscurity was intended: "Christ declares that he intentionally spoke obscurely, in order that his discourse might be a riddle to many." He grounds the difference between hearers in God's choice: "No other reason will be found for this distinction, except that God calls to himself those whom he has gratuitously elected." He also insists that the fault lies in the hearers, not in the word. The word of God "in its own nature, is always bright," and "its light is choked by the darkness of men." On Matthew's form of the quotation he writes: "Matthew ascribes this to the hearers, that they may endure the blame of their own blindness and hardness; for the one cannot be separated from the other." On Mark's "so that" he says the teaching is "not, strictly speaking, or by itself, or in its own nature, but by accident, the cause of blindness."

*The Wesleyan tradition (John Wesley, Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament, 1755).* Wesley says parables made "the truths delivered to sink the deeper into humble and serious hearers," and that "At the same time, by an awful mixture of justice and mercy, it hid them from the proud and careless." He reads 13:12 as a rule about use: whoever "improves what he hath, uses the grace given according to the design of the giver," will be given more. On 13:13, more is withheld because "they use not that which they have already." On the soils he writes that "it is not the will of God that hinders, but their own voluntary perverseness."

*The Catholic Church (Catechism, §546).* The Catechism presents the parables as an invitation that calls for a decision: "Through his parables he invites people to the feast of the kingdom, but he also asks for a radical choice." It reads the sower as a question put to each hearer: "The parables are like mirrors for man: will he be hard soil or good earth for the word?" It ends: "One must enter the kingdom, that is, become a disciple of Christ, in order to 'know the secrets of the kingdom of heaven'. For those who stay 'outside', everything remains enigmatic."

*On "because" and "so that."* Older commentators read through Bible Hub divide over how much Matthew's *hoti* changes. Barnes says of Matthew's "because" and Mark's and Luke's "that," "there is no difference": Matthew "simply states the 'fact,'" and the others the "intention." The Pulpit Commentary calls the difference "more formal than real." Ellicott allows that Matthew's words "might mean that our Lord adopted the method of parables as a condescension to their infirmities." He then judges the penal sense to be "even here the true meaning," and adds, "even here we may venture to trace beneath the penalty an element of mercy." On Mark he adds that "the acceptance of a foreseen result was, in Hebrew forms of thought, expressed as the working out of an intention." The Expositor's Greek Testament takes Matthew's wording to mean "they are dull of apprehension, therefore I do my best to enlighten them." Of Mark's purpose clause, taken at face value, it says, "This cannot really have been the aim of Jesus," and it refers readers to Matthew, "where the statement is softened somewhat." Meyer reads Matthew's parables as teaching "suited to their intellectual poverty and obtuseness," meant to become "the starting-point of a further gradual development of fuller understanding and ultimate conversion." Bengel, on Mark 4:12, sees "judicial blindness" added to a blindness the hearers already had.

Recent scholars are divided in the same way, as Jung reports them (all secondhand). Craig Blomberg says that "the majority of scholars agree" that "Matthew has weakened Mark's purpose clause and turned it into a result clause." Davies and Allison write that "Matthew did not want to leave the impression that Jesus intended from the beginning to leave sinners in their plight." Robert Gundry sees an intention "to lessen the divine responsibility." R. T. France, D. A. Carson and Douglas Hare deny that the change softens anything. Jung himself concludes that "Matthew does not attempt to minimize the tone of the divine determination substantially," and that Matthew "strives to point to both divine initiative and human responsibility." Lo, citing Arland Hultgren, reads Matthew's *hoti* as emphasizing the hearers' "existing rebellious nature." He also reports Joachim Jeremias's view that Mark's *hina* may simply introduce a quotation (secondhand).

The readings above are not four labels for one view. They disagree over where the difference between hearers comes from. On Calvin's reading it comes from God's free election, with the blame resting on the hearers. On Wesley's it comes from how hearers use the grace given them. Chrysostom sets God's gift beside the hearers' own choice. The Catechism speaks of a choice to enter and of those who "stay 'outside'." They also differ over what the parables do to those outside: deepen a deserved darkness, or keep a door open. This lesson does not decide between them. A reader troubled about their own standing before God needs a pastor who knows them, not a study note.

**2. The rocky and thorny ground (13:20-22): were these hearers ever truly saved?** The parable says the rocky-ground hearer "receives it with joy" yet "has no root" and "quickly falls away" (13:20-21). The thorny-ground hearer's word is choked "and it becomes unfruitful" (13:22). Readers disagree about whether either had saving faith. The question touches the doctrine of perseverance. This curriculum's Matthew 7:13-29 lesson reports four traditions on that doctrine at length. Its Matthew 10:16-42 lesson left the question there. What follows is limited to what named readers say about these verses.

*Calvin* calls the rocky-ground response "temporary faith." He holds that such hearers "are not truly regenerated by the incorruptible seed, which never fadeth." His next sentence qualifies this: "Still, those persons who take delight in the word of God, and cherish some reverence for it, do in some manner believe." He concludes: "as that Spirit is never extinguished, so it is impossible that the faith, which he has once engraven on the hearts of the godly, shall pass away or be destroyed."

*Wesley* reads the two soils differently from each other. Of the rocky ground: "No deep work of grace: no change in the ground of his heart." Of the thorny ground, he says the hearer "hath root in himself is deeply convinced, and in a great measure inwardly changed." Then the thorns spring up "till they gradually choke it, destroy all its life and power, and it becometh unfruitful."

*The Catholic Church's Catechism* does not cite this parable on the point, so what follows is its general teaching. It states that faith "is an entirely free gift that God makes to man. We can lose this priceless gift." It continues: "To live, grow and persevere in the faith until the end we must nourish it with the word of God" (§162).

*Chrysostom* (Homily 44) emphasizes change. In a field, he says, rock stays rock, "but in the things that have reason it is not so. There is such a thing as the rock changing, and becoming rich land." He reads the good soil, named last, as "not suffering them to despair, but giving a hope of repentance."

The text gives each side something to point to. Luke's form says the rocky-ground hearers "believe for a season" (Luke 8:13). Matthew's says such a hearer "has no root" (13:21). This lesson does not decide what "believe" and "no root" add up to.

**A misuse to name plainly.** Isaiah's "this people" and Matthew's "them" have often been read as a standing verdict on the Jewish people as a whole. Gill is one example. He writes that "the judicial blindness here predicted was to go on among them, until the land of Judea was utterly destroyed by the Romans." Poole says the words are found in the New Testament "applied to the Jews," and Calvin says Paul quotes them in Acts 28 "to charge the Jews with obstinate malice." Several things can be checked against the text. First, the hearers in 13:2 are a crowd on a Galilean shore. Second, the disciples whose eyes are blessed in 13:16 are themselves Jews. Chrysostom says so: "these too were Jews." Third, in Isaiah the words were addressed to "this people" in the prophet's own day, and the chapter ends with "the holy seed" as a stump that remains (Isaiah 6:13, BSB). The Catholic Church's Second Vatican Council rejected the collective reading directly: "the Jews should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God, as if this followed from the Holy Scriptures" (*Nostra Aetate* 4, 1965).

**A pastoral note on "bad soil."** Some readers finish this parable afraid that they are the path, the rock or the thorns. The readers named above give the parable different jobs, but none of them presents it as a test for diagnosing yourself. Dale Allison (Working Preacher, 2008) calls Matthew 13 "a sort of mini theodicy," an account of why Jesus' message met such a mixed response. He writes that the parable "implicitly exhorts believers (who should recognize a bit of themselves in all four seeds) to join the fourth sort of hearer." Elisabeth Johnson (Working Preacher, 2011) warns against "equating oneself or one's community with the good soil," and observes that "Jesus does not use the parable to exhort hearers to 'be good soil,' as though we could make that happen." Calvin, on 13:23, writes that "every one who does not fall off from the sincere worship of God is reckoned a good and fertile soil." Chrysostom, on the thirtyfold yield, says that "he that brings forth sixty is saved also, and not he only, but also the producer of thirty." These readers differ about the second dispute above. Their words are reported here so that a worried reader can see how they described the parable's purpose. They are not reported to settle anyone's standing. If this fear weighs on you, talk with a pastor who knows you. If it keeps returning or becomes overwhelming, talk with a licensed counselor as well.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, a model of the shape and not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 13:22: "The seed sown among the thorns is the one who hears the word, but the worries of this life and the deceitfulness of wealth choke the word, and it becomes unfruitful" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the hearer does not reject the word; the word is "choked" by two named things. Chrysostom notes the wording: "He said not, the world, but the care of the world; nor riches, but the deceitfulness of riches." Inference (this lesson's): for a rural crowd, the picture was of something good crowded out slowly by ordinary pressures, not overturned by one decision.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Ordinary worry and the pull of money can slowly crowd out attention to God's word. Noticing what is crowding it out is part of hearing.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the two named pressures, and the slow, crowding way they work. Not carried forward: any verdict on whether a particular person is "thorny ground," any claim that hardship or poverty is itself a spiritual failure, and any timetable for fruit.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: an explained parable, functioning as a warning and a description. It is not a command and not a test of salvation. Promise scope: 13:23 describes the good-soil hearer as one who "bears fruit and produces a crop." It does not promise a measurable yield. The three different yields (13:23) mean that even the good soil is not uniform. Calvin writes that "we have no right to despise those who occupy a lower degree of excellence."

**Modern domain and situation.** Work and money. A father working two jobs after a layoff has stopped going to his small group and rarely opens his Bible. He tells a friend he feels like "thorny ground" and wonders whether he ever really believed.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: honest examination without self-condemnation. One faithful response: he names to his pastor or a trusted friend what is crowding his attention. He finds one small, sustainable practice, such as reading a short passage on his commute. He asks his church about practical help, and he looks into free financial counseling for the money pressure itself.

**Cautions.** This parable is not a tool for labeling oneself or others (Johnson's caution, in Positions). Financial strain is a real burden; a nonprofit credit counselor can help with it in ways a study note cannot. If worry has become constant, or comes with hopelessness, a doctor or a licensed counselor is the right next step. In the United States the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988) is available at any hour. The bridge above is an example for the learner to test, not counsel for any particular person.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Matthew has *hoti* ("because") where Mark and Luke have *hina* ("so that"), yet Barnes says "there is no difference" and Jung argues that Matthew's divine passives in 13:11-12 ("has been given," "will be given," "will be taken away") keep the stress on God's initiative. Can one conjunction settle the question? What else in 13:10-17 would you weigh?
2. Davies and Allison observe (as Jung reports) that the text "runs smoothly if 13:14-15 is omitted." Read 13:13 and then 13:16 aloud without it. What does the passage lose or gain? What evidence besides smoothness would decide whether Matthew wrote it?
3. Luke's rocky-ground hearers "believe for a season" (Luke 8:13); Matthew's hearer "has no root" (13:21). Calvin calls this "temporary faith"; Wesley distinguishes the rocky ground from the thorny ground, which he says was "in a great measure inwardly changed." Which words in Matthew 13:20-22 would you need to define before you could choose between them?
4. Matthew's yields run from a hundredfold down, and Mark's run up from thirtyfold. Calvin calls Jerome's reading of the three yields as "virgins, widows, and married persons" a case of "absurdly tortured" gradations. What, if anything, should a reader make of the order and the numbers?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 13:1-23 in order, from the boat to the explanation. (b) State the disciples' question in the BSB's words. (c) Name the change of mood between Isaiah 6:9-10 and Matthew 13:14-15, and the Greek translation Matthew follows.

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) the scene, the parable and the three Gospels' yields; (1:00-2:30) "because" and "so that," then Isaiah 6:9-10 in the Hebrew (commands) against the Septuagint and Matthew (descriptions); (2:30-3:30) the first dispute, with all four readings in their own words; (3:30-4:15) the soils and the second dispute, pointing to lesson 15; (4:15-5:00) the misuse named in Positions, ending on one open question.

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: "Grading 13:14-15 as an explicit quotation of Isaiah overstates it, since Matthew changes Isaiah's commands into statements; that is a paraphrase, not a quotation." (Check: what does the Septuagint of Isaiah 6:9-10 read, and how closely does Matthew's Greek follow it?) A second runs the other way: "Reporting a 'mercy' reading of 13:13 softens Jesus' plain words; 'not to them' (13:11) and the Isaiah quotation are judgment." (Check: Ellicott and Chrysostom each speak of both judgment and mercy here. Where does each one place the weight?)

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) The passage does not identify the "them" of 13:11-15 with the Jewish people as a whole. The crowd is on a Galilean shore, and the disciples blessed in 13:16 are Jews too. (b) Matthew 13:20-22 does not use the words "saved" or "lost" of the rocky or thorny hearers. Whether they had saving faith is a question readers bring to the text (Positions 2). (c) The yields in 13:8 and 13:23 do not make the smaller yields anything less than good soil. All three are yields of the same good ground, and Calvin says the master "bestows the general designation, good, even on inferior soils."

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words in both texts:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Isaiah 6:9-10 | quotation | explicit | Fulfillment formula naming Isaiah; Matthew follows the Septuagint's statements, not the Hebrew's commands |
| Mark 4:1-20 | parallel | strong | Same sequence and near-identical parable; yields reversed, "so that" for "because," "forgiven" for "heal" |
| Luke 8:4-15 | parallel | strong | Same sequence; only "a hundredfold," "believe for a season," "by persevering produce a crop" |
| Luke 10:23-24 | parallel | strong | Same saying in a different scene; "kings" for "righteous men," "desired" for "longed" |
| 1 Peter 1:10-12 | allusion | plausible | Two commentators say Peter builds on the saying; little shared wording, and the shared verb "long" is used of angels |

Or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`. The candidate considered and not authored is Genesis 26:12, "Isaac sowed seed in the land, and that very year he reaped a hundredfold" (BSB). It is the only Old Testament verse in this app's BSB with "hundredfold" (a script search), and several commentators on 13:8 cite it (Ellicott, Gill, Bengel, the Pulpit Commentary, Vincent), but as evidence that such a harvest was possible, not as a passage Matthew points to. Meyer says such details "should not be pressed." The Greek differs as well: the Septuagint of Genesis 26:12 has *hekatosteuousan krithen*, "barley yielding a hundredfold," and Matthew has *hekaton*. No source read for this lesson argues that Jesus or Matthew meant to evoke Isaac. Graded honestly, this would be a shared word, not a connection. Psalm 78:2, quoted at 13:35, falls outside this passage and is left to the lesson that covers it.

## Sources and Limits

- **Every BSB quotation in this lesson and its five connection files was checked by script against this app's own corpus** (`web/public/bible/BSB/*.json`) before and after drafting.
- **Every count and "only" claim about the BSB was produced by a script over the shipped files** ("He who has ears," "the prophecy of Isaiah," "ever hearing," "message of the kingdom," "hundredfold," and the identity of Matthew 13:15 with Acts 28:27). These count English words in the BSB, not Greek or Hebrew words.
- **The Greek and Hebrew rest on Bible Hub's pages**, fetched by raw HTTP request and saved: Swete's Septuagint of Isaiah 6 and Genesis 26, the Hebrew interlinear of Isaiah 6:9-10, the parallel-edition pages for the Greek verses cited, and Thayer's entry on *anapleroo*. None is a critical edition. Brenton's English Septuagint page returned unreadable data and was not used. Rahlfs's Septuagint is reported only through Lo. The Targum of Isaiah was not read; it is reported through Lo and Gill.
- **Read directly:** Chrysostom's Homilies 44 and 45 on Matthew (New Advent); Calvin's *Harmony* on Matthew 13 and Wesley's *Explanatory Notes* on Matthew 13 (both via Bible Hub; Calvin not checked against a critical edition); Catechism of the Catholic Church §§162 and 546; *Nostra Aetate* 4 (vatican.va); Chang-Wook Jung, *Journal of Biblical Text Research* 26 (2010), full PDF; Jonathan Lo's 2019 chapter (innsbruck university press), full PDF; Working Preacher commentaries by Dale Allison (2008), Elisabeth Johnson (2011) and John T. Carroll (2023).
- **Read through the Bible Hub commentary aggregator** (raw pages saved): Ellicott, Barnes, Meyer, Bengel, Gill, Poole, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, the Expositor's Greek Testament, the Cambridge Bible, the Pulpit Commentary and Vincent, on Matthew 13:8, 13:13, 13:14, 13:15 and 13:17, Mark 4:12, 1 Peter 1:10 and Genesis 26:12.
- **Secondhand only:** Davies and Allison, Blomberg, Gundry, France, Carson, Hare, Hagner, Stanton, Stendahl and Metzger, all through Jung; Hultgren, Snodgrass and Jeremias, through Lo. None of their works was read.
- **Gill, Calvin and Poole write harshly about Jews on these verses.** Gill is quoted once, as an example of the misuse. Their readings of the parable are reported for what they say about the parable, not as neutral judgments about any people.
- **Traditions not reported:** No Eastern Orthodox source on 13:10-17 or 13:20-22 was located beyond Chrysostom, who is a Father for both East and West. That is a sourcing gap here, not a claim that the tradition is silent. On perseverance, this lesson relies on lesson 15 for the Westminster Confession and the 1610 Remonstrance. The Catechism paragraph quoted here (§162) does not cite this parable.
- **Not done:** the major critical commentaries on Matthew (for example Davies and Allison's ICC volume, Luz, or Hagner) were not read directly. Neither was any monograph on Isaiah 6:9-10 in the New Testament, such as Craig A. Evans's work, which Jung cites. A future pass could test the interpolation and textual claims against them.
