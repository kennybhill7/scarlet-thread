Matthew 15:1-20 begins with a question about hands. "Then some Pharisees and scribes came to Jesus from Jerusalem" and asked why His disciples "break the tradition of the elders," since "They do not wash their hands before they eat" (15:1-2, BSB). Jesus answers with a question of His own, about a vow that could keep a son from supporting his parents, and with a quotation: "Isaiah prophesied correctly about you" (15:7). Then He turns to the crowd: "A man is not defiled by what enters his mouth, but by what comes out of it" (15:11). This lesson lays Isaiah 29:13 beside the Hebrew and the Greek Septuagint, because Matthew's last line follows the Greek. It reads the two laws Jesus quotes about parents where they stand. It reads the Jewish texts on handwashing, vows and the honor of parents directly, with their dates, because Christian preaching has often used this passage to picture Judaism as empty ritual. And it compares Matthew with Mark 7, which has a sentence about food that Matthew lacks.

## Context

**What happens, in order (15:1-20).** Hands (15:1-2); the counter-question and the Law on parents (15:3-6); Isaiah (15:7-9); the saying to the crowd (15:10-11); the plant and the blind guides (15:12-14); the private explanation, ending "but eating with unwashed hands does not defile him" (15:15-20).

**Who asks, and what Matthew leaves out.** The questioners came "from Jerusalem" (15:1). The BSB's "some Pharisees and scribes" renders the SBL, Nestle, Westcott-Hort and Tischendorf texts, which have no article; the Byzantine, Greek Orthodox and both Textus Receptus texts read *hoi apo Hierosolymōn grammateis kai Pharisaioi*, "the scribes and Pharisees from Jerusalem." Mark explains the custom for its readers: "the Pharisees and all the Jews do not eat until they wash their hands ceremonially" (Mark 7:3, BSB). Matthew has no such explanation. In Matthew's BSB the word "tradition" occurs only three times, all here (15:2, 15:3, 15:6).

**Handwashing in Jewish sources, with their dates.** The Torah requires priests to wash: "Whenever they enter the Tent of Meeting or approach the altar ... they must wash with water" (Exodus 30:20, BSB). Wikipedia's article on handwashing (a tertiary source) notes that this "did not pertain to the general public, nor their eating foods with washed hands." A rule for meals appears in rabbinic literature, which is later than Matthew. The Mishnah, compiled in the late second or early third century CE (per Wikipedia), states: "They wash hands for [eating] unconsecrated [food], and [second] tithe, and for terumah [heave-offering]. But for sacred food they must immerse [their hands in a mikveh]" (Mishnah Chagigah 2:5, Kulp translation via Sefaria). The Babylonian Talmud (compiled, per Wikipedia, between 70 CE and the early seventh century, "more likely ... in the middle of the sixth century") gives the rule a history, but in its account the decree concerned holy food, not ordinary meals. It quotes a teaching that "Shammai and Hillel decreed impurity on the hands," and then raises a difficulty, since Solomon is also credited with the rule: "At the time that Solomon instituted the ordinances of eiruv and of washing hands," a Divine Voice praised him. Its answer is that Solomon "decreed impurity on hands to prohibit contact with consecrated items, and Shammai, Hillel, and their disciples came and decreed impurity on hands even to prohibit contact with teruma," the priests' portion (Shabbat 14b-15a, William Davidson translation). It also reports that when Hillel and Shammai first issued the decree, "the people did not accept the decree from them," and that their disciples later reissued it and it was accepted. These are traditions recorded centuries after the figures they name. They cannot show exactly what a Pharisee required around 30 CE. The same Wikipedia article says plainly: "What regulations were already in place during the late Second Temple period is unclear."

Two more details bear on how the rabbis classed the rule. The Talmud treats the impurity of hands as a decree of the sages, in contrast to cases it calls impure "by Torah law" (Shabbat 14a-14b). And the Mishnah, in a debate about hands, records the principle that "the laws of the Torah may not be argued from the laws of the scribes" (Yadayim 3:2, Kulp). The rabbis themselves, then, kept their own rules distinct from the Torah's. The rule was also taken seriously. In one tradition in the Mishnah, Rabbi Judah says that the man the court excommunicated was "Eliezer the son of Hanoch who cast doubt against the laws concerning the purifying of the hands" (Eduyot 5:6, Kulp).

**The gift devoted to God (15:3-6).** Jesus sets two quotations from the Law against the questioners' practice. "For God said, 'Honor your father and mother' and 'Anyone who curses his father or mother must be put to death.'" (15:4, BSB). The first is Exodus 20:12, "Honor your father and mother, so that your days may be long in the land that the LORD your God is giving you" (BSB), without its promise. The second is Exodus 21:17, "Anyone who curses his father or mother must surely be put to death" (BSB). That second law is a capital law of ancient Israel's code. Calvin's *Harmony* reads its use here as "intended to inform us, that it is no light or unimportant precept to honor parents, since the violation of it is so severely punished." Nothing in Matthew uses it to call for punishment.

Then the practice: "But you say that if anyone says to his father or mother, 'Whatever you would have received from me is a gift devoted to God,' he need not honor his father or mother with it. Thus you nullify the word of God for the sake of your tradition" (15:5-6, BSB). Matthew's Greek word is *dōron*, "gift." Mark gives the Hebrew term: "'Whatever you would have received from me is Corban' (that is, a gift devoted to God)" (Mark 7:11, BSB). In this app's BSB, "Corban" occurs only there. The Expositor's Greek Testament takes the honor in question as shown "in the practical way of contributing to their support": the son keeps back what his parents "would have received" from him.

The vow formula is attested outside the Gospels: Josephus, writing after 94 CE (per Wikipedia), reports that Theophrastus listed among foreign oaths "that called Corban," which Josephus glosses as an oath that "can only be found among the Jews: and declares what a man may call A thing devoted to God" (*Against Apion* 1.22, Whiston translation). The Mishnah treats *konam* and similar words as "substitutes for the term offering [korban], and the vow takes effect" (Nedarim 1:2, Davidson). The Torah required vows to be kept: a man who vows "must not break his word; he must do everything he has promised" (Numbers 30:2, BSB). A vow against benefiting a family member was therefore a real problem in Jewish law. The Mishnah records one case: "It happened to one in Beth Horon that his father was forbidden to benefit from him" (Nedarim 5:6, Kulp). And it records a rule for releasing such vows: "Rabbi Eliezer says: They release a vow [by reference] to the honor of his father and mother but the Sages forbid." The same passage goes on: "But the Sages admit to Rabbi Eliezer that in a matter concerning himself and his father and mother one may release a vow [by reference] to the honor of his father and mother" (Nedarim 9:1, Kulp; the Hebrew, read on Sefaria, says the same). So in the Mishnah, a vow that harms one's parents can be opened by appeal to the honor owed them. Meyer (via Bible Hub) cites Nedarim 5:6 and 9:1 to show "That such vows, leading to a repudiation of the fifth commandment, were actually made and held as binding"; 9:1 also shows the later sages making room to undo them. The Mishnah is well over a century later than Jesus' ministry, so it cannot tell us what the scribes of 15:5 taught. The Expositor's Greek Testament cautions that "Ye say" gives "not the ipsissima verba of scribe-teaching" but "that drift as Jesus Himself understood it."

Rabbinic texts define honor as personal care: "What is considered honor? He gives his father food and drink, dresses and covers him, and brings him in and takes him out" (Babylonian Talmud Kiddushin 31b, Davidson). The next lines ask, "From whose funds must one give his father food and drink?" and rule that "it must be paid from the money of the father"; the son's cost is "the neglect of his work" (Kiddushin 32a). The Catechism of the Catholic Church says that "As much as they can," grown children "must give them material and moral support in old age and in times of illness, loneliness, or distress," and that "Jesus recalls this duty of gratitude" (§2218).

**Two small textual notes.** In 15:4 the SBL, Nestle 1904 and Westcott-Hort texts on Bible Hub read "God said"; the Byzantine, Greek Orthodox, Tischendorf and both Textus Receptus texts read "God commanded, saying" (the KJV's wording). Mark 7:10 has "For Moses said." In 15:6 the SBL, Nestle and Westcott-Hort texts read "the word of God" (the BSB's wording); the Byzantine, Greek Orthodox and both Textus Receptus texts read "the commandment of God"; Tischendorf reads "the law of God."

**Isaiah 29:13, line by line (15:7-9).** "You hypocrites! Isaiah prophesied correctly about you" (15:7). The verb "prophesied" occurs in Matthew's BSB only here and at 11:13. In Isaiah the words concern "These people" of "the city of Ariel where David camped" (29:1, 29:13), within a book that calls itself "the vision concerning Judah and Jerusalem" (1:1, BSB). Checked against this app's BSB, the Hebrew interlinear and the Septuagint on Bible Hub:

So Matthew's "in vain" matches the Septuagint, not the Hebrew. Calvin notes that "The words of the prophet run literally thus: their fear toward me has been taught by the precept of men," and holds that "Christ has faithfully and accurately given the meaning." Gill (via Bible Hub) reports that Grotius thought "there was a various reading, which is followed by the Septuagint, and the evangelist"; Gill thinks Christ "either added it himself" or drew on Isaiah 1:11. The Cambridge Bible says the quotation "does not follow precisely either the LXX. version or the Hebrew text."

Readers also differ on "about you." Calvin: "Although Isaiah (29:13) did not prophesy for futurity alone, but had regard to the men of his own age, yet Christ says that this prediction relates to the Pharisees and scribes, because they resemble those ancient hypocrites with whom the prophet had to contend." Barnes (see Positions 3), on Isaiah 29:13, says Jesus "quoted it as strikingly descriptive of the people when he lived, not as saying that Isaiah referred directly to his times." Chrysostom (Homily 51) calls it "a prophecy in exact accordance with His sayings, and from the very first proclaiming beforehand their wickedness." Whichever reading is taken, the words were first a prophet's charge against his own people, preserved in Israel's own Scriptures.

**What enters the mouth (15:10-11, 15:15-20).** The verb translated "defile" is *koinoō*, literally "make common," in every edition Bible Hub lists for 15:11. In Matthew's BSB, "defile" and its forms occur four times, all in this passage (15:11, 15:18, 15:20 twice). Matthew's list in 15:19 has seven items: "evil thoughts, murder, adultery, sexual immorality, theft, false testimony, and slander." Mark's list (7:21-22) has thirteen. The Expositor's Greek Testament notes that Matthew's run from murder to false testimony gives "breaches of Sixth, Seventh, Eighth, and Ninth Commandments in succession," and the Cambridge Bible that "The enumeration follows the order of the Commandments."

The ending is where the two Gospels differ most. Mark's private explanation ends with a comment in parentheses: "(Thus all foods are clean.)" (Mark 7:19, BSB). Matthew has no such sentence. His last words return to the opening question: "but eating with unwashed hands does not defile him" (15:20). The Greek of Mark 7:19 itself is divided. The SBL, Nestle 1904, Westcott-Hort and Tischendorf texts read the masculine participle *katharizōn*, which, as Rudolph notes, most English translations set in parentheses as Mark's comment. The Byzantine, Greek Orthodox and both Textus Receptus texts read the neuter *katharizon*, which the KJV renders as part of Jesus' sentence about digestion, "purging all meats." Ellicott (via Bible Hub) prefers the first construction: "He saith this . . . and in so saying, cleanseth all meats." What Matthew's silence on this point means is the first question in Positions.

**The plant and the blind guides (15:12-14).** Both sayings follow the disciples' report that "the Pharisees were offended" (15:12): "Every plant that My heavenly Father has not planted will be pulled up by its roots. Disregard them! They are blind guides" (15:13-14). Readers differ on what the plant is. Calvin reads persons ("it was rather to men that Christ referred"). Chrysostom reads "of themselves and of their traditions," against the Manichaeans, who took it to be "spoken of the law"; his reason is that "if God said, Honor your father and your mother, how is not that of God's planting, which was spoken by God?" Barnes (see Positions 3), Meyer, the Expositor's Greek Testament and the Cambridge Bible read doctrine or tradition; the Cambridge Bible calls the plant "the doctrine of the Pharisees." The "them" of 15:14 refers to the people the disciples named in 15:12. In Matthew's BSB, "blind guides" occurs three times: here and in the woes of chapter 23 (23:16, 23:24). Luke has the blind leading the blind in another setting (Luke 6:39). Calvin connects the plant to Isaiah 60:21, "the branch of My planting" (BSB), a link graded in its own connection.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 15:1-20 is built, not on what the construction is meant to teach.

**A frame of hands.** The question in 15:2 is about unwashed hands, and the last clause of 15:20 answers it. In Matthew's BSB, "wash" occurs in 15:2 (and at 6:17 and 27:24), and "unwashed" only in 15:20. Between them the subject moves from hands to parents, to Isaiah, to the mouth, to the heart.

**Three audiences.** Jesus speaks to the questioners (15:3-9), to "the crowd" (15:10-11), then to the disciples (15:12-20).

**Mouth and heart.** "Heart" occurs three times in the passage. The first is Isaiah's "their hearts are far from Me" (15:8); the last two are in the explanation, where "the things that come out of the mouth come from the heart" (15:18) and "out of the heart come evil thoughts" (15:19). Matthew puts the Isaiah quotation after the gift, where Mark has it before (Mark 7:6-13), so in Matthew the line about lips and hearts comes just before the saying about the mouth. The text itself does not draw the link.

## Positions

Two questions draw named, published disagreement, and a misuse needs to be named plainly.

**1. Does Matthew's version set aside the food laws?** Matthew lacks Mark's "(Thus all foods are clean.)" Readers explain that difference in three main ways.

*Jesus speaks of food here, more guardedly than in Mark.* Chrysostom (see Positions 3) reads 15:11 as Jesus "casting out also the observance of meats." He holds that Jesus "does not yet venture distinctly to set Himself with boldness against the meats," and that the closing words about hands are a veil: Jesus "veiled it by His conclusion, saying, But to eat with unwashen hands defiles not the man ... that He might seem to have had His occasion from thence." He adds that "Mark says, that cleansing the meats, He spoke this. He did not however express it ... for neither could they endure to be told it by Him thus distinctly." Ellicott says the principle in 15:11, "in its ultimate tendency," "swept away the whole Levitical system of ceremonial purity," and adds that "It went, as the amazement of the disciples showed, far beyond their grasp as yet." The Expositor's Greek Testament says the saying "virtually abrogates the Levitical distinctions between clean and unclean." Calvin takes 15:11 as "a general statement" that food does not pollute, because "outward things are by nature pure." The Catechism, commenting on Mark's version rather than Matthew's, says that "Jesus perfects the dietary law, so important in Jewish daily life, by revealing its pedagogical meaning through a divine interpretation" (§582).

*Not abolished here, but foreshadowed.* Meyer: "he is not saying anything against the Mosaic regulations relating to meats, though one cannot help regarding what he does say as so applicable to these, as to bring into view the prospect of their abrogation as far as they are merely ceremonial ... and, as a consequence of this latter, the triumph of the idea which they embody, i.e. their fulfillment (Matthew 5:17)." The Pulpit Commentary (see Positions 3): "We do not see that Christ here abrogated the Levitical Law, but he certainly prepared the way for its supersession and transformation," noting that "some distinctions were maintained in apostolical times."

*Handwashing only; the food laws are not in question.* David Rudolph (2002) argues from Matthew's ending. In his words, Matthew "forms an inclusio between Matthew 15:1-2 and 15:20, and emphasizes that Jesus' teaching from beginning to end is focused on the postbiblical practice of ritual handwashing; the biblical dietary laws are not at issue." He goes on: "By stripping the Markan account of all its explanatory insertions for Gentiles, Matthew restores an account of Jesus' teaching that is more in keeping with Jesus' original intent." He reads even Mark 7:19b as "a halakhic comment by Mark" for Gentile believers, not an ending of the food laws for Jews. He reports that several Matthew scholars, Davies and Allison among them, "hold that Matthew's community observed the biblical dietary laws" (citing them through David Sim, not read here).

*Objections each reading faces.* Against the first, as a reading of Matthew: Rudolph points to Matthew's own last clause, to its omission of Mark's sentence, and to Peter's later words, "I have never eaten anything impure or unclean" (Acts 10:14, BSB), which he takes to show that Peter knew of no such ruling. Against the second: Chrysostom reads 15:11 itself as "casting out also the observance of meats," not as a later prospect; Rudolph objects to "abolition vocabulary" for Mark's parenthesis as "anachronistic," since it "disregards the validity of these laws for Jewish Christians who were still well represented in the Church of Mark's day." Against the third: Chrysostom's account of 15:20 as a deliberate veil is a direct reply, and Ellicott cites the same Acts verse to show only how "slow" the disciples were to grasp the principle. In the same Gospel Jesus says, "Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets" (5:17, BSB), a verse each side reads in its own way. This lesson does not decide among them. A reader working out how this bears on their own eating, Jewish or Christian, needs their own community's teachers or a pastor, not a study note.

**2. What kind of tradition is condemned?** Jesus charges His questioners with breaking "the command of God for the sake of your tradition" (15:3). Christian traditions differ on how far the charge reaches. Calvin draws a broad rule from 15:1-9: "all modes of worship invented by men are displeasing to God." Yet he says the handwashing "could not in itself be charged with wicked superstition"; the fault was "that they did not think that God could be properly worshipped in any other way." And he rejects using 15:13 for the broad rule: "It is a mistaken view of this passage which some have adopted, that all the inventions of men ... must be rooted up and perish." The Catechism reads the charge narrowly: Jesus "disavowed certain human traditions of the Pharisees that were 'making void the word of God'" (§581). It distinguishes apostolic "Tradition" from "the various theological, disciplinary, liturgical or devotional traditions," which "can be retained, modified or even abandoned under the guidance of the Church's Magisterium" (§83). Chrysostom separates the two examples in the passage. Washing hands "had no shade of contrariety to the law," so Jesus "brings forward another tradition, which is opposed to the law." The rabbinic texts describe their own tradition in their own terms. Pirkei Avot opens: "Moses received the Torah at Sinai and transmitted it to Joshua, Joshua to the elders," and the Men of the Great Assembly said, "make a fence round the Torah" (Avot 1:1, Kulp). As noted in Context, the Mishnah itself keeps "the laws of the scribes" apart from "the laws of the Torah" (Yadayim 3:2). These are rulings addressed to the Jewish community that keeps them, not claims about anyone else, and the texts are later than Matthew. This lesson reports these accounts and does not rank them.

**3. A misuse to name plainly: "empty ritualism."** A long line of Christian commentary has read 15:1-20 as Jesus' inward religion set against a Jewish religion of outward forms, as commentaries read for this lesson show. Jamieson-Fausset-Brown write of "The drivelling nature of their multitudinous observances," and go on to describe the scribes' handling of the divine law in similarly scornful terms. Barnes says the traditions, "now contained" in the Talmuds, are "regarded by the Jews as more important than either Moses or the prophets." Ellicott attributes the handwashing rule to pride and contempt for ordinary people. Chrysostom, quoted in Positions 1 for his reading of 15:11, says a few lines later that the food laws comprise "all the religion of the Jews"; the Pulpit Commentary turns 15:13 into a claim about what "The Jews would not see." Calvin's comment also turns the passage, in harsh terms, against the Roman Church of his day. Readings like these move from one dispute with Pharisees and scribes from Jerusalem (15:1) to a verdict on a people and its law.

Amy-Jill Levine names the pattern ("Matthew and Anti-Judaism," 2007). Pastors who proclaim that Jesus came to welcome "outcasts and marginals," she writes, "give the impression that 'Judaism' is characterized by oppressive purity laws, xenophobia, and misogyny, which create those outcasts and marginals." She adds that "purity does have numerous positive lessons from sanctification of the body to resistance to assimilation," and she commends reading Matthew 23 "as instructing the church," which, she says, defuses the impression that the Pharisees stand for "the Jews." She does not discuss 15:1-20 (her Matthew 15 section treats the Canaanite woman, 15:21-28); her points here are general.

Four things can be checked. First, Matthew 15 names Pharisees and scribes who came "from Jerusalem" (15:1). It does not say "all the Jews," as Mark 7:3 does, and in Matthew's BSB the phrase "the Jews" never occurs in chapter 15. Second, Jesus answers from Israel's own Scriptures: the Decalogue (Exodus 20:12), the Law (Exodus 21:17) and Isaiah, whose words were first a prophet's charge against his own people. Third, the Jewish texts read above show a tradition that argued about handwashing, distinguished its own rules from the Torah's, defined honoring parents as personal care, and made a way to release a vow that harmed one's parents. Those texts are later than Matthew, and they cannot show what the questioners of 15:1 taught. They do show that later rabbinic law also made the honor of parents a ground for releasing a vow. Fourth, the passage's own sharpest words, "hypocrites" (15:7) and "blind guides" (15:14), are spoken to or about particular people in the scene. A reader who leaves this passage picturing Judaism as rules without heart has taken that picture from commentators, not from Matthew 15's wording, which names particular people.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage states in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. This is a model of the shape, not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** "But you say that if anyone says to his father or mother, 'Whatever you would have received from me is a gift devoted to God,' he need not honor his father or mother with it. Thus you nullify the word of God for the sake of your tradition" (Matthew 15:5-6, BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: Jesus sets a gift devoted to God against the command to honor parents, and treats the gift as no excuse when it withholds support. Inference: for hearers who took both vows and parents seriously, the point would be that a religious act cannot cancel a duty God had already commanded. This step goes beyond what the text states and is labeled as this lesson's own.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** A religious commitment should not become a way to avoid a clear obligation of care to one's own parents.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the conflict between a religious pledge and a parent's need, and the ruling that the pledge does not cancel the duty. Not carried forward: the specific vow formula; any claim about what particular scribes taught (see Context); the death penalty of Exodus 21:17, which Jesus quotes to show the weight of the command and which no source read here applies today; and any claim that giving to God is suspect.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: an example given in a rebuke, carried to a later reader by analogy. It is not a rule for dividing money between church and family in every case. Promise scope: Jesus quotes the command without its promise ("so that your days may be long," Exodus 20:12), and the passage makes no promise of its own.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family finances and church giving. A woman has pledged a fixed monthly gift to her church's building fund. Her widowed father's medicine costs rise, and he asks her for help. She is tempted to tell him, sincerely, that her spare money is "already committed to the Lord."

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: honoring a parent through practical support. One faithful response: she tells her church's treasurer that she needs to reduce her pledge for a season, and she helps her father with the cost, perhaps along with her siblings. She does not treat the pledge as a reason to refuse him, and she does not treat helping him as failing God.

**Cautions.** This bridge does not mean that every request from a parent must be granted, that adult children must give up their own families' needs, or that anyone must stay under a parent's control. Honoring a parent does not require accepting abuse. If caring for an aging parent is overwhelming, or if there is abuse or neglect in either direction, a pastor, a counselor, a doctor or a local social-services or elder-care agency, not a study note, is the right next step. In the United States, the Eldercare Locator, a public service of the Administration for Community Living (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116), connects families to local Area Agencies on Aging. Nor should the passage be used to accuse anyone of hypocrisy for keeping a religious practice. This is a worked example, not counsel for any particular family.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Matthew's "in vain" matches the Septuagint, not the Hebrew. Calvin says Jesus "faithfully and accurately" gave Isaiah's meaning; Grotius, as Gill reports him, proposed a different Hebrew reading. What would you need to know to tell those two explanations apart?
2. The Mishnah later lets a vow be released by reference "to the honor of his father and mother." Does that make the scene in 15:3-6 easier or harder to picture, and why?
3. Matthew ends on unwashed hands, Mark on "(Thus all foods are clean.)" If Matthew used Mark, what are the possible reasons for leaving out Mark's comment, and which could be tested?
4. The plant and the blind guides follow the report that the Pharisees "were offended" (15:12). Calvin reads persons, Chrysostom persons and their traditions, the Cambridge Bible doctrine. What in 15:12-14 would you weigh to decide?

## Sources and Limits

- **Read directly, with raw copies saved:** every source in the source list. Bible Hub's commentary pages are an aggregator, not checked against printed editions; Calvin is the Calvin Translation Society English; Rudolph (described by the journal as an educator in the Messianic Jewish community) and Levine were read as text extracted from hosted PDFs; the Hebrew of Nedarim 9:1 was checked on Sefaria.
- **Secondhand or tertiary:** Grotius is known here only through Gill; Lightfoot only through the commentators; the Matthew scholars Rudolph names (Davies and Allison, Saldarini and others) only through Rudolph, who cites them through David Sim. Dates for the Mishnah, the Babylonian Talmud and *Against Apion*, and the caution about Second Temple handwashing, come from Wikipedia (tertiary) and are approximate.
- **The rabbinic texts are later than Matthew.** The Mishnah (late second or early third century) and the Babylonian Talmud (most likely mid-sixth century, per Wikipedia) report rules and attributions, including the decree credited to Solomon and to Hillel and Shammai, that cannot be dated or confirmed from these texts. They are used to show how Jewish law later treated handwashing, vows and parents, not to describe the questioners of 15:1.
- **Some commentators read here characterize the Pharisees, or Judaism, in hostile terms** (Jamieson-Fausset-Brown on 15:8 and 15:13; the Pulpit Commentary on 15:13; Barnes on 15:2 and 15:5, where he also compares Corban to "taboo" in the Sandwich Islands; Ellicott on 15:2; the Pulpit Commentary on 15:11; Chrysostom in parts of Homily 51, including "all the religion of the Jews"; Calvin on 15:1-6, who also attacks the Roman Church). Where such commentators are quoted for a textual point, the quotation is limited to that point. Where they show the misuse named in Positions, they are cited as examples of it. Calvin's claim that the gift rule profited the priests is not used; no source read here supports it.
- **Not done:** modern critical commentaries on Matthew and Isaiah were not consulted. Rudolph (2002) is the only recent scholarly treatment of the food question read here; no recent scholar arguing the first reading in Positions 1 for Matthew was read (Rudolph quotes Gundry and Hurtado, on Mark). Levine's 2007 article does not discuss 15:1-20; its Matthew 15 section treats 15:21-28. No Eastern Orthodox source beyond Chrysostom was located; that is a sourcing gap, not a claim about the tradition. The Hebrew word Grotius proposed was not seen; Gill's page omits it.
- **Every BSB-attributed quotation in this lesson and its five connection files was printed from `web/public/bible/BSB/*.json` before drafting and re-checked afterward** by a script asserting each is a literal substring of the BSB text. Every count or "only" claim was produced by a script over the same files, case-insensitive and on word boundaries: "tradition" in Matthew only at 15:2, 15:3, 15:6; "defile" and its forms four times, all in 15:11-20; "blind guides" at 15:14, 23:16, 23:24; "Corban" only at Mark 7:11; "prophesied" in Matthew at 11:13 and 15:7; "unwashed" in Matthew only at 15:20; "heart" three times in 15:1-20; seven items in Matthew 15:19 and thirteen in Mark 7:21-22.
