Matthew 14:1-21 puts two meals side by side. At the first, a ruler's birthday feast, a promise made in front of guests ends with a prophet's head "on a platter" (14:8, BSB). At the second, in a "solitary place" (14:13), five loaves and two fish feed "about five thousand men ... in addition to women and children" (14:21). Between them stands one short sentence: "When Jesus heard about John, He withdrew by boat privately to a solitary place" (14:13). This lesson reads both scenes with their parallels. For John's death the most important one is outside the Bible: the Jewish historian Josephus, who tells the same death with a different cause, a named prison and no banquet. For the feeding, the parallels are Mark, Luke and John, and two older stories readers have heard behind it, Elisha's twenty loaves and the manna in the wilderness. Each is graded by what the texts actually share in Greek, not only in English. Two disputes are reported and left open: whether the "took ... blessed ... broke ... gave" of 14:19 looks ahead to the Last Supper, and what named readers think happened in the wilderness.

**A note before reading.** This passage narrates the killing of a man for what he said, and the grief of those who buried him. If you have lost someone to violence, or are afraid for your own safety, a study note is not the right support. In the United States, the VictimConnect Resource Center (call or text 1-855-4VICTIM, 855-484-2846, or chat online at victimconnect.org) supports victims and survivors of crime, and the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988) is available at any hour.

## Context

**The shape of the passage.** Herod hears about Jesus and says, "This is John the Baptist; he has risen from the dead!" (14:2, BSB). Matthew then steps back to tell how John died (14:3-12), and returns to Jesus, who withdraws, heals, and feeds the crowd (14:13-21).

**Tetrarch and "king."** Matthew calls Herod "the tetrarch" at 14:1, the only use of that word in Matthew's BSB (a script search), and "the king" at 14:9. Bengel notes the difference ("strictly tetrarch; see Matthew 14:1"), and the Expositor's Greek Testament calls the royal title "only by courtesy." Mark calls him "King Herod" (Mark 6:14). Ellicott identifies him as the son of Herod the Great who ruled Galilee and Peraea.

**Herodias and "not lawful."** John had said, "It is not lawful for you to have her" (14:4), but Matthew does not say which law he meant. Barnes and Poole point to Leviticus 18:16: "You must not have sexual relations with your brother's wife" (BSB). John Chrysostom (Homily 48) sets the rule beside the levirate law of Deuteronomy 25:5, under which a man married his brother's widow if the brother died "without a son." Because Herodias had a child, Chrysostom says, "this marriage was no longer permitted." Josephus agrees that the marriage broke Jewish law: Herodias "took upon her to confound the laws of our country, and divorced herself from her husband while he was alive" (Antiquities 18.5.4, Whiston).

**The daughter.** Matthew never names her; the BSB has "the daughter of Herodias" (14:6) and "the girl" (14:11). The name Salome comes from Josephus, who says Herodias's first husband "had a daughter, Salome" (18.5.4). Meyer, Bengel and the Cambridge Bible take the dancer to be this Salome. In the BSB the name Salome occurs only at Mark 15:40 and 16:1, for a different woman (a script search). The text says the request was "prompted by her mother" (14:8) and that Herod "ordered" and "sent" (14:9-10).

**The birthday.** A birthday appears only three times in the BSB: Pharaoh's (Genesis 40:20), and Herod's in Matthew 14:6 and Mark 6:21. Barnes, Gill and Bengel cite Genesis 40:20 for the custom of a royal birthday feast. Mark's Herod swears "up to half my kingdom" (Mark 6:23), words a script search finds elsewhere only in Esther (5:3, 5:6, 7:2). Matthew does not have them.

**Josephus on the same death.** Josephus mentions John while explaining why Herod Antipas lost a war to Aretas, king of Arabia, whose daughter Antipas had divorced in order to marry Herodias (18.5.1). The two accounts, set side by side without harmonizing:

| | Matthew 14:3-12 | Josephus, Antiquities 18.5.2 and 18.5.4 (Whiston) |
|---|---|---|
| Why John was killed | John had said, "It is not lawful for you to have her" (14:4); Herodias's daughter asks for his head (14:8) | Herod "feared lest the great influence John had over the people might put it into his power and inclination to raise a rebellion" and thought it best "by putting him to death, to prevent any mischief he might cause" |
| The people | Herod "was afraid of the people" and so did not kill John (14:5) | Herod's fear of John's following is why he killed him |
| Where | "in the prison" (14:10), unnamed | "Macherus, the castle I before mentioned" |
| The occasion | a birthday banquet, a dance, an oath before guests | none mentioned |
| Herodias's first husband | "his brother Philip" (14:3) | Antipas "lodged with Herod," his half-brother (18.5.1); Herodias "was married to Herod [Philip], the son of Herod the Great" (18.5.4), where "[Philip]" is Whiston's insertion |
| Herodias's daughter | unnamed | "Salome" |
| Afterward | John's disciples bury him and tell Jesus (14:12) | "some of the Jews thought that the destruction of Herod's army came from God, and that very justly, as a punishment of what he did against John" |

Josephus also calls John "a good man." The two accounts agree on the ruler, the victim, the unlawful marriage and an execution in custody. They differ on the cause, the setting and one name. How or whether to combine them is not settled here; the first-husband question is taken up in Positions 1.

**Elijah is not named here.** In Mark and Luke, people around Herod say Jesus may be Elijah (Mark 6:15; Luke 9:8). Matthew 14 does not use the name (a script search); Matthew has already called John "the Elijah who was to come" (11:14), and later Jesus says, "Elijah has already come, and they did not recognize him, but have done to him whatever they wished" (17:12). Several commentators read Herodias through Jezebel, the queen who threatened Elijah's life (1 Kings 19:2). Bengel: "This princess was hostile to the latter Elias, as Jezebel to the former." Ellicott on 14:5: "In Herodias, as in Jezebel, there was no halting between two opinions." MacLaren calls her "the Jezebel to this Ahab," and Jamieson-Fausset-Brown on Mark 6:20 says she "wrought upon him, as Jezebel upon Ahab." Matthew 14 names neither Jezebel nor Ahab, and the stories end differently: Elijah "ran for his life" (1 Kings 19:3), and John is killed. Teach-Back 5 explains why this comparison is not authored as a connection.

**The withdrawal (14:12-13).** Only Matthew has John's disciples go and tell Jesus (the Pulpit Commentary: "Matthew only"; Mark 6:29 ends at the tomb). Then: "When Jesus heard about John, He withdrew" (14:13). The verse gives no reason, and commentators supply different ones. Ellicott proposes "the strong personal emotion which the death of one whom Jesus had known and loved could not fail to cause" and a wish to avoid popular excitement. Calvin proposes that Christ withdrew "in order to keep his trembling disciples at a distance from the flame." Meyer reads a precaution, "lest His own death should be precipitated." Mark and Luke tie the retreat to the apostles' return instead (Mark 6:30-31; Luke 9:10). Bengel: "It is not said with what manifestation of feeling Jesus received this announcement"; he adds, "doubtless He received it as it befitted the Lord." The text records that the news came, that Jesus withdrew, that the crowds followed, and that He had compassion and healed (14:13-14). It does not say what He felt, and this lesson does not offer the scene as a pattern for anyone's grief.

**The only miracle in all four Gospels?** Ellicott calls the feeding "the only miracle recorded by all the four Evangelists," and the Cambridge Bible says the same. Wikipedia, a tertiary source, words it "the only miracle—aside from the resurrection—recorded in all four gospels." Scripted searches of this app's BSB fit the narrower claim. "Twelve basket" occurs only in Matthew 14:20, Mark 6:43, Luke 9:17 and John 6:13. The walking on the sea, which follows in Matthew, Mark and John, has no Lukan account. The servant's ear is cut off in all four Gospels, but only Luke has Jesus heal it (Luke 22:51). All four feeding accounts use the Greek *kophinos* for the baskets; Matthew keeps a different word, *spyris*, for the four thousand (15:37; 16:10).

**The counts.** "Five loaves" appears in Matthew at 14:17, 14:19 and 16:9; John has "five barley loaves" (John 6:9). The phrase "in addition to women and children" occurs in the BSB only at Matthew 14:21 and 15:38 (a script search). The Pulpit Commentary: "Only Matthew mentions the presence of other than men." Ellicott adds that "all the four use the word which emphasises the fact that all the five thousand were men" (*andres*).

**"A solitary place" and "a desolate place."** These two BSB phrases (14:13, 14:15) render one Greek adjective, *eremos*, the Septuagint's word for the wilderness where Israel ate manna (Exodus 16:14). Matthew has no manna vocabulary; a script search finds "manna" nowhere in Matthew's BSB. The explicit manna link belongs to John's account, where the crowd says, "Our fathers ate the manna in the wilderness, as it is written: 'He gave them bread from heaven to eat.'" (John 6:31, BSB). Chrysostom draws on the wilderness when reading Matthew: "that which in the wilderness seemed to the Jews marvellous, (they said at least, Can He give bread also? Or prepare a table in the wilderness?) this He shows forth in His works." The question he quotes is Psalm 78:19-20. Bengel sets the leftovers against the manna: they "were preserved for future eating, not, like manna, as a memorial."

**Elisha's twenty loaves (2 Kings 4:42-44).** "'Give it to the people to eat,' said Elisha. But his servant asked, 'How am I to set twenty loaves before a hundred men?' ... So he set it before them, and they ate and had some left over, according to the word of the LORD" (BSB). Barnes, on 2 Kings 4:43, calls this "a faint foreshadowing of our Lord's far more marvelous feeding," naming shared details: "the distribution through the hands of others; the material, bread; the surprised question of the servant; and the evidence of superfluity." Keil and Delitzsch call it "a type of the miraculous feeding of the people by Christ," with "this distinction between them, that the prophet Elisha did not produce the miraculous increase of the food, but merely predicted it." The Greek overlap is real but thin; it is laid out in conn-matt14-feeding-2kings4. Chrysostom, for his part, points to Elijah's flour and oil (1 Kings 17:16), not to Elisha.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 14:1-21 is put together. They are claims about the text's shape, not about what that shape is meant to teach.

**A flashback inside a frame.** The Expositor's Greek Testament notes that the "for" of 14:3 "implies that the following story is introduced to make the king's theory intelligible." The story returns to the present at 14:12-13, when John's disciples bring the news to Jesus.

**Two meals.** The guests at Herod's feast are *synanakeimenoi*, "those reclining with him" (14:9; BSB "his guests"). The crowd in the wilderness is told *anaklithenai*, "to recline" (14:19; BSB "to sit down"). Both verbs mean reclining at a meal and share the prefix *ana-*, though they are built on different roots. One meal is a birthday feast ending in a death; the other is bread and fish in an empty place, ending with leftovers. This contrast is this lesson's own observation, and no source read here argues that Matthew built it on purpose.

**"Withdrew."** The BSB's "withdrew" occurs eight times in Matthew (a script search). Twice it follows news about John: "When Jesus heard that John had been imprisoned, He withdrew to Galilee" (4:12) and "When Jesus heard about John, He withdrew" (14:13). At 12:15, after a plot, the same order appears as in 14:13-14: "Jesus withdrew from that place. Large crowds followed Him, and He healed them all." Meyer compares 14:13 with 4:12 and 12:15.

**Through the disciples' hands.** Jesus tells the disciples, "You give them something to eat" (14:16), and in the end "He broke the loaves and gave them to the disciples, and the disciples gave them to the people" (14:19). Bengel calls the disciples' role "a prelude to their future administration." The Pulpit Commentary notes that John's account does not have the disciples distribute, and that "Christ's work through his agents" is "an important point with the synoptists."

## Positions

Three questions in this passage draw named, published disagreement. None is settled here. Each reading is given in its holders' words where a source was read directly, and marked as secondhand where it was not.

**1. Herodias's first husband: Philip, or Herod?** Matthew 14:3 and Mark 6:17 call him "his brother Philip." Josephus calls him "Herod," a son of Herod the Great by Mariamne the high priest's daughter, and gives the name Philip to the tetrarch who later married Herodias's daughter (18.5.4). Luke 3:19 says only "his brother's wife Herodias." Two readings are on record.

*The Gospel name reflects a confusion.* The Pulpit Commentary reports that "many critics (e.g. Ewald; Schurer, I. 2:22) suppose the account in Matthew and Mark to be mistaken, and due to a confusion of Herodias with her daughter." Meyer reports Ewald's form of the view, that Mark erred, and that "the omission of the name Philip in Matthew and Luke (Luke 3:19) should be regarded as intended to correct it" (Ewald and Schurer secondhand). This reading can point to a textual detail. Bengel reports that the Latin Vulgate's Matthew has only "fratris" ("of his brother"), and his editors judge that "Φιλίππου looks like a gloss of the harmonies from Mark 6:17." In the Greek editions on Bible Hub, all print "Philip" at Matthew 14:3 except Tischendorf's eighth edition, which brackets it.

*He bore both names.* Meyer calls him "Herod Philip (Mark 6:17), called by Josephus simply Herod," and distinguishes him from Philip the tetrarch. Meyer grants that "No doubt it is strange that the two sons of Herod the Great should have borne the name Philip," and replies that "this was only a surname" and that Herod "had also two sons, both of whom were called Antipater." The Pulpit Commentary: "although it is curious that two sons of Herod the Great should have been called Philip, yet, in view of their being by different mothers, it cannot be pronounced impossible." Barnes writes simply "Herod Philip."

This lesson reports both readings and does not rank them. Neither affects what Matthew says John objected to.

**2. "Took ... blessed ... broke ... gave": does 14:19 look ahead to the Last Supper?** In Matthew's BSB, "spoke a blessing" occurs only at 14:19 and 26:26 (a script search). Both verses have Jesus take bread, bless, break, and give "to the disciples"; the Greek verbs are the same four in most editions. At 26:26 the RP Byzantine Majority Text reads "gave thanks" (*eucharistesas*) instead of "blessed," as Matthew 15:36 does at the second feeding. Readers agree that the acts recur. They differ on whether the recurrence carries meaning.

*A sign pointing to the Eucharist.* The Catechism of the Catholic Church: "The miracles of the multiplication of the loaves, when the Lord says the blessing, breaks and distributes the loaves through his disciples to feed the multitude, prefigure the superabundance of this unique bread of his Eucharist" (§1335). Ellicott begins with the ordinary meaning: "The act was natural and simple enough, the 'saying grace' ... of the head of a Jewish household." He goes on: "Looking, however, to the teaching which followed the miracle, as in John 6, and to our Lord's subsequent use at the Last Supper of the same words and acts ... we can hardly be wrong in thinking that ... there was present to His mind the thought that hereafter He would, through the same instrumentality, impart to souls that hungered after righteousness the gift of communion with Himself." Bengel, on "all" in 14:20, reasons from the lesser to the greater: "How much more can all partake of the one body of the Lord" in the Supper.

*Table grace and thanksgiving.* John Calvin: "In this passage, as in many others, blessing denotes thanksgiving. Now Christ has taught us, by his example, that we cannot partake of our food with holiness and purity, unless we express our gratitude to God, from whose hand it comes to us." John Chrysostom (Homily 49) reads the upward look as showing the Son's relation to the Father, and as "instructing us not to touch a meal, until we have given thanks to Him who gives us this food." The Pulpit Commentary suggests Jesus "may well have used the blessing that is still used over bread ('Blessed art thou, Jehovah our God, King of the world, that causest bread to come forth from the earth')." Meyer: "In saying grace Jesus did what was done by the father of a family," though he hears in Luke's wording "the idea of a consecrating prayer, as in the case of the Lord's supper."

Calvin's comment on the discourse later in John 6 (6:53-54) shows how one reader holds the two together. He writes that "this discourse does not relate to the Lord's Supper, but to the uninterrupted communication of the flesh of Christ, which we obtain apart from the use of the Lord's Supper." In the same comment he adds: "And yet, at the same time, I acknowledge that there is nothing said here that is not figuratively represented, and actually bestowed on believers, in the Lord's Supper; and Christ even intended that the holy Supper should be, as it were, a seal and confirmation of this sermon."

Each reading can point to something in the text. The first can point to the same four acts with the same recipients at 14:19 and 26:26. The second can point to the ordinary Jewish blessing over bread said by the head of a household, and to 15:36, where the same acts are told with "gave thanks." This lesson does not decide between them.

**3. What happened in the wilderness?** Matthew states the result, "They all ate and were satisfied" (14:20), and does not describe how the food increased. Three readings are on record. Each has met a named objection, given in the closing paragraph.

*A multiplication of the food.* Chrysostom: "The five loaves He broke and gave, and the five multiplied themselves in the hands of the disciples." Calvin reads the leftovers as showing "that the power of Christ had not only created out of nothing the food that was necessary for immediate use, but that, if it should be required, there was also provision for future wants." Ellicott: "The act was distinctly, if we accept the facts of the case, one of creative power."

*Sharing prompted by example.* Paulus, whose explanation Strauss calls "the rationalistic interpretation," was not read for this lesson; he is reported by David Friedrich Strauss and by Meyer. In Strauss's summary, Paulus held that the crowd, largely pilgrims, "cannot have been quite destitute of provisions." Jesus, "in order then to induce the better provided to share their food with those who were in want," arranged a meal and "himself set the example of imparting what he and his disciples could spare." Meyer's summary: "the hospitable example of Jesus may have induced the people to place at His disposal the provisions they had brought along with them." Among the sources read here, this is the earliest named form of the reading that the crowd simply shared its own food.

*A story shaped by earlier stories.* Strauss (*The Life of Jesus Critically Examined*, fourth German edition, translated by George Eliot) rejects both the miracle and Paulus's explanation. He argues that the narrative grew from the manna and quails of Moses and, more directly, from Elisha's loaves. "Who does not know," he writes, "that in general the legend does not easily imitate, without at the same time surpassing"? On his reading, the feeding of five thousand with five loaves outdoes Elisha's hundred with twenty. He concludes that "the evangelical narrative was designedly composed so as to convey this sense," and that if this sense belonged to popular Jewish legend, the narrative is "without doubt a product of that legend."

*Objections each reading faces.* Against the multiplication, Strauss argues that the process cannot be pictured without absurdity: "Loaves, which in the hands of the distributors expand like wetted sponges,—broiled fish, in which the severed parts are replaced instantaneously ... plainly belong to quite another domain than that of reality." Against Paulus, Strauss answers from the text: "It is altogether arbitrary to insert that distribution between the sentences, He gave them to the disciples, and the disciples to the multitude (Matt. xiv. 19), and, they did all eat and were filled (v. 20)." Meyer calls the explanation "inconsistent with the accounts of all the evangelists," and Ellicott calls it "ludicrously inadequate." Chrysostom, long before Paulus, says Jesus chose the wilderness "that no one might think that any village lying near contributed ought to the meal." Against Strauss, Ellicott answers: "It is hardly possible to imagine four independent writers—independent, even if two of them were derived from a common source—reproducing, in this way, a mere legend." This lesson reports all three readings and the objections to each, and ranks none.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, a model of the shape and not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** "'They do not need to go away,' Jesus replied. 'You give them something to eat.' 'We have here only five loaves of bread and two fish,' they answered. 'Bring them here to Me,' Jesus said" (Matthew 14:16-18, BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the disciples propose a sensible plan, "Dismiss the crowds so they can go to the villages and buy themselves some food" (14:15). Jesus refuses the plan and puts the need back in their hands. They name what they have, and He asks them to bring it to Him. Inference (this lesson's own): the scene shows the disciples' small supply brought to Jesus and handed out by the disciples themselves, which holds on every reading in Positions 3.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When a need is larger than what is on hand, what is on hand can still be brought and offered, rather than the need being sent away as someone else's problem.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: naming honestly what one has, and bringing it rather than dismissing the need. Not carried forward: any expectation that supplies will multiply, any verdict on Positions 3, and any reading of the bridge as a command to modern readers.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a narrated command addressed to the Twelve in one setting ("You give them something to eat"), carried by analogy. Promise scope: Matthew 14:13-21 contains no promise to the reader. It narrates what happened once.

**Modern domain and situation.** Church and neighborhood. A small church's food pantry opens on a Saturday with half the usual donations, and twice the usual line forms. One volunteer suggests closing early and posting a list of other agencies.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: honest stewardship of what is on hand. One faithful response: the volunteers count what they have, divide it so that everyone in line receives something, give each person the referral list as well, and tell the church plainly on Sunday what the need was. They do not promise anyone more than they have.

**Cautions.** This example is about one kind of shortfall and is not counsel for anyone's particular situation. It does not ask volunteers to give beyond what is wise for their own households. If you or your family do not have enough to eat, in the United States you can call 211 to be connected with local food programs. This passage should never be used to tell a hungry person to wait for a miracle instead of receiving help. The bridge above is an analogy the learner is testing, not a prescription.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Matthew says Herod's fear of the people kept John alive (14:5); Josephus says Herod's fear of John's following got him killed. Do these conflict, or do they describe different moments? What would you need to know to say, and is "harmonize" or "choose" the right first move?
2. Herod "wanted to kill John" (14:5), yet "The king was grieved" (14:9). The Pulpit Commentary calls this "a verbal contradiction to ver. 5, but after some weeks' or months' delay psychologically quite possible." What does Matthew gain by leaving both in?
3. Elisha's meal and the manna are both graded plausible here, Elisha for a shared sequence with thin Greek overlap, the manna for a shared setting with no shared vocabulary in Matthew. What new evidence would move either one to strong, or down to no connection at all?
4. Taking, blessing, breaking and giving are what any Jewish head of household did at a meal. How would you tell a deliberate echo between 14:19 and 26:26 from the shared shape of an ordinary meal? Does the Byzantine "gave thanks" at 26:26 change your answer?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 14:1-21 in order, including where the flashback begins and ends. (b) Quote what John said to Herod, as the BSB has it. (c) Name three differences between Matthew and Josephus on John's death.

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) the two scenes; (1:00-2:00) Josephus beside Matthew, without harmonizing; (2:00-3:00) the feeding in four Gospels, with the counts; (3:00-4:00) Elisha and the manna, and their grades; (4:00-5:00) 14:19 and 26:26, or Positions 3, ending on an open question.

3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection to one reading first. If you are stuck, here is one: "Grading the Elisha background only plausible undersells it. Command, objection about too little food, eating, leftovers: the sequence is too close to be chance." (Ask what the Greek shares and what it does not, and whether any shared word is uncommon.) A second runs the other way: "Grading 14:19 and 26:26 strong oversells it. These are just the acts of any Jewish meal, so their recurrence proves nothing." (Ask whether the grade is for the recurrence or for its meaning, and read the connection's rationale.)

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 14 does not name Herodias's daughter; the name Salome comes from Josephus (18.5.4). (b) Matthew 14:13 does not say why Jesus withdrew or what He felt; commentators supply different motives. (c) Matthew 14:19-20 does not describe how the food increased; it states that all ate and were satisfied and that twelve basketfuls were left.

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a row and defend its type and label from the words in both texts:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mark 6:30-44 | parallel | strong | Same scene in the same order with shared wording; Mark alone has the shepherdless sheep, two hundred denarii and groups of hundreds and fifties |
| John 6:1-15 | parallel | strong | Same miracle and numbers told independently; John alone has Philip, the boy, barley loaves and the Passover |
| 2 Kings 4:42-44 | allusion | plausible | Command, objection, eating and leftovers; thin Greek overlap; Matthew names no prophet |
| Exodus 16:4-18 | motif | plausible | Bread for a crowd in the *eremos*; no manna vocabulary in Matthew; John 6:31 makes the link for John's account |
| Matthew 26:26 | motif | strong | The same four acts and recipients; graded for the recurrence, with its meaning disputed in Positions 2 |

Or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`. The main candidate not authored is Herodias as Jezebel (1 Kings 19:1-2), with John as Elijah. Bengel, Ellicott, MacLaren and Jamieson-Fausset-Brown draw the comparison, and Matthew has called John "the Elijah who was to come" (11:14). But Matthew 14 names neither Elijah, Jezebel nor Ahab, shares no wording with 1 Kings, and ends differently: Elijah escapes alive. If authored, it would be a `motif` at `plausible` at most, and a reviewer may reasonably decide it should be. Luke 9:10-17 is a strong parallel left unauthored only to stay within five connections.
