Matthew 20:17-34 is three scenes on one road. Jesus takes the Twelve aside on the way up to Jerusalem and tells them, for the third time, that He will be killed and raised (20:17-19). Then "the mother of Zebedee's sons" asks for the two best seats in His kingdom, and the scene ends with "the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many" (20:28, BSB). Last, two blind men outside Jericho call Him "Lord, Son of David" and receive their sight (20:29-34). This lesson reads each scene against Mark and Luke, word by word. It grades the Old Testament thread most often attached to 20:28, Isaiah 53, with a named scholar on each side. It sets out six readings of what "ransom" means, each in its own tradition's words, with objections for every reading. And it reports, without settling, a puzzle Augustine already addressed: one blind man or two, leaving Jericho or approaching it. Every BSB quotation below was checked by script against this app's own Bible files.

## Context

**What happens, in order.** Jesus foretells His death to the Twelve (20:17-19). The brothers' mother asks for the seats at His right and left; He asks whether they can drink "the cup," and the seats "belong to those for whom My Father has prepared them" (20:20-23). The ten are "indignant," and Jesus answers with greatness as service, ending at 20:28 (20:24-28). Leaving Jericho, two blind men cry out, are silenced by the crowd, cry "all the louder," and are healed (20:29-34).

**The third Passion prediction (20:17-19).** Matthew has three such predictions: 16:21, 17:22-23 and 20:18-19. The recurrence of "the third day" across them is already traced by conn-matt17-passion-matt16 and is not duplicated here. What is new in the third can be checked by script over this app's BSB. It is the first to name "the Gentiles" and the manner of death: "to be mocked and flogged and crucified" (20:19). It is also the first time any form of "crucify" or "mock" occurs in Matthew; both return in the Passion narrative (chapters 26-27). Mark adds "spit on Him" and has "after three days" (Mark 10:34). Luke adds that "everything the prophets have written about the Son of Man will be fulfilled" and that the disciples "did not understand any of these things" (Luke 18:31, 34). Matthew names no prophet here.

**Who asked: the mother, or the brothers?** In Mark, "James and John, the sons of Zebedee" ask for themselves (Mark 10:35). In Matthew their mother asks, but Jesus answers in the plural ("You do not know what you are asking"), and "the brothers answered" (20:22). Augustine says Mark "represents the sons of Zebedee to have made the request themselves," and that "it is to them rather than to her, according to Matthew no less than according to Mark, that the Lord returned His reply." Chrysostom: "It is probable both things were done. I mean, that they took their mother with them, with the purpose of making their entreaty stronger." Calvin thinks it "probable that, being restrained by bashfulness, they had the dexterity to employ their mother." Meyer sees no "essential difference between the present passage and Mark 10:35." The Cambridge Bible: "For once St Matthew is more graphic and true to detail than St Mark." Matthew never names her. She appears again at the cross as "the mother of Zebedee's sons" (27:56); Mark's list at that point has "Salome" (Mark 15:40). Ellicott, Meyer and the Cambridge Bible identify her as Salome by comparing the lists. Neither Gospel states it.

**The cup, and a baptism the BSB does not print.** In Mark, Jesus asks, "Can you drink the cup I will drink, or be baptized with the baptism I will undergo?" (Mark 10:38). In the BSB's Matthew there is only the cup. The Greek editions differ: the baptism words stand in Matthew 20:22-23 in the Byzantine Majority, Greek Orthodox and Textus Receptus editions on Bible Hub, and are absent from the Nestle 1904, Westcott-Hort, SBL and Tischendorf editions, which the BSB follows. Ellicott says they "have probably been added to bring St. Matthew's narrative into harmony with St. Mark's." On the cup itself, the Expositor's Greek Testament calls it an "emblem of both good and evil fortune in Hebrew speech (Psalm 11:6; Psalm 23:5); here of suffering." Barnes cites, among others, Isaiah 51:17, where Jerusalem has drunk "the cup of His fury" (BSB), and Psalm 75:8. Calvin: "It is uncertain if, by the word cup ... our Lord alluded to the mystery of the Holy Supper; but as it had not yet come into use, I choose to interpret it more simply as denoting the measure of afflictions which God appoints to every one." Chrysostom hears in "You will indeed drink My cup" the promise that "you shall be counted worthy of martyrdom." Acts 12:2 reports that Herod "had James, the brother of John, put to death with the sword." The cup Jesus calls His own returns in Gethsemane (conn-matt20-cup-matt26).

**"A ransom for many" (20:28).** The Greek, *dounai tēn psychēn autou lytron anti pollōn*, reads the same in every edition Bible Hub lists and matches Mark 10:45 after the first words (*hōsper*, "just as," against Mark's *kai gar*, "For even"). Bible Hub's concordance lists *lytron* in the New Testament only in these two verses. By script, "ransom" occurs in the BSB New Testament only there and at 1 Timothy 2:6, "who gave Himself as a ransom for all," where Ellicott notes the Greek is *antilytron*. Ellicott calls *lytron* "a price made for deliverance": "No shade of doubt accordingly rests on the meaning of the word." He adds that, "Without stating a theory of the atonement," the phrase implied that Jesus' death "was, in some way, representative and vicarious." The Cambridge Bible stresses the preposition: "Not ὑπέρ, 'on behalf of,' but ἀντί, 'in the place of.'" The Expositor's Greek Testament calls *lytron* a term "suggesting rather than solving a theological problem as to the significance of Christ's death, and admitting of great variety of interpretation." What "ransom" implies, and to whom, is the first dispute in Positions.

**Isaiah 53 behind "many."** Matthew gives 20:28 no fulfillment formula. The text most often set beside it is the fourth Servant song. In the BSB the Servant's soul "is made a guilt offering" (Isaiah 53:10); "My righteous Servant will justify many" (53:11); "He has poured out His life unto death ... Yet He bore the sin of many" (53:12). Swete's Septuagint uses the same Greek word for "many" in 53:11-12 as Matthew does, but *lytron* does not occur in Isaiah 53. R. T. France, in the 1967 Tyndale Biblical Theology Lecture (*Tyndale Bulletin* 19, 1968), argues for an allusion in Mark's form of the saying: "Most scholars take it for granted that its use in Mark 10:45 is a deliberate echo of Isaiah 53; it is hardly the word one would expect unless it had some such purpose." He concludes that "The cumulative effect of these parallels in word and thought between Mark 10:45 and Isaiah 53 is sufficient to demand a deliberate allusion by Jesus to the role of the Servant as His own." He also reports the other side. The Hebrew word for the guilt offering in 53:10 "is never translated in the LXX" by *lytron*, so C. K. Barrett and Morna Hooker (*Jesus and the Servant*, 1959) "argue for an essential difference in meaning." In France's summary, Hooker concludes that *lytron* is "an allusion to the idea of redemption in 'Deutero-Isaiah' in general, not to the Servant." Hooker was not read directly; her view comes through France, her critic. The Catholic Catechism holds that "Jesus himself explained the meaning of his life and death in the light of God's suffering Servant" (§601). The OCA's *The Orthodox Faith* quotes Isaiah 53 and says these words "tell the story of his Messianic mission." The connection conn-matt20-ransom-isaiah53 grades the link.

**"Son of David" (20:30-31).** "Jericho" occurs in Matthew only at 20:29, and "Son of David" in nine verses: 1:1, 1:20 (said to Joseph), 9:27, 12:23, 15:22, 20:30, 20:31, 21:9 and 21:15. After chapter 1 it comes from people asking for healing (9:27; 15:22; 20:30-31), crowds wondering at a healing (12:23), and the crowds and children shouting "Hosanna to the Son of David!" (21:9, 15) in the scenes that follow this one. The link from 1:1 to 2 Samuel 7:12-16 is already authored (conn-matt1-sonofdavid-2sam7), and lesson 19 reports why Matthew may tie the title to healing; neither is repeated here. Calvin reads the cry as a confession of "that person whom God had promised to be the only Author of salvation."

**Two blind men, twice.** Matthew tells of two blind men at 9:27-31 as well. By script, "two blind men" occurs in the BSB only at 9:27 and 20:30, "have mercy on us" in Matthew only at 9:27, 20:30 and 20:31, and "touched their eyes" only at 9:29 and 20:34. The settings differ: a house, a question about faith and an order of silence in chapter 9; a roadside, a silencing crowd and compassion here. Ulrich Luz, quoted by Christian Think Tank, holds that both duplications come from Matthew: "One blind man becomes two, one story becomes two," for "Matthew did not have our problems concerning historical truthfulness." R. T. France's commentary, quoted on the same page, judges "the differences between the two stories are sufficient to cast doubt on the assumption that the one story is simply a 'doublet' of the other." Meyer rejects D. F. Strauss's proposal that the Jericho pair is "a supposed reminiscence of Matthew 9:27 ff." Luz, France's commentary and Strauss are secondhand here. The connection conn-matt20-blind-matt9 grades the shared wording only.

**Greatness and service (20:25-27).** By script, "lord it over" occurs in the BSB at Exodus 9:17, Matthew 20:25, Mark 10:42, Luke 22:25, 2 Corinthians 1:24 and 1 Peter 5:3. Luke's form comes at the Last Supper: "I am among you as one who serves" (Luke 22:27). Augustine judges that "the same sentiment was expressed by the Lord on a second occasion." Calvin reads the saying as about church office: "Christ appoints pastors of his Church, not to rule, but to serve." The saying is addressed to whoever "wants to become great" or "wants to be first" (20:26-27), just after the vineyard parable closes, "the last will be first, and the first will be last" (20:16).

**A note on authority.** The Salvation Army's positional statement on spiritual abuse (approved by its International Headquarters in 2005) says that "Spiritual authority is given for the care of God's people and its misuse is a betrayal of the Spirit of Christ, contrary to Biblical principles of servant leadership." It names "authoritarian leadership" and "the suppression of criticism or alternative ideas" as marks of abusive structures. It cites other texts, not Matthew 20, and this lesson found no source it read that documents 20:25-28 being used to demand submission, so it names no such case. The text itself speaks to those who want to be great; it tells no one to accept mistreatment from a leader. If someone who claims spiritual authority over you is controlling, shaming or harming you, you deserve help from a person you trust outside that group. A study note is not that help.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 20:17-34 is built, not on what the arrangement is meant to teach.

**A prediction, then a request.** In Matthew, as in Mark, the third prediction is followed at once by a request for rank. Chrysostom draws the link: the disciples "could not understand His sayings," so far "that the sons of Zebedee at the same time came to Him, and spoke to Him of precedence." Meyer and the Pulpit Commentary tie it instead to the promise of "twelve thrones" (19:28).

**Two questions.** Jesus asks the mother, "What do you want?" (20:21), and the blind men, "What do you want Me to do for you?" (20:32). In Mark the two are identical in the BSB (Mark 10:36, 51). The seats are refused (20:23); the sight is granted at once (20:34). The pairing is the lesson's own observation.

**Right and left.** The mother asks that "one of these two sons of mine may sit at Your right hand, and the other at Your left" (20:21). By script, the only other verses in Matthew pairing "right" and "left" around Jesus are 25:33 and 27:38, where "Two robbers were crucified with Him, one on His right hand and the other on His left." Whether the echo is intended is left to the reader.

## Positions

Two disputes attach to this passage: what "a ransom for many" means, and how the Jericho accounts relate. Each reading is reported in its own words.

**1. What is the "ransom," and to whom is it paid (20:28)?** The verse does not say to whom the ransom is paid. Six readings are reported here. They are not all mutually exclusive, and several traditions hold more than one.

*Ransom paid to the devil.* In the *Great Catechism*, Gregory of Nyssa (fourth century) argues that "we had voluntarily bartered away our freedom" and that justice required paying "to the master of the slave whatever ransom he may agree to accept" (chapter 22). Seeing Christ's miracles, the enemy "saw also in Him an opportunity for an advance, in the exchange, upon the value of what he held. For this reason he chooses Him as a ransom" (chapter 23). Then "the Deity was hidden under the veil of our nature, that so, as with ravenous fish, the hook of the Deity might be gulped down along with the bait of flesh, and thus, life being introduced into the house of death, and light shining in darkness, that which is diametrically opposed to light and life might vanish" (chapter 24). The editor of the NPNF English translation notes that this teaching "is shared by Origen, Ambrose, and Augustine." The Expositor's Greek Testament also names "the view of Origen and other Fathers." Origen's own commentary on Matthew (book 16) was not read, so he is reported only secondhand.

*A ransom to neither the devil nor the Father: Gregory of Nazianzus.* In his second Easter oration (Oration 45.22), Gregory asks: "To Whom was that Blood offered that was shed for us, and why was It shed?" Since "a ransom belongs only to him who holds in bondage," he weighs both answers. "If to the Evil One, fie upon the outrage!" "But if to the Father, I ask first, how? For it was not by Him that we were being oppressed." He asks why the Son's blood would "delight the Father, Who would not receive even Isaac" (compare Genesis 22:13). His answer: "the Father accepts Him, but neither asked for Him nor demanded Him; but on account of the Incarnation, and because Humanity must be sanctified by the Humanity of God, that He might deliver us Himself, and overcome the tyrant, and draw us to Himself by the mediation of His Son."

*Satisfaction: Anselm.* Anselm of Canterbury's *Cur Deus Homo* (*Why God Became Man*) rejects the devil's rights: "I do not see the force of that argument," he writes, since "neither the devil nor man belong to any but God" (1.7). He defines sin as failing "to render to God his due," which "robs God of his own and dishonors him," and says that "every one who sins ought to pay back the honor of which he has robbed God; and this is the satisfaction which every sinner owes to God" (1.11). For Anselm, "satisfaction or punishment must needs follow every sin" (1.15); satisfaction is the alternative to punishment. The price "paid to God for the sin of man" must "be something greater than all the universe besides God," so "none but God can make this satisfaction," while "none but a man ought to do this"; therefore "it is necessary for the God-man to make it" (2.6).

*Penal substitution: Calvin.* In his commentary on 20:28, Calvin writes that Christ "declares that his life is the price of our redemption; whence it follows, that we obtain an undeserved reconciliation with God, the price of which is to be found nowhere else than in the death of Christ." In the *Institutes* (2.16.5) he cites the verse ("He himself declares that he gave his life a ransom for many (Mt. 20:28)") and states the mechanism: "Our acquittal is in this that the guilt which made us liable to punishment was transferred to the head of the Son of God (Is. 53:12). We must specially remember this substitution in order that we may not be all our lives in trepidation and anxiety."

*Eastern Orthodox: victory over death.* The Orthodox Church in America's *The Orthodox Faith* (vol. 1) reports both older answers, that "the 'payment' was made to the devil" and that it was "made to God the Father," and then quotes Gregory of Nazianzus's oration at length. It continues: "In Orthodox theology generally it can be said that the language of 'payment' and 'ransom' is rather understood as a metaphorical and symbolical way of saying that Christ has done all things necessary to save and redeem mankind enslaved to the devil, sin and death, and under the wrath of God." Christ "'paid the price' rather, we might say, to Reality Itself." Of the three "aspects" of salvation it names, the "deepest and most comprehensive" is "the destruction of death by Christ's own death." It quotes the Liturgy of St Basil: "He gave Himself as a ransom to death, in which we were held captive, sold under sin." The OCA page does not use the term *theosis* (deification). A related formula comes from Athanasius, a fourth-century bishop honored in both East and West: "He was made man that we might be made God" (*On the Incarnation* 54).

*Roman Catholic.* The Catechism says that the Scriptures foretold the death of "the righteous one, my Servant" as "the ransom that would free men from the slavery of sin" (§601). "By his obedience unto death, Jesus accomplished the substitution of the suffering Servant," and "Jesus atoned for our faults and made satisfaction for our sins to the Father" (§615). "The redemption won by Christ consists in this, that he came 'to give his life as a ransom for many' (Mt 20:28), that is, he 'loved his own to the end' (Jn 13:1)" (§622).

*Objections each reading faces, from named sources.* The ransom to the devil meets Gregory of Nazianzus's "fie upon the outrage!" and Anselm's denial that the devil "has any right in holding man" (1.7). The OCA text adds that the devil's "rights over man were won by deceit and tyranny." Anselm's satisfaction, and the Catechism's language of "satisfaction ... to the Father" (§615), meet Gregory's question "if to the Father, I ask first, how?" They also meet the OCA's statement that Christ paid the price "not to God the Father in the sense that God delights in His sufferings and received 'satisfaction' from His creatures in Him." Calvin's penal substitution meets the same OCA paragraph, which describes the view that Christ's death was "the proper punishment that had to be paid to satisfy God's wrath" and says that Christ paid the price "not in some legalistic or juridical or economic meaning." It also has a rival in Hermann Wendt's reading, reported by the Expositor's Greek Testament, that the word carries "simply the idea" that Jesus' example of service unto death "tends by way of moral influence" to free people "from every form of spiritual bondage." The Orthodox reading of the ransom as metaphor meets Ellicott, quoted in Context, who holds that "No shade of doubt accordingly rests on the meaning of the word," a price paid for deliverance. It also meets Meyer, who holds that *anti* "can only be understood in the sense of substitution in the act of which the ransom is presented as an equivalent." Gregory of Nazianzus's answer, that the Father "neither asked for Him nor demanded Him," meets Anselm's reasoning that "it is not right to cancel sin without compensation or punishment" (1.12).

*"For many."* A narrower question is how far "many" reaches. Calvin: "The word many ... is not put definitely for a fixed number, but for a large number; for he contrasts himself with all others," as in Romans 5:15, where Paul "embraces the whole human race." The Catechism: "this last term is not restrictive, but contrasts the whole of humanity with the unique person of the redeemer," and "Christ died for all men without exception" (§605). John Gill reads it as "for as many as were ordained to eternal life ... but not for every individual of human nature; for many are not all." Meyer reads "all" of the death "as an objective fact" and "many" of "the subjective appropriation of its results."

This lesson does not decide among these readings. They differ on what the ransom pays, to whom, and for whom. A reader weighing them for their own faith needs a pastor, priest or teacher of their own tradition, not a study note.

**2. One blind man or two, leaving Jericho or approaching it (20:29-34)?** Matthew has "two blind men" as Jesus and the disciples "were leaving Jericho" (20:29-30). Mark has one, "Bartimaeus, the son of Timaeus," also "leaving Jericho" (Mark 10:46). Luke has one, "As Jesus drew near to Jericho" (Luke 18:35). Three kinds of answer are reported here.

*Two or more events.* Augustine (*Harmony of the Gospels* 2.65) takes Matthew's two men and Mark's one as the same event. Mark names one because he "was of pre-eminent note and repute in that city." Luke's account, he holds, is "really narrating only a similar miracle which was wrought in the case of another blind man." He grants that "the name of the city, and the resemblance in the deed, favour the supposition that there was but one such occurrence," but asks his reader to prefer "that He wrought two miracles of a similar nature and in similar circumstances" over the view that "the gospel is unveracious." The Pulpit Commentary lists Lightfoot and Greswell with Augustine on this side.

*One event, harmonized.* Calvin rejects multiplying events. Andreas Osiander, he writes, made "four blind men out of one," and "nothing can be more frivolous than this supposition." His "conjecture" is "that, while Christ was approaching to the city, the blind man cried out, but that, as he was not heard on account of the noise, he placed himself in the way, as they were departing from the city." On the number, "at first one blind man implored the favor of Christ, and ... another was excited by his example." Mark and Luke name one "either because he was better known, or because in him the demonstration of Christ's power was not less remarkable than it was in both." Barnes holds that Mark and Luke "do not say, however, that there was no more than one," so that "If they had said that there was only one healed, there would have been a contradiction."

*A difference acknowledged.* Meyer says the difference over two men "proves that, in point of authenticity, Matthew's account compares unfavourably with the characteristic narrative of Mark, which bears traces of being the original account of what took place." The Cambridge Bible: "It is of course possible that St Luke narrates a separate miracle. The only other solution is to suppose an inaccuracy in an unimportant detail." Donald Hagner (Word Biblical Commentary, writing on 8:28), as quoted by Christian Think Tank, sees the same phenomenon as with the two demoniacs in "Matthew's doubling of the single blind man of Mark 10:46-52." Luz, quoted in Context, reads both doubled stories as Matthew's own. The Pulpit Commentary takes a related view without speaking of error: "we weary ourselves in vain in seeking to harmonize every little detail in the Gospel narratives," because "Inspiration extends not to petty circumstances."

*Objections each reading faces.* Against two or more events, the Pulpit Commentary grants that it is "not absolutely impossible" but calls it "altogether improbable" and an "extravagant hypothesis." Against Calvin's harmonization stands the same commentary's judgment that "we weary ourselves in vain in seeking to harmonize every little detail," and Augustine's own choice to treat Luke's account as a second miracle rather than fit it into one event. Against a difference in Matthew's account, the Pulpit Commentary (on 20:30) says that "St. Matthew is doubtless accurate," since "Tradition might easily drop one of the sufferers ... but it is not likely to have multiplied one into two." Barnes's point that the other Gospels never say "only one" also applies.

This lesson does not decide among these readings. They disagree about how far the Gospel writers' details can be fitted together, and about what such differences would imply.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from the passage in its own setting to a modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key; your own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 20:26-27: "whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant, and whoever wants to be first among you must be your slave" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the saying answers a request for the two highest seats (20:21) and the anger of the ten (20:24), setting "among you" against "the rulers of the Gentiles" (20:25). Inference, the lesson's own: for disciples arguing about rank on the way to Jerusalem, the saying redefined who counts as great among them.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Among Jesus' followers, wanting to be first is answered by serving the others, not by gaining power over them.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the reversal of rank inside a community; the example of the Son of Man (20:28). Not carried forward: the specific seats in the kingdom, which Jesus says are "not Mine to grant" (20:23), or any claim that the word "slave" approves slavery. The saying addresses those who *want* greatness; it does not tell anyone to submit to a person who demands to be served.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a teaching about conduct inside a community of disciples, carried over by analogy. Promise scope: the passage promises no reward for serving and gives no timeline; 20:23 leaves the seats to the Father.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. A newly promoted team lead in a hospital billing office is offered a private office and the choice of the best shifts, while the rest of her team covers the evening backlog.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: using a new position for the team rather than for oneself. One faithful response: she takes her turn on the evening backlog for the first month and asks each team member what is slowing their work. She then uses her new authority to fix the two problems they name most.

**Cautions.** This is not management or employment advice. "Servant" here describes how the one with authority uses it. It does not ask her to accept unsafe hours or ask anyone on her team to accept mistreatment. A person in an unsafe workplace should contact the appropriate regulator, union or employment lawyer.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. In Matthew the request comes from the brothers' mother; in Mark, from the brothers. What in Matthew 20:20-24 would help you decide whether the difference matters for reading the scene?
2. The mother asks for one son at Jesus' "right hand" and the other at His "left" (20:21). In 27:38 two robbers are crucified "one on His right hand and the other on His left." Is the echo deliberate, a coincidence of common words, or something you cannot decide from the text?
3. Matthew 20:28 does not say to whom the ransom is paid. Which parts of the readings in Positions come from the verse, and which from other texts or from theological reasoning?
4. The blind men call Jesus "Son of David" just before the crowds of 21:9 shout "Hosanna to the Son of David!" What, if anything, does the placement add to how you read the title here?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 20:17-34 in order, from the prediction to the healing. (b) Quote 20:28 in the BSB's own words. (c) Name two differences between Matthew and Mark in these verses, and one between Matthew and Luke.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the three scenes, with Mark and Luke; (1:00-2:00) the third prediction, the mother's request and the cup; (2:00-4:00) "a ransom for many": the word, Isaiah 53 (France and Hooker), and the six readings with one objection each; (4:00-5:00) the Jericho crux and the doublet with 9:27-31, with a named reader for each view.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: "The ransom-to-the-devil idea was a fringe speculation nobody really held." Before answering, read Gregory of Nyssa's own words in Positions and the NPNF editor's note listing others who shared it. A second candidate, running the other way: "Penal substitution is just the plain meaning of 'ransom.'" Before answering, reread the Expositor's Greek Testament on the word in Context, and Gregory of Nazianzus and the OCA in Positions.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 20:28 does not say to whom the ransom is paid; every answer in Positions supplies that from elsewhere. (b) Matthew 20:20-28 does not name the mother; "Salome" comes from comparing Matthew 27:56 with Mark 15:40. (c) Matthew 20:25-27 does not tell anyone to submit to a leader who demands it; it addresses those who want to be great.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words of both texts. The five attached to this lesson:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mark 10:32-52 | parallel | strong | Same three scenes in the same order; the ransom saying is identical in the BSB and nearly so in Greek; Mark has the brothers ask and one blind man |
| Luke 18:31-43 | parallel | strong | Same prediction and a Jericho healing in the same order; Luke has no request or ransom saying here, one man, and "drew near to Jericho" |
| Isaiah 53:10-12 | allusion | plausible | "Many" and the giving of life are shared, but *lytron* is not in Isaiah 53; France argues a deliberate allusion, Hooker (via France) denies it |
| Matthew 9:27-31 | motif | strong | "Two blind men," "have mercy on us" and "touched their eyes" recur only in these scenes in the BSB; whether they are one event is disputed |
| Matthew 26:39-42 | motif | strong | The cup Jesus says He must drink (20:22-23) returns in Gethsemane; the noun is missing from 26:42 in some Greek editions |

The Isaiah row is this lesson's thread connection to the Old Testament. It is graded plausible, not strong, because the decisive word is absent from Isaiah 53 and a named scholar contests the link, though the Catechism and the OCA text read the passages together. Two candidates were considered and not authored. **Isaiah 51:17 and Psalm 75:8** behind "the cup" (20:22): Barnes lists five Old Testament verses and Poole seven, and Ellicott and the Expositor's Greek Testament add others (Psalm 11:6, 23:5, Ezekiel 23:33), so a link to any one verse is not warranted (`no_warrant_yet`). **Matthew 17:24-27** behind "ransom": the Expositor's Greek Testament proposes the temple tax as the clue, since the half-shekel is called a *lytron* in the Septuagint of Exodus 30:12. But *lytron* does not occur in Matthew 17, so the link remains one commentator's proposal.

## Sources and Limits

- **Read directly, with raw copies saved:** Augustine's *Harmony of the Gospels* 2.64-65, Chrysostom's Homily 65, Gregory of Nazianzus's Oration 45 and Athanasius's *On the Incarnation* (New Advent); Gregory of Nyssa's *Great Catechism*, chapters 22-24, and Calvin's *Institutes* 2.16 (CCEL); Anselm's *Cur Deus Homo* (Fordham); Calvin's Harmony on Matthew 20 and Meyer on Matthew 20 (Bible Hub); the Bible Hub commentary pages on 20:20, 20:22, 20:28, 20:29 and 20:30; the Catechism (§§601, 605, 615, 622); the OCA's *Orthodox Faith* page on redemption; France's 1968 lecture; Swete's Septuagint of Isaiah 53; the Bible Hub Greek parallel texts and the Strong's entry for *lytron*; and the Salvation Army statement.
- **Secondhand only:** Origen (through the Expositor's Greek Testament and the NPNF editor's note to Gregory of Nyssa); Morna Hooker and C. K. Barrett (through France, who argues against them); Ulrich Luz, R. T. France's NICNT commentary, and Donald Hagner (quoted by Christian Think Tank, an apologetics site); D. F. Strauss, Osiander and Hermann Wendt (through Meyer, Calvin and the Expositor's Greek Testament).
- **France's article** was read from an OCR text of a PDF scan. The OCR garbles Greek and Hebrew, so only English sentences were quoted, and each was checked against the extracted text; one OCR slip ("gr~nted" for "granted") was corrected in quoting. France writes on Mark 10:45; this lesson applies his argument to Matthew 20:28 because the two verses are identical in Greek after their first words.
- **Not read:** Origen's *Commentary on Matthew* book 16 (a draft English translation is posted on Academia.edu but could not be fetched without an account), Hooker's *Jesus and the Servant*, modern critical commentaries on Matthew, and any Orthodox commentary on Matthew 20. Gustaf Aulén's *Christus Victor* was not read and is not cited. The OCA page names no author, and the lesson does not attribute it to one. No Jewish reading of Isaiah 53 is reported here; lesson 16 reports individual, remnant and national readings of the Servant through a tertiary source.
- **Polemic not reproduced.** Calvin's commentary on 20:28 includes an attack on Catholic "satisfactions" (penances), and Calvin's *Institutes* and other older commentaries use polemical language elsewhere. None of it is quoted.
- **Spiritual abuse.** No source read for this lesson documents Matthew 20:25-28 being used to demand submission. The lesson therefore names no such case and gives no hotline. The Salvation Army statement defines the problem and does not cite this passage.
- **Bible Hub reproduces public-domain commentaries without page numbers;** attributions follow its section labels.
- **Counts.** Every count or "only" claim was produced by a case-insensitive, word-boundary Node script over this app's BSB files (English words), and its output was kept. The count for *lytron* comes from Bible Hub's concordance.
- **Every BSB quotation in this lesson and its five new connection files was checked by a Node script** against `web/public/bible/BSB/*.json`, before and after drafting.
