Matthew 7:13-29 closes both the Sermon on the Mount, begun at 5:1-2, and -- a fact about this specific curriculum, not about the passage itself -- the 30-unit pilot this lesson is the last of. Four short units run in sequence: two gates and two roads (7:13-14), true and false prophets known "by their fruit" (7:15-20), a courtroom scene in which impressive religious activity in Jesus's own name is not enough to be known by him (7:21-23), and two builders tested by the same storm (7:24-27), followed by the crowd's own reaction and Matthew's narrative comment on why it differed from their usual experience of teaching (7:28-29). Three of these four units are, on the evidence this lesson found, mostly Context and Literary Design material -- real, checkable facts about wording, structure, and parallel accounts in other Gospels, without a comparably substantive named scholarly division behind them. The third unit, 7:21-23, is not: "I never knew you; depart from Me, you workers of lawlessness!" (7:23, BSB) sits at the center of real, long-running, high-stakes disagreement across major Christian traditions about what it means for a person's own standing before God, and this lesson's Positions section below reports four such traditions in their own terms, at the same length and with the same discipline this curriculum's Matthew 5:21-48 lesson already gave a comparably serious dispute over divorce and remarriage -- without resolving which is correct.

## Context

**What the text says, in order (7:13-29).** Two gates and two roads: "Enter through the narrow gate. For wide is the gate and broad is the way that leads to destruction, and many enter through it. But small is the gate and narrow the way that leads to life, and only a few find it" (7:13-14, BSB). This app's own BSB wording is worth stating plainly against common memory: the line many readers recall is the KJV's "strait is the gate, and narrow is the way" -- this app does not ship that wording, and "Enter through the narrow gate... small is the gate and narrow the way" (BSB) is what this lesson quotes from and drafts against. No well-documented manuscript dispute over how 7:13 opens was found for this lesson (unlike some of the real textual-critical questions this curriculum has already reported elsewhere, e.g. the Lord's Prayer's doxology at 6:13); this is a translation-wording note, not a textual-critical one.

False prophets and their fruit (7:15-20): "Beware of false prophets. They come to you in sheep's clothing, but inwardly they are ravenous wolves" (7:15, BSB), tested by a repeated, near-verbatim refrain: "By their fruit you will recognize them" (7:16, BSB) and "So then, by their fruit you will recognize them" (7:20, BSB) -- the same clause opening and closing the unit, checked directly against this app's own shipped BSB text. Whether "fruit" names behavior or teaching content is itself a minor, named dispute, reported briefly in Positions below.

"I never knew you" (7:21-23): "Not everyone who says to Me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the kingdom of heaven, but only he who does the will of My Father in heaven" (7:21, BSB), followed by a scene in which people address Jesus directly on judgment day, naming specific religious activity done "in Your name": "did we not prophesy in Your name, and in Your name drive out demons and perform many miracles?" (7:22, BSB). The reply is stated once, plainly: "I never knew you; depart from Me, you workers of lawlessness!" (7:23, BSB). The Greek adverb behind "never" here, *oudepote*, is glossed by a Greek lexicon aggregator (reporting Thayer) as "not even at any time, i.e. never at all" -- an "absolute negation of time," not a claim this lesson is making on its own authority about what that negation implies for these people's prior status, which is exactly what Positions below reports four traditions disagreeing about. 7:22's three named activities -- prophesying, driving out demons, "many miracles" -- are worth stating as a plain textual observation: the text does not describe people who merely claimed to be religious in some vague sense; it describes people who did, or at minimum were remembered by the text's own narrative logic as claiming to have done, real or apparently real spiritual activity carried out in Jesus's own name, which sharpens rather than softens the warning that follows.

Two builders (7:24-27): "Therefore everyone who hears these words of Mine and acts on them is like a wise man who built his house on the rock" (7:24, BSB), tested by "the rain," "the torrents," and "the winds" (7:25, BSB); "everyone who hears these words of Mine and does not act on them is like a foolish man who built his house on sand" (7:26, BSB), tested by the identical storm, with the identical result stated in reverse: the wise man's house "did not fall, because its foundation was on the rock" (7:25, BSB), while the foolish man's house "fell—and great was its collapse!" (7:27, BSB, quoted with its own unspaced dash, not this lesson's own double-hyphen convention).

The crowd's reaction (7:28-29): "When Jesus had finished saying these things, the crowds were astonished at His teaching, because He taught as one who had authority, and not as their scribes" (7:28-29, BSB) -- again worth stating precisely against common memory: this app's own wording is "as one who had authority," not the KJV's "as one having authority."

**"By their fruit" -- behavior, doctrine, or both?** F. Dale Bruner's own survey of the commentary tradition on 7:16, reported via the Wikipedia article on that verse used here, identifies two readings without resolving between them: a behavioral reading, tracing to John Chrysostom and held by Eduard Schweizer and Ulrich Luz, under which "if their behaviour is not pious, one should not expect their words to be"; and a doctrinal reading, held by Augustine, Jerome, Martin Luther, and John Calvin, under which false prophets are "noticeable by teachings that don't conform to correct doctrine." The same article reports a close parallel in the deuterocanonical book of Sirach -- "fruit discloses the cultivation of a tree, so a person's speech discloses the cultivation of his mind" -- itself read either way depending on whether "speech" names conduct or content. This lesson's own Positions section below treats this distinction briefly, since the stakes are real but considerably narrower than 7:21-23's.

**A real tension this lesson does not resolve: "do not judge" and "by their fruit you will recognize them" sit three verses apart in the same discourse.** Matthew 7:1 opens this chapter's immediately preceding unit with "Do not judge, or you will be judged" (7:1, BSB); 7:15-20 then instructs the same audience to test others -- specifically, to recognize false prophets "by their fruit." Telling a wolf from a sheep by its fruit requires exactly the kind of evaluative judgment of another person's life that 7:1 warns against, stated here as a plain textual observation about where these two instructions sit in Matthew's own sequence, not as an argument this lesson is resolving about how they fit together. (A companion lesson in this curriculum, on Matthew 7:1-12, may address this same tension from its own side of the passage boundary; this lesson does not assume or depend on how that lesson treats it.) This tension is left open in Questions to Carry below.

**7:22's three readings of real or claimed spiritual power.** The Wikipedia article on Matthew 7:22 used here reports three distinct scholarly readings of what it means that these people prophesied, drove out demons, and performed miracles "in Your name." Davies and Allison read the activity as genuine but spiritually insufficient: "The verse does not deny that these unholy may have made correct prophecies and driven out demons." Jerome reads the power itself as unconnected to the performer's own spiritual state, citing Saul, Balaam, and Judas as biblical examples of people who exercised real power while spiritually corrupt: "To prophesy, to work wonders, to cast out daemons by divine power, is often not of his deserts who performs the works." Pseudo-Chrysostom reads the activity as counterfeit outright: demons "do not cast them out, but seem only to cast them out, the daemons acting in concert with them." This lesson reports all three without choosing one; see Questions to Carry below.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 7:13-29 is built, not on what that construction is meant to teach.

**The whole Sermon's own opening and closing frame, checked directly against the text.** Matthew 5:1-2 opens the Sermon: "Jesus saw the crowds, He went up on the mountain and sat down. His disciples came to Him, and He began to teach them, saying" (5:1-2, BSB). Matthew 7:28 closes it with a matching narrative statement: "When Jesus had finished saying these things, the crowds were astonished at His teaching" (7:28, BSB). Inside that frame, both of this unit's own parable-pairs point back explicitly to the whole intervening discourse rather than only to their own immediate context: "everyone who hears these words of Mine and acts on them" (7:24, BSB) and "everyone who hears these words of Mine and does not act on them" (7:26, BSB) both use the same phrase, "these words of Mine" -- a phrase that, read where Matthew places it, has no antecedent shorter than everything Jesus has said since 5:3. This is a checkable fact about where Matthew positions this phrase in relation to the Sermon's own stated beginning, not a claim about what the Sermon as a whole is meant to accomplish.

**Four units, each closing on a stark binary, with no middle option offered in the text's own wording.** Two gates and two roads, one leading to "destruction" and one to "life" (7:13-14); two kinds of tree, one that "bears good fruit" and one that "bears bad fruit," with no third kind of tree described (7:17-18); two builders, tested by the identical storm, with one house standing and one falling (7:24-27); and, inside 7:21-23's own scene, one group told "depart from Me" against the alternative Matthew 7:21 itself names, "he who does the will of My Father in heaven." Each of these four binaries is stated in the text's own wording, not supplied by this lesson; whether the four are meant to be read as a single escalating argument or as four separate illustrations of a shared point is not something this lesson's own observation of the pattern can establish on its own.

**7:29's authority claim lands on the word the Sermon has been enacting since 5:21.** The Wikipedia article on Matthew 7:29 used here reports that scribal teaching in this period conventionally cited prior authorities -- "a sermon by the rabbis would have been replete with references to scripture to validate each teaching" -- while Matthew's own narrative comment states the contrast plainly: "the contrast presented by Matthew is that Jesus speaks based on his own authority, not that of past figures," citing Robert Gundry, with Davies and Allison cited in support. This curriculum's own Matthew 5:21-48 lesson already identified the mechanism this closing comment is naming: six units in that passage, each built on citing what "was said to the ancients" before Jesus's own counter-statement, introduced every time by some form of "But I tell you" (5:21-48, that lesson's own Context section). 7:29's "not as their scribes" is Matthew's own narrator naming, after the fact, exactly the pattern that lesson already traced verse by verse -- a checkable link between this unit's closing comment and the rest of the Sermon's own documented rhetorical shape, not a new claim this lesson is introducing on its own.

## Positions

Two separate, named disputes attach to this passage. The first carries the most substantive, highest-stakes disagreement this entire 30-unit pilot curriculum has reported and is given the longest treatment accordingly; the second is real but considerably narrower, and is reported briefly.

**What does "I never knew you" (7:23) mean for these people's own history, and what, if anything, does this text establish about whether a once-genuine believer could ever reach the same position?** This is the single most theologically consequential passage in this unit -- comparable in real stakes to the Matthew 5:31-32 divorce dispute this curriculum's Matthew 5:21-48 lesson already treated at length -- and this lesson reports four distinctly named traditions here, in their own terms, without ranking them.

The Reformed tradition reads this text's final sentence as corroborating evidence for its own doctrine of the perseverance of the saints, not as a stand-alone proof text invented for the purpose. John Calvin's own commentary on 7:23 (quoted here via a secondary reproduction of his commentary, not independently checked against a critical edition -- see Sources and Limits) states plainly that these people's profession to Christ "is deceitful and false," and that Christ "never reckoned them among his own people, even at the time when they boasted that they were the pillars of the church." The Westminster Confession of Faith states the doctrinal structure this reading rests on, without naming Matthew 7:21-23 by verse: those "whom God hath accepted in His Beloved, effectually called, and sanctified by His Spirit, can neither totally nor finally fall away from the state of grace" (17.1), because this perseverance "depends not upon their own free will, but upon the immutability of the decree of election" (17.2). On this reading, the "many" of 7:22 performed real or claimed religious activity without ever once standing in the relationship "knew" names -- the text's own emphatic negative (Context above) describes, on this reading, an unbroken absence rather than a relationship that existed and later ended.

A pastoral, revivalist reading found in some evangelical traditions reaches a similar conclusion about this specific group of people by a different route, without building on the Reformed doctrine of election stated above. Paul Washer's widely circulated 2002 sermon on this passage, quoted here from a third-party transcript of the spoken sermon rather than a formally edited published text (see Sources and Limits), names those who "claim to be my disciples who confessed me as Lord and, yet, you lived as though I never gave you a law to obey" as the ones Christ addresses in 7:23. This reading's own emphasis is self-examination -- whether a hearer's own life shows the fruit 7:15-20 already names -- rather than a doctrine of who was or was not chosen before the foundation of the world; it shares the Reformed reading's conclusion about this text without sharing, or requiring, its confessional architecture.

The Catholic Church's Catechism cites this exact verse directly rather than only a related theme: discussing prayer and the will of God, it states that "Jesus teaches us that one enters the kingdom of heaven not by speaking words, but by doing 'the will of my Father in heaven'" (§2826, quoting Matthew 7:21 by its own footnote). The Council of Trent's Decree on Justification states a matching structure as a formal canon, without naming this verse: "If any one saith, that justifying faith is nothing else but confidence in the divine mercy which remits sins for Christ's sake; or, that this confidence alone is that whereby we are justified; let him be anathema" (Session VI, Canon XII). On this reading, "Lord, Lord" names exactly the kind of bare confidence Trent's canon addresses; what "doing the will of My Father" adds is read not as a second, separate requirement stacked alongside faith, but as the grace-enabled obedience Catholic teaching holds genuine faith itself produces -- so that 7:21-23's crowd is read as people who mistook profession for the obedient faith the text actually names.

The Arminian and Wesleyan tradition's own confessional starting point is the 1610 Remonstrance, whose fifth article states that those "incorporated into Christ by a true faith" are given grace sufficient "to enable them to persevere in the faith" -- but the Remonstrants left open, in their own stated words, "whether they are capable, through negligence, of forsaking again the first beginnings of their life in Christ," a question they said "must be more particularly determined out of the Holy Scriptures" (Article V, quoted here via a secondary source reproducing the 1610 text, not independently checked against a primary critical edition -- see Sources and Limits). This tradition's broader, later-settled position holds that a genuinely regenerate believer can, through unbelief, fall away and lose what was once real. What this lesson did not find, after a real search, is a named Arminian or Wesleyan source reading Matthew 7:21-23 itself as describing such a case, rather than describing people who were false from the start; one Arminian-identified writer located for this lesson reads this passage's own "many" as never having been saved to begin with, reserving her tradition's own apostasy argument for other texts instead (see Sources and Limits for why that source is not cited by name above). The real disagreement this tradition has with the Reformed reading, on the evidence this lesson found, may not be about what 7:21-23 itself describes so much as about what doctrinal conclusion the passage is entitled to support: the Reformed reading treats this text as corroborating evidence that no genuine believer can ever reach this position by any road; the Arminian/Wesleyan tradition denies that this specific text settles that broader question, while locating its own case for a real, reversible falling-away elsewhere in the New Testament.

These four readings are not simply four labels for one agreed conclusion. They agree that the people Jesus describes in 7:21-23 presumed on a standing with him they did not have, and that producing religious activity in his name is not, by itself, evidence of being known by him. They disagree, with real consequence for how a reader assesses their own life and the lives of people they love, about why: because such people were never elect and could not have been otherwise (the Reformed reading); because a verbally professed but untransformed faith is exposed, in the present, as never having been real to begin with (the false-convert reading); because genuine faith is inseparable from the grace-enabled obedience bare confidence alone was never sufficient to produce (the Catholic reading); or because this text names one real category of false profession without settling whether a different, once-genuine believer could reach a similar point by an entirely different road (the Arminian/Wesleyan reading). This lesson states what each tradition's own document or named representative says and does not resolve which is correct. A reader working through this question for their own standing, or anxious on behalf of someone they love, needs a pastor or counselor who actually knows them, not a study note -- the stakes here are at least as serious as the divorce question this curriculum's Matthew 5:21-48 lesson already named as needing exactly that kind of care, and the same caution applies here with at least equal force.

**Does "by their fruit you will recognize them" (7:16, 7:20) test behavior, teaching content, or both?** A narrower, considerably lower-stakes dispute than the one above, reported briefly. As Context notes, F. Dale Bruner's own survey of the commentary tradition identifies a behavioral reading (Schweizer, Luz, tracing to Chrysostom: conduct is the tell) against a doctrinal reading (Augustine, Jerome, Luther, Calvin: false teaching itself is the fruit in view). The passage's own wording -- "false prophets" already names a teaching office in 7:15, while the tree-and-fruit image in 7:16-20 is not itself specific to either conduct or doctrine -- does not by itself settle which the metaphor is reaching for, or whether it is reaching for both at once. This lesson reports both readings without choosing one.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage states in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. This is a model of the shape, not an answer key -- the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 7:24-25: "Therefore everyone who hears these words of Mine and acts on them is like a wise man who built his house on the rock. The rain fell, the torrents raged, and the winds blew and beat against that house; yet it did not fall, because its foundation was on the rock" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the two houses in this parable are not tested by ordinary weather but by the same violent storm -- "the rain fell, the torrents raged, and the winds blew" strikes both houses identically (7:25, 7:27, BSB); the difference the text reports is entirely in what was underneath each house before the storm arrived, not in anything either builder does once the storm is already underway. Inference: for the parable's own force to land, the point is not that hearing good teaching feels different from not hearing it in ordinary life -- by the parable's own account, both houses may well look the same on a calm day. The difference the text locates is a prior, often invisible choice (what foundation was laid) that only becomes visible under pressure the builder did not get to choose the timing of. This inference goes one step beyond the text's own stated wording, quoted directly in Context above, and is labeled here as this lesson's own step.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Acting on a true teaching, specifically before any pressure arrives to test it, is what the teaching's own value depends on; hearing it without acting on it leaves a person in a position that looks identical to having acted on it, right up until the moment it no longer does.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the structural point that the test comes later and is not chosen by the person being tested, and that "hearing" by itself, however attentive, is the text's own named alternative to "acting," not a lesser form of the same thing. Not carried forward: the specific images of rock, sand, rain, and wind, or any claim that this passage specifies how much time may pass between hearing a teaching and a test of it arriving -- the text states that both houses were built before the storm, not how long before.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a command carried by direct analogy from the passage's own two-part comparison ("everyone who hears... and acts," "everyone who hears... and does not act," 7:24, 7:26, BSB), not an inference stretched from a narrative detail the text does not emphasize. Promise scope: 7:24-25 states a consequence for the wise builder (the house does not fall) but does not specify when a "storm" will arrive for any individual reader, how severe it will be, or that every faithful response will be followed by a storm at all -- nothing here warrants predicting a specific present-life outcome or timeline.

**Modern domain and situation.** Workplace safety training. A warehouse employee completes a required safety certification on proper lifting technique and machine lockout procedure. Eight months pass with no incident. During a high-pressure shift with a supervisor pushing to finish a rush order, the employee is tempted to skip the lockout step "just this once" because it would save several minutes and no one is watching.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: a direct, costly act of following a known, already-learned instruction under real pressure to abandon it, not a decision made in the abstract with no cost attached. One faithful response: the employee follows the lockout procedure in full despite the time cost and the supervisor's visible impatience, because the procedure was "heard" (learned, certified) specifically in order to be acted on at the moment it is inconvenient, not only when it is easy.

**Cautions.** This does not mean every deviation from a learned procedure under pressure is a moral failure equivalent to the foolish builder -- real emergencies sometimes require real judgment calls a training module cannot anticipate, and a workplace with a genuinely unsafe or retaliatory culture around safety procedures is itself a separate problem requiring a supervisor, HR, or a regulator, not private resolve. It also does not mean every "storm" in a person's life is a direct consequence of an earlier choice to hear and not act, in the way the parable's own two builders are each responsible for their own foundation -- the text pictures a controlled case (a builder choosing their own foundation), not every hardship a person faces. The passage states a specific comparison about acting on known teaching before pressure arrives; the bridge above is an analogy the learner is testing, not a general theory of why bad things happen to people who hear good teaching.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Matthew 7:1 ("Do not judge, or you will be judged") and Matthew 7:15-20 ("by their fruit you will recognize them") sit three verses apart in the same discourse, and testing a prophet's fruit requires some real evaluation of that person's life. What would you need to see in how Matthew's own Gospel uses "judge" elsewhere, or in how these two instructions were understood by their first hearers, to tell apart a forbidden judgment of another person's standing before God from a permitted, even commanded, recognition of what their life is producing?
2. This passage's four traditions in Positions above largely agree that the specific people in 7:21-23 were never, at any point, in the relationship "knew" names (the lexical point in Context above). What would practically distinguish, in a living person's own life rather than in a doctrinal system, "being known by God" from "not being known by him" -- and is that something any person, including the person asking, could actually assess about themselves with confidence, or is 7:21-23's own warning partly aimed at the presumption that such confidence is available?
3. Davies and Allison read 7:22's prophecy, exorcism, and miracles as genuine but spiritually insufficient; Jerome reads the same activity as real power simply unconnected to the performer's holiness, citing Saul, Balaam, and Judas; Pseudo-Chrysostom reads it as counterfeit activity that only appears to succeed. These three readings are not interchangeable: they disagree about whether the power itself was real. What would you need to find elsewhere in Scripture (Numbers 22-24's account of Balaam, for instance, or Acts 19:13-16's account of exorcists who invoked Jesus's name without authority) to judge among these three, rather than assuming whichever reading already matches your own prior view of whether God's power can operate through someone with no real relationship to him?
4. The Wikipedia article on Matthew 7:23 used here reports a real scholarly disagreement, not treated at length in this lesson's own Positions section, over whether Jesus is presented in that verse as the one rendering judgment (France, Schweizer) or as a witness testifying before a separate judge (Hare, reading "I will tell them plainly" as courtroom-witness language). What would you need to check about how this exact Greek verb is used elsewhere in the New Testament, or about how this scene relates to other judgment scenes in Matthew (e.g. 25:31-46), to judge between these two readings of who is doing what in this particular courtroom image?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) name, in order, this unit's four parts and the topic each one addresses (two gates and roads; false prophets known by fruit; the "Lord, Lord" scene; two builders), plus the closing narrative comment at 7:28-29. (b) Recite the refrain that opens and closes the false-prophets unit word for word, and say at which two verses it falls. (c) State, without adding anything to the BSB's own wording, what "these words of Mine" in 7:24 and 7:26 refers back to, and where in Matthew's own text that reference begins.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the four-part structure and the whole Sermon's own 5:1-2/7:28 opening-and-closing frame (see Literary Design); (1:00-2:00) false prophets and the fruit test, including the real behavior-versus-doctrine dispute over what "fruit" names (see Context and Positions); (2:00-4:00) the "I never knew you" scene -- state plainly what all four traditions in Positions agree on before naming at least two of the four in their own terms, without picking one; (4:00-4:30) the wise and foolish builders, closing the Sermon on an image tested by pressure rather than by profession; (4:30-5:00) one open question from above, stated on purpose as the last thing taught.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first; the point of this prompt is that you do the forming, not that you pick from a list. If you are stuck, here is one worked candidate, stated at full strength: naming four traditions on "I never knew you" without saying which is correct is a way of refusing to say anything at all on the single highest-stakes question this entire curriculum has raised -- a reader genuinely afraid they might be one of the "many" in 7:22 deserves an answer, not four footnoted positions, and a study lesson that will not choose is quietly teaching that the question cannot be answered. Before you answer it, state what this lesson's own Positions section already does: it quotes each tradition's own stated document or named representative directly (Calvin's commentary, the Westminster Confession, Washer's own sermon, the Catholic Catechism's own footnoted citation of this verse, Trent's own canon, the 1610 Remonstrance's own words) rather than paraphrasing a position no one actually holds, and it names the specific, practical disagreement (why these people were never known, not merely that they weren't) rather than only the surface agreement that all four traditions find the "many" of 7:22 condemned. A second worked candidate, running the other direction: if Trent's Canon XII and the Westminster Confession's doctrine of election describe the same specific people in 7:21-23 the same way, does the deep disagreement between Reformed and Catholic theology on justification actually bear on how to read this particular text, or is this one of the places those two traditions' conclusions converge for different underlying reasons -- and if so, what does that convergence itself suggest about how much interpretive weight this one passage can bear?
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 7:21-23 names specific activities -- prophesying, driving out demons, performing miracles -- "in Your name" (7:22, BSB) and does not, in its own wording, state whether the power behind those specific acts was real, counterfeit, or genuine-but-irrelevant to the actor's own standing; that further step belongs to the three named readings in Context above (Davies and Allison, Jerome, Pseudo-Chrysostom), not to the text's own stated content. (b) Matthew 7:24-27 states that acting on Jesus's words produces a house that withstands a storm and that not acting on them produces one that falls; it does not state, in its own wording, that every person who experiences hardship failed to act on good teaching, or that every person who avoids hardship built on rock -- the parable pictures a controlled comparison (the same storm testing two builders' own prior choices), not a general claim about why anyone's life goes well or badly.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection from this passage and defend it: name its type, its evidence label, and point to the words in both texts that support that grade.
