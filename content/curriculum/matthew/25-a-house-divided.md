Matthew 12:22-37 begins with a healing and ends with a warning about words. A man "who was blind and mute" is brought to Jesus and healed "so that he could speak and see" (12:22, BSB). The crowds ask, "Could this be the Son of David?" (12:23). The Pharisees answer, "Only by Beelzebul, the prince of demons, does this man drive out demons" (12:24). Jesus answers with a divided kingdom and a strong man's house, then says that "the blasphemy against the Spirit will not be forgiven" (12:31), and ends: "by your words you will be acquitted, and by your words you will be condemned" (12:37). This lesson practices three skills. It checks a proposed Old Testament background for the strong man (Isaiah 49:24-25) in the English and the Greek, and grades it honestly. It separates an echo that belongs to Luke's wording ("the finger of God," Exodus 8:19) from what Matthew's own wording supports. And it reports how named traditions define the sin against the Holy Spirit. Many readers have feared they committed it, so the lesson also says where to find help.

## Context

**What happens, in order (12:22-37).** "Knowing their thoughts" (12:25), Jesus answers the charge in four steps: a kingdom "divided against itself" (12:25-26); the Pharisees' own "sons" who drive out demons (12:27); "if I drive out demons by the Spirit of God, then the kingdom of God has come upon you" (12:28); and the strong man (12:29). Then come "He who is not with Me is against Me" (12:30), the saying on blasphemy (12:31-32), the tree known "by its fruit" (12:33), "You brood of vipers" (12:34), and "every careless word" (12:36-37). The scene sits between the Pharisees' plot to "kill Jesus" (12:14) and their demand for "a sign" (12:38).

**"Could this be the Son of David?" (12:23).** Lesson 19 in this curriculum (Matthew 9:18-38) already treats the title "Son of David" in Matthew's healing scenes, with Novakovic and Duling, and lesson 1 carries the connection from 1:1 to 2 Samuel 7. That material is not repeated here. One point is new. The Greek question begins with *mēti*; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown notes that questions in this form "suppose doubt, and expect rather a negative answer," so the sense is "Can it possibly be?" Meyer calls it a "Question of imperfect yet growing faith." For the Pulpit Commentary, the same man is "at once the object of hope in the minds of the multitudes, and of the deepest opposition on the part of the Pharisees."

**A charge that returns.** Lesson 19 noted that the charge of 9:34 "comes back at 12:24 in a fuller form." Matthew 10:25 refers to it too: "If the head of the house has been called Beelzebul, how much more the members of his household!" (BSB). Lesson 21 covers 10:25 but does not discuss the name. Ellicott observes that the charge itself concedes something: "the Pharisees admitted the reality of the work of healing which they had witnessed."

**The name "Beelzebul," and Baal-zebub of 2 Kings 1.** In this app's BSB, "Beelzebul" occurs seven times, all in the Gospels (Matthew 10:25, 12:24, 12:27; Mark 3:22; Luke 11:15, 11:18, 11:19; checked by script). The Greek spelling varies: *Beelzeboul* in the Nestle 1904 and Byzantine texts on Biblehub, *Beezeboul* in Westcott and Hort, while older English Bibles have "Beelzebub" (the Cambridge Bible: "The MSS. vary between Beelzebul and Beelzebub"). Commentators connect the name with "Baal-zebub, the god of Ekron," whom King Ahaziah sent to consult in 2 Kings 1:2 (BSB). Ellicott takes Baal-zebub as "the 'Lord of flies'" and as a god "consulted as an oracle (2Kings 1:2) in cases of disease." Barnes reports "Beelzebul" as a form meaning "god of dung" or "filth." The Cambridge Bible gives another derivation, "Lord of the dwelling," and adds that "This meaning would be very appropriate in relation to 'the master of the house'" of 10:25. Meyer prefers that derivation too, against the "god of dung" reading. This is reported background to the name. No source read for this lesson argues that Matthew 12 is building on the 2 Kings 1 story, so it is not authored as a connection.

**Who the accusers are, and a caution.** In Matthew the speakers are "the Pharisees" (12:24). In Mark they are "the scribes who had come down from Jerusalem" (3:22), and in Luke "some of them" in the crowd (11:15, all BSB). Jesus and his disciples are themselves Jews, and so is the hope behind the crowd's question about "the Son of David." The warning about blasphemy answers what particular people said. Even so, these verses have been read as a verdict on the Jewish people as a whole. Thomas Aquinas reports such a reading from John Chrysostom, in which "the Jews are said not to be forgiven this sin," and were punished "both in the present life, through the Romans, and in the life to come" (*Summa* II-II q.14 a.3). The text says nothing of the kind. Aquinas himself, reporting Augustine in the same question, says the Lord did not say this "as though they had sinned against the Holy Ghost, since they were not yet guilty of final impenitence, but He warned them." Amy-Jill Levine (*Currents in Theology and Mission*, 2007) writes that "congregations may associate the Pharisees with 'the Jews,'" and that preachers "appropriately defuse this impression by reading Matthew 23, for example, as instructing the church." Of anti-Jewish readings of Matthew she writes, "Our task is to prevent this abuse of the text." The same applies to "brood of vipers" in 12:34. In Matthew it is spoken to particular opponents in the story (3:7; 12:34; 23:33), and it is checked as a recurring phrase in the connections below.

**Matthew's "by the Spirit of God" (12:28), and Luke's "finger of God."** Luke 11:20 is identical in the BSB except for one phrase: "But if I drive out demons by the finger of God, then the kingdom of God has come upon you." The Greek shows the same single difference: *en pneumati Theou* in Matthew, *en daktylō Theou* in Luke (Nestle 1904 on Biblehub). Commentators on Luke hear Exodus 8:19 in Luke's phrase, where Pharaoh's magicians say, "This is the finger of God" (BSB). The Septuagint uses the same two words, *daktylos theou* (Exodus 8:15 in its numbering, on Blue Letter Bible). The Cambridge Bible, Bengel ("Comp. Exodus 8:19"), Gill ("The power of God, referring to Exodus 8:19"), the Geneva notes, Ellicott and the Pulpit Commentary all name it on Luke 11:20, and Barnes does on Matthew 12:28 ("Compare Exodus 8:19"). Ellicott and the Pulpit add Exodus 31:18, where the tablets are "inscribed by the finger of God" (BSB). The Expositor's Greek Testament says Luke's phrase "recalls Exodus 8:9" (so printed on Biblehub, evidently for 8:19) and judges Matthew's "Spirit" to be "doubtless the original expression." On the evidence read, the Exodus echo is well supported for Luke 11:20, where the two words match. It is not authored as a connection for Matthew, whose wording does not use them (see Teach-Back 5).

**"The kingdom of God" in Matthew.** Matthew usually says "kingdom of heaven." In this app's BSB, "kingdom of heaven" occurs 32 times in 31 verses of Matthew, and "kingdom of God" 5 times (6:33; 12:28; 19:24; 21:31; 21:43; checked by script). Matthew's "Spirit of God" also recalls the Isaiah quotation just before this passage, "I will put My Spirit on Him" (12:18, BSB); see Literary Design.

**The strong man (12:29) and Isaiah 49:24-25.** Four commentators read on Biblehub connect it with Isaiah 49:24-25: "Can the plunder be snatched from the mighty, or the captives of a tyrant be delivered?" and the answer, "Even the captives of the mighty will be taken away, and the plunder of the tyrant will be retrieved" (BSB). The Expositor's Greek Testament says, "The parable seems based on Isaiah 49:24-25." The Pulpit Commentary says, "there is probably a tacit reference to Isaiah 49:25." Meyer says the language "may have been suggested by a recollection of Isaiah 49:24 f." Gill glosses the strong man as the devil, "see Isaiah 49:24." Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, commenting on Isaiah, reads the verse first of Israel's captivity and "Secondarily," of "Satan and Death" as "'the mighty' conquerors of man," citing Matthew 12:29. The Cambridge Bible on Isaiah reads the passage of Israel's release from Babylon and does not mention Matthew. The Greek overlap is thin. The Septuagint's captor is a *gigas* ("giant") holding *skula* (spoils), with *ischyontos* ("one who is strong") in 49:25. Matthew has *ischyros* ("strong man"), *skeuē* ("possessions") and the verbs *harpazō* and *diarpazō*. Only the root *ischy-* is shared, and Luke 11:22, not Matthew, has *skula*. The connection is graded allusion and plausible: a real picture shared with a prophet, proposed by named readers who hedge their own words.

**The words in 12:31-32.** Four times in two verses something "will be forgiven" or "will not be forgiven." The editions on Biblehub differ slightly in 12:31. The Nestle 1904 and Westcott and Hort texts end "will not be forgiven," while the Byzantine and Stephanus texts add "to men" a second time. Where Matthew has "either in this age or in the one to come" (12:32), Mark has "he is guilty of eternal sin" (3:29). Mark alone adds the reason: "because they were saying, 'He has an unclean spirit'" (3:30). Augustine told his hearers that "perhaps there is not in all holy Scripture found a more important or more difficult question." Poole says it has "much more perplexed poor Christians in their fits of melancholy and under temptations." Named readings are in Positions below.

**Tree, fruit and words (12:33-37).** The saying that "a tree is known by its fruit" (12:33) repeats the image of 7:16-20 ("By their fruit you will recognize them"), and here it is applied to speech: "For out of the overflow of the heart, the mouth speaks" (12:34). Luke 6:45 has the same sentence word for word in the BSB, in the Sermon on the Plain. Poole reads 12:36-37 as "arguing from the less to the greater": if careless words come to account, how much more the charge of 12:24. Jamieson-Fausset-Brown imagines the speakers' excuse and Jesus' reply: "It was not nothing, and at the great day will not be treated as nothing." In the BSB, "acquitted" occurs only here in the New Testament and once in the Old (Deuteronomy 25:1; checked by script). The Greek verb is *dikaiōthēsē*, which older English versions render "justified." The second dispute in Positions reports how that word has been read.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 12:22-37 is built, not on what the arrangement is meant to teach.

**"Spirit" at the hinge.** "Spirit" with a capital S occurs four times in Matthew 12 in this app's BSB: in the Isaiah quotation ("I will put My Spirit on Him," 12:18), in Jesus' claim ("by the Spirit of God," 12:28), and in the two blasphemy verses (12:31, 12:32). The chapter's other two "spirits" are lowercase, "an unclean spirit" and "seven other spirits" (12:43, 12:45). Counts were checked by script.

**Houses.** The word recurs: "every city or household divided against itself will not stand" (12:25); "a strong man’s house... Then he can plunder his house" (12:29); and, a few verses on, the unclean spirit's "I will return to the house I left" (12:44). In 10:25 Jesus is "the head of the house." If Meyer and the Cambridge Bible are right that "Beelzebul" can mean "lord of the dwelling," the name itself joins this cluster. That derivation is debated, and the cluster stands without it.

**From deeds to words.** The passage opens with a man who could not speak being made to speak (12:22) and ends on the speech of his critics: "a word against the Son of Man" (12:32), "how can you who are evil say anything good?" (12:34), "every careless word" (12:36), "by your words... by your words" (12:37). "Good," "bad" and "evil" crowd into 12:33-35 (checked by script: "good" six times, "bad" twice, "evil" four times).

## Positions

Two named disputes attach to this passage. The first reaches into readers' lives and ends with a pastoral note.

**What is the blasphemy against the Spirit (12:31-32), and can a person commit it today?** Four readings are reported here in historical order, each in its own words.

*Final impenitence (Augustine; the Catechism of the Catholic Church).* Augustine preached a whole sermon on 12:32 (Sermon 71 in the Benedictine numbering; New Advent prints it as "Sermon 21 on the New Testament"). He holds that "This impenitence then is the blasphemy of the Spirit, which shall not be forgiven," "the persevering hardness of an impenitent heart." He adds at once: "this impenitence or impenitent heart may not be pronounced upon, as long as a man lives in the flesh. For we are not to despair of any so long as the patience of God leads the ungodly to repentance." Later in the sermon he ties this to the Church: the one who speaks against the Spirit is he who "with an impenitent heart resists the Unity of the Church, where in the Holy Spirit is given the remission of sins." The Catechism of the Catholic Church (§1864) quotes 12:31 and says: "There are no limits to the mercy of God, but anyone who deliberately refuses to accept his mercy by repenting, rejects the forgiveness of his sins and the salvation offered by the Holy Spirit. Such hardness of heart can lead to final impenitence and eternal loss."

*Three meanings held together (Thomas Aquinas).* Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* (II-II, question 14) reports that "Three meanings have been given to the sin against the Holy Ghost": the earlier Fathers' literal one, "to utter a blasphemy against the Holy Spirit," as the Pharisees did when "they ascribed to the prince of devils those works which Christ did"; Augustine's "final impenitence"; and sin "through certain malice," which rejects with contempt what keeps a person from sin, so that "hope is removed by despair, and fear by presumption." Taken as final impenitence, it is unpardonable because "the mortal sin wherein a man perseveres until death will not be forgiven in the life to come." Taken in the other two senses, it is called unpardonable because "considered in itself, it deserves not to be pardoned," like a disease called incurable. He adds: "This does not, however, close the way of forgiveness and healing to an all-powerful and merciful God, Who, sometimes, by a miracle, so to speak, restores spiritual health to such men," and "We should despair of no man in this life."

*Knowing, malicious contempt for the Spirit's work (John Calvin).* Calvin, in his *Harmony of the Evangelists*, calls it "an atrocious crime, knowingly and willingly to pour contempt on the Spirit of God," committed by those who "slander his gifts and power, contrary to the conviction of their own mind." He limits it: Paul "obtained pardon, because he had done it ignorantly," and the passage "refutes also the error of those who imagine that every sin which is voluntary, or which is committed in opposition to the conscience, is unpardonable." He holds that "he who has been truly regenerated by the Spirit cannot possibly fall into so horrid a crime," and that those who have fallen into it "never rise again," for God hardens their hearts "so that they never have any desire towards repentance."

*A settled state that puts a person beyond repentance (John Piper).* John Piper, a modern evangelical pastor, answered the question directly on his *Ask Pastor John* podcast (2016). He quotes the nineteenth-century commentator Henry Alford: "It is not a particular species of sin which is here condemned... but a definite act showing a state of sin, and that state a willful determined opposition to the present power of the Holy Spirit." Piper's own conclusion: "Can a true believer, whose salvation is eternally secure in Christ, still be guilty of blaspheming the Holy Spirit? No... Christians cannot commit that kind of sin. They don’t settle in like that." In a 1984 sermon he had defined it as "an act of resistance which belittles the Holy Spirit so grievously that he withdraws forever with his convicting power so that we are never able to repent and be forgiven."

*Objections each reading faces.* Each reading has been answered from another. Against final impenitence, Calvin writes that if that were the meaning, "it would have been in vain and to no purpose for Christ to say, that it is not forgiven in the present life," and Piper's 1984 sermon rejects it for the same reason, citing 12:32 and 1 John 5:16. Aquinas's "certain malice" reading, which counts despair and presumption among the kinds of this sin, meets Calvin's rejection of "the error of those who imagine that every sin which is voluntary, or which is committed in opposition to the conscience, is unpardonable." Against Calvin's and Piper's view that the sin can be fixed during a life, Augustine answers that it "cannot be taken hold of in any one... as long as he is still in this life," and Aquinas that God "sometimes" restores even such men. Against the view that a true Christian cannot commit it, the Puritan Matthew Poole describes it in terms of one who "hath made a profession of religion and godliness, and afterwards falleth off," and admits, "We can define nothing certain in the case." Barnes narrows it in another direction, to the charge actually made against Jesus: "This sin was a very different thing from what is now often supposed to be the sin against the Holy Spirit." This lesson does not choose among them.

*"If you fear you have committed it."* Some writers address that fear directly, and only those who say it are named here. Matthew Henry's *Concise Commentary* says that "humble and conscientious believers, at times are tempted to think they have committed the unpardonable sin, while those who have come the nearest to it, seldom have any fear about it," and that "those who fear they have committed this sin, give a good sign that they have not." Two unsigned Ligonier Ministries devotionals say the same. One quotes Henry and says, "Pastors both past and present agree that a person who worries that he has committed the unforgiveable sin has not really done so." The other says, "If you are concerned about it, you can be assured that you haven’t committed it." When the question was put to R. C. Sproul in a Ligonier question-and-answer transcript, his answer as printed defined the sin as "a verbal thing" and did not repeat the line, so it is not attributed to him here. Augustine and Aquinas do not use the line either; their counsel, reported above, is not to despair of anyone in this life. Calvin and Piper reach comfort by a different route, holding that a true believer cannot commit the sin.

*A pastoral note.* For some readers this section is not abstract. Fear of having committed "the unforgivable sin" can return again and again, and no explanation seems to settle it. The International OCD Foundation describes scrupulosity as "A form of obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) involving religious or moral obsessions," and a packet for faith leaders on its website lists "That you have committed the unpardonable sin" among common obsessions. That packet makes a distinction worth knowing. A person with "a tender conscience" can "find comfort when advised by a religious leader," while people with scrupulosity "cannot have their fear or concern resolved by competent explanation." It also notes that repeated reassurance, including "lists of reassuring Bible passages," can keep the cycle going. If a reading above brings lasting peace, that is good. If the fear keeps coming back, please talk with a pastor you trust, and also with a licensed counselor. Scrupulosity is treatable, and the Foundation's fact sheet names cognitive behavior therapy with exposure and response prevention as "the primary psychological treatment," adding that treatment "may also include consultation from leaders of the patient’s faith tradition." The Foundation keeps a directory of therapists and support groups at iocdf.org/find-help. If the fear ever comes with thoughts of harming yourself, in the United States you can call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, which is available 24 hours a day. Elsewhere, call your local emergency number.

**How does "by your words you will be acquitted" (12:37) relate to justification by faith?** The BSB's "acquitted" renders the same Greek verb other versions translate "justified," and readers have asked how judgment by words fits with "a man is justified by faith apart from works of the law" (Romans 3:28, BSB) and "a man is justified by his deeds and not by faith alone" (James 2:24, BSB). Three readings are reported here.

*Words as evidence, not the ground (John Calvin).* Calvin reads 12:37 as "a common proverb" that Christ "turns" so that "a wicked speech, being the indication of concealed malice, is enough to condemn a man." "A man is justified by his words, not because his speech is the ground of his justification, (for we obtain by faith the favor of God, so that he reckons us to be righteous persons;) but because pure speech absolves us in such a manner, that we are not condemned as wicked persons by our tongue." He argues that the pairing with "condemned" supports his view of the word: "he is accounted righteous, and is acquitted before the tribunal of God." He rejects the Catholic use of the verse as an attempt "to set aside the righteousness of faith." Poole, also Reformed, draws a related point: "If there were no other text in the Bible to prove that we have need of another righteousness, than any of our own... this text alone would be enough."

*Faith as the root, with justice that increases and works judged (the Council of Trent).* The Council of Trent's Decree on Justification (1547) does not comment on Matthew 12:37, so this is the framework a Catholic reader brings to it, not an exegesis of the verse. Trent says "we are therefore said to be justified by faith, because faith is the beginning of human salvation, the foundation, and the root of all Justification," and "freely, because that none of those things which precede justification-whether faith or works-merit the grace itself" (chapter VIII). The justified, "faith co-operating with good works, increase in that justice," and Trent cites James 2:24 for this (chapter X). Each person's "whole life" is to be "examined and judged, not by the judgment of man, but of God, who... will render to every man according to his works" (chapter XVI), and "God forbid that a Christian should either trust or glory in himself, and not in the Lord."

*Faith, works and words as one character (Ellicott).* Ellicott's commentary sets the three side by side. "Justified" here means "the opposite of 'condemned,'" and "men are said to be justified by faith (Romans 3:28 et al.), justified by works (James 2:24), justified—as here—by words. All three—faith, works, words—are alike elements of a man’s character." "Of the words and the works men can in some measure judge, and they are the tests by which a man should judge himself." He concludes that "it is therefore by faith rather than by works that a man is justified before God, though the faith is no true faith unless it moulds the character."

These readings share a starting point that Barnes, Bengel and the Expositor's Greek Testament also state: words show the heart ("words are viewed as the index of a good or bad heart," the Expositor's). They differ on what "justified" names and how works and words relate to it. This lesson does not settle that.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key. Your own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 12:34-35: "For out of the overflow of the heart, the mouth speaks. The good man brings good things out of his good store of treasure, and the evil man brings evil things out of his evil store of treasure" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the saying answers a specific remark. Faced with a good act they could not deny, a man healed, the speakers explained it by the worst motive available (12:24). Bengel's gloss is that "Words exhibit the righteousness or unrighteousness, which is in the heart." Inference: for the first hearers, the warning was that how a person explains someone else's good deed shows something about the speaker.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** How we speak about other people's evident good, especially when we would prefer to think badly of them, is worth attention, because speech comes from what we store up.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: attention to what our explanations of others' actions reveal. Not carried forward: the specific charge of 12:24, the saying on blasphemy, or any claim that a particular word puts a person beyond forgiveness.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a principle drawn by analogy from a saying addressed to particular opponents. Promise scope: none. 12:35 describes, it does not promise.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. A team lead notices that whenever a rival colleague's project succeeds, she explains it to others as luck or self-promotion.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: honest speech. One faithful response: she stops offering motives she cannot know, and when the colleague's work is good she says so plainly. She does not have to pretend to like him.

**Cautions.** This bridge is not about monitoring every word for fault. A reader who finds that 12:36 ("every careless word") starts a cycle of checking and re-checking past words should read the pastoral note in Positions above. And naming real wrongdoing, with evidence and through proper channels, is not the speech this passage warns against.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. "He who is not with Me is against Me" (12:30) sits beside "whoever is not against us is for us" in Mark 9:40 (BSB). This lesson did not research how the two sayings fit together. What would you look at in each setting before deciding?
2. Matthew writes "by the Spirit of God" (12:28) and Luke "by the finger of God" (11:20). The Expositor's Greek Testament thinks Matthew's is original. What evidence would you need to decide which Gospel changed the wording, and does Matthew 12:18 bear on it?
3. Jesus says a word "against the Son of Man will be forgiven" but not one "against the Holy Spirit" (12:32). Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin and Barnes each explain the difference differently. Read 11:19 and 12:24 side by side. Which explanation fits Matthew's own sequence best?
4. The phrase "every careless word" (12:36) comes right after "brood of vipers" (12:34). Are the "careless" words of 12:36 the same as the evil words of 12:34, or a wider class?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) list the steps of Jesus' answer to the Beelzebul charge in order (divided kingdom; your sons; Spirit of God and kingdom of God; the strong man; with or against). (b) Quote 12:31 in the BSB's own words. (c) Say what Luke has where Matthew has "the Spirit of God."
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the healing, the crowd's question and the charge, with the parallels in Mark 3 and Luke 11 (see Context and connections); (1:00-2:00) the argument of 12:25-29, and the Isaiah 49:24-25 proposal with its grade (see Context); (2:00-3:30) the four readings of blasphemy against the Spirit and the objections each faces (see Positions); (3:30-4:30) the pastoral note, stated without adding to it; (4:30-5:00) tree, fruit and words, with "acquitted" in 12:37.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then answer it. Form your own first. If you are stuck, here is one: "Saying 'if you fear you have committed it, you have not' turns a hard saying into a comfort the text never offers." Weigh it against what Matthew Henry, the Ligonier devotionals, Augustine and Aquinas actually say, and against the International OCD Foundation's warning about repeated reassurance. A second candidate, running the other way: "Calvin's and Piper's view that the sin can be committed in this life will terrify sensitive believers." Answer it from Calvin's own limits (Paul's ignorance; not every sin against conscience) and Piper's "Christians cannot commit that kind of sin."
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 12:31-32 does not, by itself, say who has committed this sin. Jesus addresses a charge made by particular people, and Mark 3:30 explains the saying from that charge. (b) "brood of vipers" (12:34) does not describe a people. In Matthew it is addressed to particular opponents (3:7; 12:34; 23:33). (c) Matthew 12:37 does not, by itself, settle how words, works and faith relate in justification. The three readings in Positions all accept the verse.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Defend the type and label of one connection from the words of both texts. The four attached to this lesson:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mark 3:22-30 | parallel | strong | Same charge, arguments and blasphemy saying in the same order; "unless he first ties up the strong man" identical; Mark's "eternal sin" and 3:30 differ |
| Luke 11:14-23 | parallel | strong | Same scene; 12:27 and 12:30 identical with Luke 11:19 and 11:23; "Spirit of God" against "finger of God" |
| Isaiah 49:24-25 | allusion | plausible | Shared picture of plunder taken from a strong captor, proposed by four commentators who hedge; Greek shares only the root *ischy-* |
| Matthew 3:7 | motif | strong | "You brood of vipers" verbatim in English and Greek at 3:7, 12:34 and 23:33, always to particular opponents |

The Isaiah row is this lesson's thread back to the Old Testament, and it is graded plausible on purpose. Two candidates were considered and not authored. **Exodus 8:19** ("This is the finger of God," BSB) is a well-supported echo for Luke 11:20, which shares the two words in English and Greek, and several commentators name it there. Matthew 12:28 says "Spirit," so a Matthew-to-Exodus row would rest on Luke's wording, not Matthew's. **2 Kings 1:2** (Baal-zebub) is the reported background of the name "Beelzebul," but no source read here argues that Matthew 12 alludes to the Elijah story.
