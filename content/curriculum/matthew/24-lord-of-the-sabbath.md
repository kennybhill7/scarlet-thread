Matthew 12:1-21 holds two short Sabbath disputes and then a long quotation from Isaiah. In the first, Jesus' hungry disciples pick grain on the Sabbath, and Jesus answers with three Old Testament precedents and a claim: "the Son of Man is Lord of the Sabbath" (12:8, BSB). In the second, He heals a withered hand in a synagogue, and "the Pharisees went out and plotted how they might kill Jesus" (12:14, BSB). Jesus withdraws, heals the crowds, and tells them not to make Him known. Matthew then quotes Isaiah 42:1-4 with his own formula: "This was to fulfill what was spoken through the prophet Isaiah" (12:17, BSB). This lesson does three kinds of checking. It lays the Isaiah quotation beside the Hebrew and the Greek Septuagint, because Matthew's wording follows each in places and neither in others. It reads Jesus' precedents where they stand. And because Sabbath disputes are a classic place where Christian preaching has turned "the Pharisees" into a picture of Judaism as cold legalism, it reads the Jewish Sabbath texts directly, with their dates, so the reader can see what Jewish teachers actually debated. Every BSB quotation below was printed from the app's own Bible files before drafting and checked again by script afterward.

## Context

**What happens, in order (12:1-21).** The disciples pick grain on the Sabbath and the Pharisees object (12:1-2). Jesus answers with David (12:3-4), the priests (12:5), the temple (12:6), Hosea (12:7) and the claim of 12:8. In "their synagogue" (12:9), they ask, "Is it lawful to heal on the Sabbath?" in order to accuse Him (12:10). Jesus answers with a sheep in a pit and heals a withered hand by a word (12:11-13). The Pharisees plot (12:14); Jesus withdraws, heals "them all," and warns them "not to make Him known" (12:15-16). The quotation follows (12:17-21).

**Placement.** The chapter opens "At that time," as the prayer of 11:25 does. So the invitation "Come to Me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest" (11:28) is followed at once by two disputes about the day of rest. That is a fact about Matthew's order. Whether Matthew meant "rest" and "Sabbath" to be heard together is a reader's inference that no source read here argues.

**What the charge was.** Picking a neighbor's grain by hand was allowed by the Torah: "you may pluck the heads of grain with your hand, but you must not put a sickle to your neighbor's grain" (Deuteronomy 23:25, BSB). The objection is about the day. Calvin's *Harmony* says so: "The Pharisees do not blame the disciples of Christ for plucking ears of corn from a field that was not their own, but for violating the Sabbath." The Mishnah, compiled around the end of the second century CE (per Wikipedia), lists thirty-nine primary categories of labor prohibited on Shabbat, beginning "One who sows, and one who plows, and one who reaps" (Mishnah Shabbat 7:2, William Davidson translation via Sefaria). The Mishnah is later than Jesus' ministry. It shows how later rabbinic law classified reaping. It cannot show what rule these Pharisees applied, and Matthew does not say.

**David and the consecrated bread (12:3-4).** Jesus asks, "Have you not read what David did when he and his companions were hungry?" (12:3, BSB). The episode is 1 Samuel 21:1-6, where David comes "to Nob, to Ahimelech the priest" (21:1) and "the priest gave him the consecrated bread, since there was no bread there but the Bread of the Presence" (21:6, BSB). Matthew's "only for the priests" (12:4) assumes Leviticus 24:9: "It belongs to Aaron and his sons" (BSB). Matthew names no priest. Mark does: "During the high priesthood of Abiathar" (Mark 2:26, BSB). In 1 Samuel, Abiathar is Ahimelech's son, who "escaped" and "fled to David" (1 Samuel 22:20, BSB). Commentators on Mark 2:26 (via Bible Hub) differ: Ellicott suggests the name of "the more famous priest of the two"; the Cambridge Bible reports that "Some therefore suppose that there is a clerical error here in the MSS"; the Expositor's Greek Testament allows "a natural error" or the Fathers' view that father and son "both bore both names." Matthew and Luke (6:3-4) name no priest, and this lesson does not choose among the readings.

**The priests in the temple (12:5).** "Or haven't you read in the Law that on the Sabbath the priests in the temple break the Sabbath and yet are innocent?" (12:5, BSB). No verse of the Law says the priests break the Sabbath. The Law gives them Sabbath duties. Ellicott, Barnes, Poole, Gill, Meyer and the Expositor's Greek Testament (via Bible Hub) point to Numbers 28:9-10: "On the Sabbath day, present two unblemished year-old male lambs" and "This is the burnt offering for every Sabbath" (BSB). Calvin says the word "is not strictly accurate, and is accommodated to his hearers," because "when the Law enjoins men to abstain from their employments, it does not forbid them to perform the services of religion." Only Matthew has this argument. The same kind of reasoning appears in later rabbinic debate. In the Mekhilta of Rabbi Yishmael, Rabbi Akiva argues: "If the saving of a life overrides the sacrificial service, which overrides the Sabbath, how much more so does the saving of a life override the Sabbath!" (Tractate Shabbata 1, Silverstein translation via Sefaria). Talmud Yoma 85a-b gives Akiva a version of the same argument, with the premise that "Temple service overrides Shabbat." Recent scholars date the Mekhilta's final compilation to the third century, though one placed it in the eighth (as Wikipedia summarizes them). These texts are later than Matthew. They show a shared way of reasoning from the temple, not that either side borrowed from the other.

**The "something greater than the temple" (12:6).** "But I tell you that something greater than the temple is here" (12:6, BSB). The Greek adjective is neuter, *meizon*, "a greater thing," in the Nestle 1904, Westcott-Hort, Byzantine Majority and Scrivener texts on Bible Hub. Only the Stephanus 1550 text has the masculine, behind the KJV's "one greater." The BSB's "something" follows the neuter. Vincent's Word Studies notes that "the neuter πλεῖον, more" is used at 12:41-42 as well, where the BSB nonetheless renders "One greater than Jonah" and "One greater than Solomon." Meyer reads 12:6 as Jesus pointing "to His own person and character as surpassing the temple in sanctity and greatness," and lists, then rejects, readings that take it as the messianic work, the disciples' interests, or the mercy of 12:7. Bengel adds: "He does not say, 'I am greater.'"

**Hosea 6:6, a second time (12:7).** "If only you had known the meaning of 'I desire mercy, not sacrifice,' you would not have condemned the innocent" (12:7, BSB). Matthew alone quotes this clause, here and at 9:13. This curriculum's Matthew 9:1-17 lesson checks the quotation in English, Greek and Hebrew, and its connection (conn-matt9-mercy-hosea6) names 12:7 as a recurrence, so that work is not repeated. One detail is new: "innocent" occurs twice in 12:1-21, for the priests (12:5) and for the disciples (12:7). In the BSB, the disciples stand where the priests stood.

**"Lord of the Sabbath" (12:8), and a saying Matthew does not have.** "For the Son of Man is Lord of the Sabbath" (12:8, BSB). All three Synoptic Gospels have the line (Mark 2:28 adds "even"). Only Mark puts before it "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27, BSB). In the Mekhilta, Rabbi Shimon ben Menassia reads Exodus 31:14 as "Sabbath is given to you and you are not given (i.e., 'surrendered') to the Sabbath" (Silverstein). Talmud Yoma 85b (Davidson) gives the same reading of the same verse to a different sage, Rabbi Yonatan ben Yosef: "Shabbat is given into your hands, and you are not given to it to die on account of Shabbat." In both it is one proof that saving a life overrides the Sabbath. Both collections were compiled well after Jesus' ministry (the Babylonian Talmud over the third to sixth centuries, per Wikipedia), and they disagree on who said it. The resemblance to Mark 2:27 is real; who influenced whom, if anyone, cannot be settled from these texts.

**The withered hand and the sheep (12:9-14).** "If one of you has a sheep and it falls into a pit on the Sabbath, will he not take hold of it and lift it out? How much more valuable is a man than a sheep! Therefore it is lawful to do good on the Sabbath" (12:11-12, BSB). Only Matthew has the sheep here; Luke has a similar question in another scene ("son or ox," Luke 14:5). Amy-Jill Levine names the form: "an argument from the lesser (the sheep in the pit) to the greater (the man in the synagogue), is called in Hebrew a qal V homer argument, and it is found frequently in rabbinic literature" (the PDF's rendering of *qal va-chomer*). Matthew argues the same way at 6:26: "Are you not much more valuable than they?" Levine also notes, "Nor does Jesus actually 'practice medicine,' for he does not touch the man." In 12:13 the healing comes by a command, "Stretch out your hand."

Jewish groups did not agree about the animal in the pit. A sectarian rule from the Dead Sea Scrolls community, the Damascus Document, forbids lifting it out: "No one should help an animal give birth on the Sabbath; and if it falls into a well or a pit, he may not lift it out on the Sabbath." It goes further for people: "Any living human who falls into a body of water or a cistern shall not be helped out with ladder, rope, or instrument" (CD 11:13-14, 16-17, trans. Edward Cook; editorial marks omitted). The later rabbinic texts take another line. In Talmud Shabbat 128b, for an animal fallen into an aqueduct, "one brings cushions and blankets... and places them beneath the animal," because "Causing a living creature to suffer is a Torah prohibition" (Davidson). For human life, "a case of uncertainty concerning a life-threatening situation overrides Shabbat" (Mishnah Yoma 8:6), and "one may desecrate Shabbat for a woman giving birth" (Mishnah Shabbat 18:3). On Bible Hub's 12:11 page, the Expositor's Greek Testament infers from the form of Jesus' question that "the theory and practice of contemporary Pharisees must have been milder than in the Talmudic period." No first-century Pharisaic ruling on the point was read for this lesson.

**The plot (12:14).** "But the Pharisees went out and plotted how they might kill Jesus" (12:14, BSB). In Matthew's BSB, "plotted" occurs only here, and the only earlier figure who seeks Jesus' death is Herod (2:13). The group is specific: "the Pharisees," named in 12:2 and 12:14 (the synagogue scene itself says only "they," 12:10). Mark adds "with the Herodians" (Mark 3:6). Luke says the scribes and Pharisees "were filled with rage and began to discuss with one another what they might do to Jesus" (Luke 6:11). When Matthew narrates the plot that leads to the arrest, the conspirators are "the chief priests and elders of the people" (26:3-4). In the very next verse here, "Large crowds followed Him" (12:15). Matthew 12 narrates one group's hostile response in one place. It does not set "the Jews" against Jesus.

**Isaiah 42:1-4 in Matthew, line by line (12:17-21).** In the BSB this quotation runs 76 words, the longest passage Matthew introduces as fulfilled Scripture (by a script over the shipped text; next is Isaiah 6:9-10 at 13:14-15, 57 words). Meyer calls it "a very free rendering of the original Hebrew text, yet not without some reminiscences of the LXX." Line by line, with the Greek checked in Swete's Septuagint and the Nestle 1904 text of Matthew (both via Bible Hub):

Where Matthew most plainly does not follow the Septuagint is its opening, which names the servant Jacob and Israel. That question returns in Positions.

**The Servant and the baptism.** Matthew's "My beloved, in whom My soul delights" (12:18) shares *agapētos* and a form of *eudokeō* with the voice at the baptism: "This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased!" (3:17, BSB). This curriculum's connection conn-matt3-baptism-isaiah42 already grades that link, so it is not duplicated. In Matthew's BSB, "beloved" occurs in three verses: 3:17, 12:18 and 17:5.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 12:1-21 is built, not on what the construction is meant to teach.

**Two disputes, then a quotation.** Each dispute has a Sabbath action, a question of what is "lawful," an answer from Scripture or common practice, and a closing ruling (12:8; 12:12). "Lawful" or "unlawful" occurs four times in 12:1-14: the Pharisees use it of the disciples (12:2), Jesus of David (12:4), the questioners in the synagogue (12:10), and Jesus at the end: "Therefore it is lawful to do good on the Sabbath" (12:12). Then the register changes: a plot (12:14), a withdrawal (12:15), a command to silence (12:16) and the long quotation.

**The quotation set against its setting.** The servant "will not quarrel or cry out," and no one "will hear His voice in the streets" (12:19). This comes right after a quarrel and a command "not to make Him known" (12:16). Commentators via Bible Hub tie the two together. Meyer says that "Matthew 12:17 refers to Matthew 12:16." Poole writes that "when the Pharisees took counsel against him, he made no opposition, but peaceably withdrew." Barnes reads the "streets" line as meaning "that he should not seek publicity and popularity." Matthew's placement invites these readings; the text does not spell out the match.

**"Nations" at both ends.** "Nations" occurs twice in 12:1-21, in the quotation's first and last lines (12:18, 12:21). The Pulpit Commentary says 12:21 "completes the parallelism with the end of the first stanza (ver. 18)." How this reach to the nations fits with 10:5 ("Do not go onto the road of the Gentiles") and 28:19 is a question for the whole Gospel.

## Positions

Two matters are taken up here: a real, named dispute, reported in its participants' own words and not resolved; and a misuse of this passage, named plainly.

**Who is the servant of Isaiah 42:1-4?** Matthew applies the passage to Jesus; that is a statement about Matthew's text. Who the servant is in Isaiah itself has been answered in several ways, within Judaism and within Christianity.

*Jewish readings.* The Septuagint, a Jewish translation into Greek, names the servant in the verse: "Jacob is my servant... Israel is my chosen" (Brenton). Rashi (11th century) reads "Behold My servant Jacob," and "Israel is called 'My chosen one,'" citing Isaiah 45:4 (Rosenberg translation via Sefaria). Abraham ibn Ezra (12th century) reports three views and adds his own: "Most of the commentators refer this expression to the pious Israelites; the Gaon to Cyrus; I to the prophet, who speaks here of himself, as in 49:6" (Friedlander translation, 1873). The Aramaic Targum Jonathan renders: "Behold, my servant, the Messiah, whom I bring, my chosen in whom one delights" (1871 English via Sefaria); Wikipedia dates it "probably" to the second century, "thoroughly revised" later. Radak (David Kimhi, 12th-13th century) also reads the Messiah. Sefaria serves his comment only in Hebrew, and this lesson's own translation of its opening is: "Behold My servant: this is the King Messiah, as we explained." He reads the servant's justice for the nations as peace "between nation and nation, so that there will be no more war between them," citing Isaiah 2:4. Nearby, Isaiah itself calls Israel God's servant: "But you, O Israel, My servant, Jacob, whom I have chosen" (41:8, BSB); "You are My Servant, Israel" (49:3, BSB).

*Christian readings.* John Calvin reads the passage of Christ: "to no other person could the following statements be applied, and the Evangelists place the matter beyond all controversy. (Matthew 12:17-21.)" He keeps its setting in the return from exile, calling that deliverance "only the foretaste of that redemption which they at length obtained through Christ." Keil and Delitzsch (via Bible Hub) hold that the servant here "is distinct from Israel, and has so strong an individuality and such marked personal features, that the expression cannot possibly be merely a personified collective." They keep a link to the nation through the image of a pyramid: "The base was Israel as a whole... the apex is the person of the Mediator of salvation springing out of Israel." The Catechism of the Catholic Church says that "The Messiah's characteristics are revealed above all in the 'Servant songs'" (§713), and calls Jesus' baptism "the acceptance and inauguration of his mission as God's suffering Servant" (§536).

*An ideal-Israel reading.* The Cambridge Bible for Schools and Colleges, a Christian commentary, heads Isaiah 42:1-4 "Israel as the Lord's Servant": "Israel according to its idea,—the Divine ideal after and towards which Jehovah is fashioning the people." It warns that "it is the vividness of the personification that leads many readers to think that an individual must be meant. But such impressions are not greatly to be trusted," and concludes, "Certainly no historic individual of that age can possibly be the subject of the picture." The Pulpit Commentary on Matthew 12:18 joins the two: "Primarily, as would appear, Israel in its ideal, up to which true Israelites came in measure, but only One came fully."

*Objections each reading faces.* Against the servant as Israel, Barnes argues that in Isaiah 42:6 the servant "is plainly distinguished from the people," citing "I will give thee for a covenant of (with) the people" (KJV-based wording; BSB: "appoint you to be a covenant for the people"). Against the ideal-Israel reading, Keil and Delitzsch's "cannot possibly be merely a personified collective" (above) is the reply. Against the prophet himself, Keil and Delitzsch argue that what is said "goes infinitely beyond anything to which a prophet was ever called," and Gill rejects Ibn Ezra (the prophet), Saadia Gaon (Cyrus) and Rashi (Israel) by name. Against the servant as the Messiah or any individual, the Cambridge Bible's warning about personification applies (above), and Barnes notes that the Septuagint's translators "evidently" read the servant as the Jewish people. This lesson does not decide among them. Amy-Jill Levine, writing of Matthew's use of Isaiah 7 at 1:22-23 rather than of Isaiah 42, offers one way to hold such disagreement: "Isaiah has multiple meanings: for his original audience, for the church, for the synagogue, for any who hold his words sacred." She adds: "Religious educators should explain how Jewish and Christian readings, although diverse, both point to Isaiah's meaning, fulfilled and unfulfilled."

**A misuse to name plainly: "the legalistic Pharisees."** A long line of Christian commentary has read Matthew 12:1-14 as Jesus' compassion set against a Jewish religion of rules without love. Commentaries read for this lesson show it. On 12:11 the Expositor's Greek Testament writes, "Humanity was what was lacking in the Pharisaic character." Calvin's comment on 12:1-2 attributes the objection chiefly to the Pharisees' malice, envy and hypocrisy, more than to religious scruple. Such readings turn one group's objection in one synagogue into a verdict on a people and its law. Levine names and corrects the move. Of the grainfields: "By arguing from both scriptural precedent (David) and current example (priests), Jesus shows his continuity with the Jewish tradition." Of both scenes: "Jews then, and now, just like Christians, continue to debate how to honor the Sabbath and keep it holy. The point is the sanctity of the day, not Mosaic Torah or Jewish Law vs. Jesus' compassion." The Catechism likewise describes Jesus as recalling, "often with rabbinical arguments, that the sabbath rest is not violated by serving God and neighbor" (§582), and says he "never fails to respect the holiness of this day" (§2173).

Four things can be checked. First, Matthew 12:1-21 names "the Pharisees" (12:2, 12:14), a particular group; it never says "the Jews," and in the same paragraph "Large crowds followed Him" (12:15). Second, Jesus' answers rest on Israel's own Scriptures (1 Samuel 21, Numbers 28, Hosea 6), and in this Gospel He says, "Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets" (5:17, BSB). Third, the Jewish texts above show a real debate, not one rigid rule: the Damascus Document forbids lifting an animal from a pit, the Talmud provides cushions for it, and the Mishnah lets doubt about danger to life override Shabbat. Each is addressed to its own community, as rulings for those who keep the Sabbath, not verdicts on anyone else, and the rabbinic ones are later than Matthew. Fourth, Michael Mulder (2026) describes the Sabbath in Jewish practice as "a day of gladness," welcomed "as a bride." A reader who leaves this passage picturing Judaism as joyless rule-keeping has taken that picture from commentators, not from Matthew 12.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage states in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. This is a model of the shape, not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** "How much more valuable is a man than a sheep! Therefore it is lawful to do good on the Sabbath" (Matthew 12:12, BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: Jesus answers a question about healing on the Sabbath by appealing to what His hearers would do for an animal, then reasoning from lesser to greater. The conclusion is a ruling about the day. Inference: for hearers who kept the Sabbath, this would have sounded like a claim about how the holy day is rightly kept, not permission to stop keeping it. This step goes beyond what the text states and is labeled as this lesson's own.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** A practice set apart for God is not broken by real good done for someone in need, and should not become a reason to withhold that good.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the lesser-to-greater reasoning and the ruling that doing good is "lawful" on the holy day. Not carried forward: any claim that the passage settles which day Christians keep, or whether a weekly Sabbath binds them (traditions differ), or that rest is unimportant; the text defends doing good on the day without setting the day aside.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a ruling about what is permitted, given in an argument ("Therefore it is lawful..."). It is not a command for every case, and not a license to abandon one's practice of rest. Promise scope: the passage contains no promise. It states what is lawful.

**Modern domain and situation.** Keeping a day of rest. A man keeps Sunday free of paid work, a practice he values. One Sunday his elderly neighbor calls, frightened: a pipe has burst and water is spreading across her kitchen. He wonders whether going to help counts as breaking his day of rest.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: an act of mercy within a practice of rest. One faithful response: he goes, shuts off the water, moves what he can and helps her call for repair. He does not treat the help as a failure of his practice, nor the afternoon as proof that his rest day does not matter. Next Sunday he keeps it as before.

**Cautions.** The ruling can be misused in both directions. It does not license filling every rest day with "good works" until rest disappears, and it does not license an employer or a church to demand that. If overwork or exhaustion is affecting your health, a doctor or counselor, not a study note, is the right next step. Nor should the passage be used to look down on people, Jewish or Christian, who keep a strict Sabbath; the Jewish texts read above themselves set Sabbath restrictions aside to save life and to help a mother in childbirth. This bridge is a worked example for the learner to test, not counsel for any particular person.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Jesus' three precedents work differently: David is need overriding a rule about holy bread; the priests are temple duty overriding Sabbath rest; Hosea is a principle. Which does the most work, and does "something greater than the temple" (12:6) depend on the priests' case in a way it does not depend on David's?
2. The Mekhilta and Yoma 85b both say, in different sages' names, that the Sabbath is "given to you," to argue that saving a life overrides it. Mark 2:27 has a similar saying; Matthew does not. What would you need to know about dates and transmission before saying how these relate?
3. Matthew follows the Septuagint in his last line ("In His name the nations") but not in its opening ("Jacob... Israel"). If Matthew knew that opening, what might explain leaving it out? Which explanations could be tested?
4. The servant "will not quarrel or cry out" (12:19), yet Jesus argues sharply in 12:1-14 and more sharply in chapter 23. Do these need reconciling, and if so, how?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 12:1-21 in order, saying what the text records before what any tradition concludes. (b) Name the line of the Isaiah quotation where Matthew follows the Septuagint against the Hebrew, and say what the Hebrew reads. (c) Name two things in Matthew's grainfield scene that Mark and Luke lack.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) the two scenes; (1:00-2:00) the three precedents and their sources (connections); (2:00-3:00) the Jewish Sabbath texts with dates and audiences, and the misuse named in Positions; (3:00-4:15) Isaiah 42:1-4 line by line and the readings of the servant; (4:15-5:00) the scope of 12:12, ending on one open question.
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own first. If you are stuck, one candidate, at full strength: Matthew himself says the Pharisees "plotted how they might kill Jesus," so a lesson that keeps defending them and quoting rabbinic texts softens Matthew's own judgment. Before answering, notice that the lesson reports 12:14 in full and limits it only to the group Matthew names. A second candidate, running the other way: Matthew's formula reads Isaiah 42 as fulfilled in Jesus, though the Septuagint's own wording names Jacob and Israel and Matthew changes several lines; this reads Jesus into a text about Israel. Before answering, compare how Calvin, Keil and Delitzsch, the Cambridge Bible and Levine handle the servant as Israel and as an individual (Positions).
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 12:1-14 does not say Jesus abolished the Sabbath; its rulings are about what is "lawful" on it (12:12), argued from the Law and the Prophets. (b) Matthew 12:14 does not say "the Jews" plotted; it names "the Pharisees," and the next verse has large crowds following Him. (c) Matthew 12:17-21 does not settle who the servant is in Isaiah's own setting; it states how Matthew applies the passage, and readers in both traditions answer the Isaiah question differently.

## Sources and Limits

- **Read directly, with raw copies saved:** every source in this lesson's source list, including Bible Hub's commentary pages (no page references to printed editions), Calvin (Calvin Translation Society English, not checked against a critical edition), the Sefaria texts, the Damascus Document page, and Levine's and Mulder's articles.
- **Radak's English is this lesson's own translation.** No published English of his comment was read. Meyer and Gill also report that Kimhi reads the servant as the Messiah, which agrees with the Hebrew as read here.
- **Secondhand or tertiary:** Saadia Gaon's reading (Cyrus) is known only through Ibn Ezra and Gill. Dates for the Mishnah, Mekhilta, Babylonian Talmud and Targum Jonathan come from Wikipedia (tertiary) and are approximate. The Damascus Document is quoted from one translation only.
- **Calvin's comment on 12:6-8 was not read.** The Bible Hub page breaks off inside his comment on 12:5.
- **Some commentators read here characterize the Pharisees in anti-Jewish terms** (the Expositor's Greek Testament on 12:11, Calvin on 12:1-2), and Ellicott on 12:19 contrasts Jesus with "the wrangling of Jewish scribes," while Poole, on Bible Hub's 12:21 page, reports a reading of "judgment unto victory" as "the final destruction of the Jews by the Romans." Neither remark is used elsewhere in this lesson. Where such commentators are quoted for a textual point, the quotation is limited to that point. Where they show the misuse named in Positions, they are cited as examples of it.
- **Not done:** modern critical commentaries on Matthew and on Isaiah 40-55 were not consulted, so the Servant discussion rests on older public-domain commentary, the Catechism, Levine and the Jewish commentators on Sefaria. No Eastern Orthodox source on these texts was located; that is a sourcing gap, not a claim that the tradition is silent. Which day Christians should keep, and whether the Sabbath command binds Christians, are not covered.

Both scripts and their outputs are kept with this lesson's working files.
