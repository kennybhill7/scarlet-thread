Matthew 5:1-12 opens the Sermon on the Mount with eight short units -- nine, depending how the last one is counted -- that all share one form: "Blessed are X, for Y." That repeated form is a real, checkable fact about the text, visible to anyone who lines the English up verse by verse. What scholars and traditions then do with that form -- whether the blessing names a status already true of the people described or a condition they must first meet, whether "poor in spirit" sharpens or softens Luke's plain "poor," whether the whole passage is ethics for every Christian or a kingdom-ethic addressed to disciples specifically -- is a different kind of claim, argued in print by named scholars who disagree with each other. This lesson keeps those two kinds of claim apart on purpose, the same discipline the Genesis 3 lesson in this curriculum uses for Genesis 3:15: textual observation in one place, named position in another, and the reader's own conclusion left as the reader's own.

## Context

**Setting (5:1-2).** "When Jesus saw the crowds, He went up on the mountain and sat down. His disciples came to Him, and He began to teach them, saying:" (BSB). Jesus speaks seated -- a teaching posture in the ancient world, not a casual one -- to disciples who have "come to Him," though a wider crowd is present too: David Turner (Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society, 2010) notes that the Sermon "historically was intended primarily for his immediate audience of disciples, although the crowd of uncommitted onlookers also heard it (Matt 5:1-2)." Matthew does not name the mountain, and this lesson does not supply a name for it.

**The *ashre* form and its Old Testament background.** Each unit in 5:3-11 opens with "Blessed are..." -- in Greek *makarioi*, the standard rendering of the Hebrew *ashre*, a word K.C. Hanson (The Context Group, 1990) describes as a "formulaic expression: *ashre* followed by a nominal construction," a value judgment pronouncing someone's situation or conduct honorable rather than a command. Ian Paul (Psephizo) traces the form's "long pedigree (in the Hebrew term *ashre*) in the psalms (Ps 1.1, 32.1-2, 40.4, 119.1-2, 128.1)." Psalm 1:1 (BSB) is the best-known instance: "Blessed is the man who does not walk in the counsel of the wicked, or set foot on the path of sinners, or sit in the seat of mockers" -- a judgment on a way of life, not a command to adopt one. Daniel Lioy (Conspectus, 2016) traces further linguistic and conceptual connections specifically between Psalm 1 and Matthew's Beatitudes. The form belongs to wisdom-literature vocabulary before it is Gospel vocabulary: Psalm 1 opens the Psalter with exactly this word.

**A textual pattern across the unit (5:3-10).** Verse 3 and verse 10 end with the identical clause -- "...for theirs is the kingdom of heaven" (BSB, word for word in both verses), both present tense ("is"). The six units between them (5:4-9) all state their reason in the future tense: "they will be comforted... they will inherit... they will be filled... they will be shown mercy... they will see God... they will be called sons of God" (BSB). Turner calls this an inclusio: "the eight beatitudes are bracketed by two (5:3, 10) which have an identical causal clause which affirms the present possession of the blessings of the kingdom," while "the middle six beatitudes (Matt 5:4-9) uniformly use the future tense... to speak of the eschatological blessings which will come to disciples." Hanson, working independently from a social-science angle, reports the same pattern: verses 3 and 10 "form an inclusio," with the verses between them in future tense "emphasizing participation in the 'kingdom.'" Two scholars working from different methods, checking the same verses, reporting the same pattern, is as close to a settled textual fact as this lesson gets.

**A shift at verse 11.** Verses 3-10 describe people in the third person ("Blessed are those who..."); verse 11 switches to direct second-person address -- "Blessed are you when people insult you, persecute you, and falsely say all kinds of evil against you because of Me" (BSB) -- continuing through verse 12. This is commonly read as an expansion of the eighth beatitude's persecution theme (5:10), now addressed directly to the disciples in front of Jesus rather than stated as a general class. The grammatical shift itself is checkable; what is then concluded about why Matthew makes it belongs in Literary Design and Positions, not here.

**A pastoral-safety note on verses 10-12.** These verses bless being persecuted "because of Me" -- persecution for righteousness' sake, tied specifically to following Jesus. This lesson does not extend that to a general principle that any mistreatment should be welcomed, or that a person suffering abuse should be told to "rejoice." If you are in a relationship where suffering is being used against you to demand silence or compliance, a qualified person, not a study note, is the right next step.

**Matthew and Luke compared, as a textual fact.** Luke 6:20-26 (BSB) records a related set of sayings in a different setting -- "Looking up at His disciples, Jesus said..." on "a level place" (Luke 6:17), not a mountain: "Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God. Blessed are you who hunger now, for you will be filled. Blessed are you who weep now, for you will laugh... Blessed are you when people hate you..." -- followed by four "woes" with no Matthean counterpart ("But woe to you who are rich... Woe to you who are well fed now... Woe to you when all men speak well of you..."). The reward clause at the end is close to verbatim between the two Gospels: "great is your reward in heaven; for in the same way they persecuted the prophets before you" (Matt 5:12, BSB) against "great is your reward in heaven. For their fathers treated the prophets in the same way" (Luke 6:23, BSB). Luke addresses his four beatitudes in direct second person throughout; Matthew's first eight are third person. Luke has "poor" without qualification; Matthew has "poor in spirit." These are checkable differences in the English text; what produced them is a named, disputed question, covered in Positions below.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 5:1-12 is built, not on what that construction is meant to teach.

**The repeated formal unit.** Hanson analyzes each of the eight makarisms in 5:3-10 as a two-part unit: a "makarism proper" (the value judgment *makarioi* plus the subject named) followed by a "grant of honor" (the conjunction *hoti*, "for," plus a present- or future-tense clause). Every one of the eight units in 5:3-10 follows this same two-part shape -- a reader can verify this by checking that every line begins "Blessed are the/those who..." and pivots on "for" exactly once. This repeated form is what makes the inclusio noted in Context (identical v.3/v.10 clause, future tense in between) visible in the first place, not a separate discovery.

**A proposed two-part grouping.** Ian Paul (Psephizo) groups the first four beatitudes (5:3-6) as addressing "situation" -- poverty, mourning, meekness, hunger -- and the second four (5:7-10) as addressing "character" -- mercy, purity, peacemaking, persecution -- attributing a similar observation to David Wenham, who reads the first group as "people under pressure and hungry for God's righteousness" and the second as "people whose lives already demonstrate the kingdom." Paul further reports a count of thirty-six Greek words in each half, with the first four lines' subjects beginning with the Greek letter pi; that specific word-count and alliteration claim was not independently checked against the Greek text in this research pass and is reported here only as Paul's own claim.

**A caution about imposing more structure than the text secures.** Several popular outlines of the Beatitudes present a formal chiasm (a mirrored A-B-C...C'-B'-A' pattern) across the eight units. This lesson did not locate, during its research, a chiastic outline for Matthew 5:3-10 specifically that is both attributed to a named scholar and independently checkable against that scholar's own published text -- the same practical problem the Genesis 3 lesson's own Literary Design section raises about chiasm proposals generally: a symmetrical-looking pattern can be a feature an author built, or a label an interpreter supplied. No specific chiastic outline is presented here as a result. The two-part "situation / character" grouping above and the inclusio in Context are reported instead, because both rest on something checkable in the English text (repeated vocabulary, tense, person) rather than on a proposed lettered correspondence this lesson could not verify.

**The ninth unit as expansion, not a structurally equal ninth member.** Several sources behind this lesson's research (not independently verified here against one specific named scholar's own text) count 5:11-12 as an expansion of the eighth beatitude's persecution theme rather than a ninth unit of equal weight, on the textual grounds already noted in Context: the shift from third person to second person, and the lengthening of the unit itself (two verses instead of one line).

## Positions

Three separate, named disputes attach to this passage. Each is reported here in terms its own participants use, not resolved.

**Are the Beatitudes conditions to be met, or declarations about people already described?** Robert Guelich's 1976 *Journal of Biblical Literature* article frames the question in its own title -- "The Matthean Beatitudes: 'Entrance-Requirements' or Eschatological Blessings?" -- and is reported by secondary sources as arguing for the second option: the Beatitudes declare a blessing already true of the people described, not a ladder of achievements a person climbs to earn entry. (Guelich's article itself returned an access error at every host tried during this lesson's research; this characterization is secondhand and is flagged as such in Sources and Limits.) A reading closer to the conditions side is on record from Hans Windisch, whose *Sermon on the Mount* study argues "the Sermon intends to proclaim commands. It presents demands that are to be literally understood and literally fulfilled" (as reported by Bert Friesen, *Direction*, 1981) -- for Windisch, the Sermon as a whole, Beatitudes included, states what a person must do to be saved. David Turner's own, more recent position (2010) sits closer to Guelich's side: "the beatitudes as eschatological blessings present the core values of a disciple whose present experience of the kingdom (5:3, 10) is supported by the promises of its future fullness (5:4-9)" -- a status disciples already hold, pointed toward a future not yet complete, rather than a bar they clear.

**Does "poor in spirit" (Matt 5:3) sharpen or soften Luke's "poor" (Luke 6:20)?** This is a live, named dispute, not a settled one. One line of explanation reads Matthew's "in spirit" as drawing out a sense already present in the Hebrew concept of the *anawim* -- the humble, afflicted poor who depend on God -- so that Matthew makes explicit what was already implicit rather than changing Jesus's meaning; Wikipedia's article on Matthew 5:3 reports this reading as associated with W.D. Davies and Dale C. Allison Jr., Ulrich Luz, R.T. France, and Gary T. Meadors (this lesson has not independently checked each of these commentators against their own text, and flags the attribution as secondhand, via a tertiary source). A second line treats Luke's plain "poor" as closer to an originally, economically literal saying, with Matthew's "in spirit" read as a theological gloss added for Matthew's own audience. Separately, Alfred Plummer's 1896 International Critical Commentary on Luke proposes that "some of our Lord's words were uttered several times" -- Jesus may simply have preached a version of this material more than once, with Matthew and Luke each recording a different telling, rather than one evangelist editing the other's (or a shared source's) wording (reported by Ian Paul, Psephizo, who also cites R.T. France's NICNT commentary making a similar suggestion). Mark Goodacre's *The Case Against Q*, also reported by Paul, argues instead that Luke's distinctive wording (including the four woes) reflects Luke's own editorial concern for the poor visible elsewhere in his Gospel, rather than a separate occasion or an earlier shared source.

**Is this passage an ethic for every Christian, or a kingdom-ethic addressed to disciples specifically?** Bert Friesen's 1981 survey in *Direction* names five historical approaches to the Sermon on the Mount's application, and the Beatitudes sit inside all five. The "liberal" approach (Hans Windisch, Adolf von Harnack) reads the Sermon as literal commands for every Christian to obey now. The classical dispensational approach (represented by C.I. Scofield) reads the Sermon as law "in its primary application" for a future, earthly kingdom, not as "the privilege [or] the duty of the Church" now, though Scofield allows it "a beautiful moral application to the Christian" short of binding law. The "interim ethics" approach (Johannes Weiss, Albert Schweitzer) reads the demands as meant for the brief interval before an imminent end that did not come as expected -- "irrelevant today," in Carl F.H. Henry's summary of the position. The "existential" approach (Martin Dibelius, Gerhard Kittel) reads the demands as deliberately "impossible to follow," a "standard by which to solve problems" rather than a literal code. The Anabaptist/Mennonite approach (Menno Simons, Hans Denck, John Howard Yoder) reads the Sermon, Beatitudes included, as the kingdom community's own ethic, binding on disciples now, with Denck's own formula: "to know Christ truly is to follow him daily in life." Writing from inside progressive dispensationalism specifically, David Turner (2010) argues against classical dispensationalism's reading on this point: the Sermon's "gracious purpose" holds "during the days of the historical Jesus, during the days of Matthew's literary activity, and during all the days of the church until the end of the age," naming Scofield's view "a serious mistake" for treating the Sermon as "only indirectly relevant to Christians today." This lesson did not locate, in its research, a comparably specific named statement from historic Reformed or Lutheran covenant theology on this exact question; that gap is noted in Sources and Limits rather than filled with an unverified claim.

Where these three disputes agree: every position above treats the passage as addressed to real people with real stakes, not as decoration. Where they differ, they differ on terms their own authors state for themselves, not on a point this lesson adjudicates.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage states in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. This is a model of the shape, not an answer key -- the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 5:9: "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called sons of God" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the word is "peacemakers," not "peace-lovers" or "peace-keepers" -- the form names people who act to produce peace, not people who merely prefer it or avoid conflict. Inference: for Jesus's first hearers, people under Roman occupation with real reasons for factional anger, naming active peacemaking (rather than passive peacefulness) as a mark of belonging to God's family would have been a pointed, costly claim, not a general nicety. The Context section above does not supply direct evidence of first-century Galilean attitudes toward peacemaking beyond this observation; this inference goes a step further than Context's cited facts, and is labeled as this lesson's own step, not a cited finding.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Producing peace between people who are at odds, at some real cost to the peacemaker, is treated here as a mark of likeness to God -- distinct from simply avoiding conflict or staying neutral.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the distinction between actively working to reconcile people and merely not adding to a conflict. Not carried forward: anything specific to first-century Galilee, Roman occupation, or "sons of God" as a culturally specific honor claim. The bridge holds only where a real, costly act of reconciliation is actually available to the person in the modern case -- not every disagreement calls for a peacemaker to step in.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader by name. Promise scope: none claimed; "they will be called sons of God" is not treated here as a promise this lesson can hand to a reader who performs a specific act.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family. Two adult siblings have not spoken since a dispute over a parent's care three years ago; a third sibling, who was not party to the original dispute, is deciding whether to step in before the next holiday gathering. A second, non-family domain where the same principle could be tested: two coworkers on a team project who have stopped communicating, with a deadline forcing the question of whether a third teammate should intervene.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: active reconciliation (bringing two parties back together), not passive peacefulness (staying out of it) and not conflict avoidance (changing the subject when both are in the room). One faithful response: the third sibling initiates a direct, individual conversation with each of the other two, separately, naming the specific issue rather than a vague call to "get along," before the holiday forces an unplanned confrontation.

**Cautions.** This does not mean every bystander to every disagreement should intervene, or that reconciliation is the third party's to force on two people who are not ready. It does not fit a situation of real danger -- intervening in a conflict that involves domestic violence, threats, or an unsafe power imbalance is not what this bridge has in view, and a qualified person, not a family member attempting mediation, is the right next step in that situation. The passage is not a conflict-resolution manual; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing, and the learner may find it does not hold in their own case.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Matthew 5:3 and 5:10 both end "for theirs is the kingdom of heaven," present tense, while 5:4-9 all use the future tense. If that bracket is deliberate, what would it change about how you read the six beatitudes in between -- and what evidence, short of asking Matthew himself, would tell you whether it is deliberate or incidental?
2. Luke 6:20-26 and Matthew 5:3-12 share a reward clause almost word for word but differ sharply elsewhere (poor vs. poor in spirit; no woes vs. four woes). What would you need to see in the Greek text itself, rather than in a commentator's summary, to judge between "one occasion, two editors" and "two occasions"?
3. If verses 11-12 really are an expansion of verse 10 rather than a separate, equal ninth beatitude, does that change anything about how the earlier eight should be read, or is it only a point about how to count?
4. Friesen's five historical approaches to the Sermon's application do not include a position this lesson could independently source from inside Reformed or Lutheran covenant theology specifically. If you located one, what would you need it to say to settle whether it agrees more with Turner's "all the days of the church" reading or with Scofield's "not... the duty of the Church" reading?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) state in order what happens in 5:1-2 (who does what) before saying anything about what the Beatitudes mean. (b) Recite the repeated two-part form every unit in 5:3-10 follows (a blessing, then a reason introduced by "for"), and state which two verses share an identical reason clause. (c) Name one place elsewhere in Scripture where this passage's language or form visibly reappears, and say what kind of connection it is: a close parallel, a shared Old Testament background, an echo, or something else.

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the setting and the repeated *ashre*/"blessed" form, with its wisdom-literature background (see Context); (1:00-2:00) the inclusio at vv.3/10 and the shift to second person at v.11, stated as textual observations, plus the caution about unverified chiastic outlines (see Literary Design); (2:00-3:30) the Matthew/Luke comparison, read side by side, with the "poor" vs. "poor in spirit" dispute named but not resolved (see Context and Positions); (3:30-4:30) where this passage's language travels elsewhere in Scripture, naming each connection's evidence label, not just listing it (see prompt 5); (4:30-5:00) one open question from above, stated on purpose as the last thing taught.

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first; the point of this prompt is that you do the forming, not that you pick from a list. If you are stuck, here is one worked candidate, stated at full strength: Matthew's "poor in spirit" is a theological softening of an originally literal, economic promise to the poor -- Luke's wording is closer to what Jesus actually said, and Matthew (or his source) spiritualized a hard saying about material poverty into something more comfortable for a less poor audience. Before answering it, state what the *anawim*-background reading (Davies and Allison, Luz, France, Meadors, as reported by Wikipedia's Matthew 5:3 article) actually claims: that "poor" in the relevant Hebrew tradition was never purely economic to begin with, so Matthew's "in spirit" may be drawing out a sense already carried by the word, not adding a new one. A second worked candidate, running the other direction: the entrance-requirement reading (Windisch) has its own problem to answer -- if the Beatitudes are commands to be literally fulfilled in order to receive the kingdom, what explains the shift to second person at v.11, which blesses disciples for something done *to* them (being persecuted), not something they achieve?

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 5:3-12 does not, in its own wording, say whether the blessing it describes is a status already true of the people named or a condition they must first meet -- the dispute in Positions is about what the passage's declarations are doing, not about which words are on the page. This is a claim about what the text states on its face, not a claim about which reading of it is right. (b) The passage does not name a specific mountain; "the mountain" (5:1) is Matthew's own phrase, and any specific identification of it is a later tradition, not this verse's own statement.

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection from this passage and defend it: name its type, its evidence label, and point to the words in both texts that support that grade. The four connections attached to this lesson sit at different tiers, which is the point of the exercise:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Luke 6:20-26 | parallel | explicit | Same speaker, same macarism form, a near-verbatim reward clause (5:12/6:23); the wording differences are real and reported, not hidden |
| Isaiah 61:1-3 | allusion | strong | Shared vocabulary and order ("poor," "mourn"/"comfort") with no quotation formula in Matthew; strengthened by Jesus's own later, explicit citation of Isaiah 61 at Luke 4:18-21 |
| Psalm 37:11 | allusion | strong | Near-identical promise ("meek... inherit the land/earth") with no formula introducing it |
| James 2:5 | allusion | plausible | Shared vocabulary ("poor," "kingdom") observed by this lesson directly; the named source read here (McKnight) documents beatitude echoes in James generally but was not found, in the material read here, to argue this specific verse pairing |

Choose one row and defend it in your own words, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` instead: name a candidate you were drawn to and say what evidence is missing.
