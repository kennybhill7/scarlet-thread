Matthew 2:1-12 ended with the Magi going home "by another route" (2:12) -- warned, but not yet out of danger, because the Magi were never the one Herod actually wanted to find. Matthew 2:13-23 is what happens to the Child himself once Herod's attention turns from the visitors to the family. In eleven verses the narrative covers a flight, a massacre, a death, a return, and a second diversion -- and inside that short span sits three of Matthew's own formula-citations (2:15, 2:17-18, 2:23), the last of which names no identifiable single source at all. This lesson keeps three kinds of claim in three different places, the same discipline the genealogy and Magi lessons in this unit already practice. Some things here are directly checkable textual facts: what Matthew's own fulfillment formula says at each of the three citations, and exactly how closely each quotation tracks its source as both are shipped in this app's own BSB text. Some are cited historical facts: Herod's and his son Archelaus's own documented record of violence. And some are named, live scholarly disputes this lesson reports rather than resolves: whether applying Hosea 11:1 to Jesus is legitimate exegesis or a later reach past the verse's own setting; what, if anything, "he will be called a Nazarene" is actually quoting; and whether the massacre of Bethlehem's children is historical at all, given that no source outside Matthew mentions it.

## Context

**What happens, in order (2:13-23).** After the Magi depart, "an angel of the Lord appeared to Joseph in a dream," telling him to get up, take the Child and Mary, and flee to Egypt, because "Herod is going to search for the Child to kill Him" (2:13, BSB). Joseph obeys that night (2:14) and stays in Egypt "until the death of Herod. This fulfilled what the Lord had spoken through the prophet: 'Out of Egypt I called My Son'" (2:15). Herod, "outwitted by the Magi," orders the death of "all the boys in Bethlehem and its vicinity who were two years old and under, according to the time he had learned from the Magi" (2:16). Matthew introduces a second citation -- "what was spoken through the prophet Jeremiah was fulfilled" -- and quotes: "A voice is heard in Ramah, weeping and great mourning, Rachel weeping for her children, and refusing to be comforted, because they are no more" (2:17-18). After Herod dies, the angel appears again, this time "in a dream to Joseph in Egypt," telling him to return, "for those seeking the Child’s life are now dead" (2:19-20); Joseph obeys (2:21). Learning that Herod's son Archelaus now reigns in Judea, Joseph is afraid and, "having been warned in a dream," withdraws instead to "the district of Galilee" and settles in Nazareth (2:22). Matthew closes with a third citation, introduced differently from the other two -- "So was fulfilled what was spoken through the prophets: 'He will be called a Nazarene'" (2:23) -- a quotation that names no single traceable source.

**Where this passage sits.** This is the fourth unit of the pilot curriculum's Matthew sequence, following the Magi (2:1-12, stage 6) and continuing directly from where that lesson's own account stopped -- a structural position in how this app organizes its material, not a claim about composition order or source history.

**Three fulfillment-citation formulas, three different relationships to their source.** Matthew's own words introduce all three citations as fulfillment, but what each one does with its source differs, and that difference is directly checkable without appeal to any secondary source. At 2:15, Matthew quotes only the second half of Hosea 11:1. Hosea's own sentence (28.11.1, BSB) reads in full: "When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called My son." Matthew quotes: "Out of Egypt I called My Son" -- leaving out the opening clause entirely. (The BSB also capitalizes the two occurrences differently: "My Son" in Matthew's quotation, referring to Jesus; "My son" in Hosea's own sentence, referring to Israel -- a translator's formatting choice reflecting each context's own referent, not a difference in the underlying Hebrew or Greek wording Matthew is quoting.) At 2:17-18, Matthew quotes Jeremiah 31:15 almost in full, with one small, checkable difference: the BSB's Jeremiah reads "mourning and great weeping"; the BSB's Matthew reads "weeping and great mourning" -- the same two nouns, reversed. At 2:23, there is no verse to compare at all: "he will be called a Nazarene" does not match any single verse in the Old Testament as it has survived, in Hebrew or in the Septuagint's Greek. Each of these three observations is checkable directly against the two texts; what explains the differences, and what (if anything) 2:23 is actually citing, is exactly the ground the Positions section below reports as disputed.

**Herod's and Archelaus's own documented record, as a cited historical fact separate from whether this specific massacre happened.** Josephus's *Antiquities of the Jews* -- not independently read for this lesson, reported here through secondary sources -- records that Herod had his wife Mariamne I executed (29 BC) on suspicion of adultery, had his brother-in-law Aristobulus III drowned (35 BC), and had two more sons, Alexander and Aristobulus IV, tried and executed on charges of treason (7 BC), followed by a third son, Antipater, shortly before Herod's own death. Modern historians reported here (Seth Schwartz; Jodi Magness) describe Herod as a ruler "loathed equally by Jewish and Christian posterity" whose own family he was, by Josephus's own account, "prepared to commit any crime" against to protect his throne. Separately, Josephus records that Herod's son Archelaus, early in his own reign and before Augustus had even confirmed his title, responded to a crowd protesting at the Temple during Passover by sending in the army; the resulting deaths are reported at roughly 3,000, after which Archelaus canceled the feast. This is why 2:22 states that Joseph "was afraid" of Judea under Archelaus specifically, rather than regarding Herod's death as the end of the danger. None of this, on its own, establishes that the massacre at 2:16 happened -- that is a separate, disputed question taken up in Positions below -- but Herod's and Archelaus's own willingness to kill, including their own family members and their own subjects, is cited here as a documented fact about the two rulers, not as evidence for or against this specific episode.

**The geography of the flight and the return.** "Egypt" in this period meant, in practical terms, the Nile Valley rather than merely the nearer border; R.T. France (cited secondhand, "The Formula Quotations of Matthew 2," *New Testament Studies* 27 (1981): 79) estimates the distance from Bethlehem to the Nile Valley at roughly 150 miles, a journey of at least a week. Egypt was also, as Craig S. Keener is reported noting, a logical destination precisely because it lay outside Herod's own jurisdiction while still holding a large, established Jewish community -- Alexandria alone is reported to have been roughly a third Jewish at this period. The return at 2:19-21 retraces the same route; the detour at 2:22 is a second, distinct decision, made only after Joseph "learned that Archelaus was reigning in Judea in place of his father Herod" -- a specific, named political fact the text itself gives as Joseph's reason for avoiding Judea and settling instead in "the district of Galilee," in a town the passage names only at its very end: "a town called Nazareth" (2:23).

## Literary Design

**Completing the four-times-repeated dream pattern this unit's predecessor already named.** The lesson on Matthew 1:18-25 documented an identical pairing -- "an angel of the Lord," appearing to Joseph specifically, "in a dream" -- recurring at 1:20, 2:13, 2:19, and 2:22, and noted that Joseph never speaks a single recorded word anywhere in Matthew's Gospel. This passage contains three of those four occurrences. The first two repeat the full formula almost verbatim: "an angel of the Lord appeared to Joseph in a dream" (2:13); "an angel of the Lord appeared in a dream to Joseph in Egypt" (2:19). The fourth occurrence varies the formula: 2:22 states only that Joseph, "having been warned in a dream," withdrew to Galilee -- dropping "an angel of the Lord" entirely. This is a directly checkable textual fact about how the pattern is built across its four instances, not, on its own, a claim about why Matthew varied the fourth one.

**A geographic and narrative shape that tracks Moses's own story, without Matthew's own words naming it.** A ruler threatened by a coming deliverer orders the deaths of a people's infant sons (Exodus 1:15-22; Matthew 2:16); the deliverer's own family flees danger and is later told it is safe to return because those who sought his life are dead -- "all the men who sought to kill you are dead" at Exodus 4:19, "those seeking the Child’s life are now dead" at Matthew 2:20, wording close enough to check directly (see the connection to Exodus 4:19-20 attached to this lesson). Nowhere in 2:13-23 does Matthew cite Exodus by name or formula the way he cites Hosea, Jeremiah, and "the prophets." Whether this double resemblance (the decree, and the return) is Matthew's own deliberate shaping of the narrative or simply how a family's exile-and-return story tends to echo Israel's best-known exile-and-return story is not something this lesson decides; what is checkable is that both resemblances are in the text, and that neither is marked by Matthew's own citation formula the way the three fulfillment quotations are.

**The three fulfillment citations, compared to each other and to the one in the previous unit.** The Magi lesson already noted that Matthew's own fulfillment-formula voice (as opposed to a citation placed in a character's reported speech, as at 2:5-6) appears three times in chapter 2: at 2:15, 2:17, and 2:23. All three of those occurrences sit inside this passage. Lined up together, the three citations show a narrowing: 2:15 names "the prophet" and quotes a real, locatable verse (Hosea 11:1), though only half of it; 2:17 names "the prophet Jeremiah" specifically and quotes a real, locatable verse (Jeremiah 31:15) almost in full; 2:23 names only "the prophets," plural, and quotes a sentence that does not match any single verse that has survived. A reader can check this narrowing directly by reading the three introductions side by side; what it means that the citation becomes less locatable each time, if it means anything at all, is not something this lesson resolves.

## Positions

Three separate, named questions get real scholarly attention in this passage, and this lesson keeps them in three separate groups rather than blending them.

**Is applying Hosea 11:1 to Jesus's infancy legitimate reading, or a reach past the verse's own setting?**

In Hosea's own eighth-century-BC sentence, "out of Egypt I called My son" looks backward, at the nation of Israel's own exodus under Moses, not forward to an individual's birth centuries later -- a fact about Hosea 11's own grammar and context that is not itself disputed by any source consulted for this lesson. What is disputed is what Matthew is doing by applying it to Jesus anyway.

Gregory K. Beale ("The Use of Hosea 11:1 in Matthew 2:15: One More Time," *Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society* 55.4 (2012): 697-715, reported here only through a search-engine-generated summary of the article, not the article's own text) argues the typology is already present inside Hosea 11 itself when the whole chapter is read together: Hosea's own later verses (11:10-11) anticipate a future, second exodus, and Matthew reads Jesus as the one who "recapitulates the history of Israel," succeeding where the nation failed. On Beale's reading, Matthew is not importing a meaning onto Hosea that the chapter does not already carry.

S.V. McCasland ("Matthew Twists the Scriptures," *Journal of Biblical Literature* 80 (1961): 143-148, reported here only secondhand, through two pages that summarize and quote the article rather than through the original or its 1994 reprint) takes the opposite position: that Matthew "twists the Scripture," finding in Hosea 11:1 "a meaning entirely foreign to the original," and that episodes like this one show "how desperately early Christians searched the Scriptures to find proof for the things happening among them."

A third position, reported across several of this lesson's sources without being tied to one named advocate in the sources actually consulted, holds that Matthew's move is a real, later Christian interpretive act -- a "fuller sense" read back into the text from the New Testament side -- rather than either a meaning Hosea's own words already carried (Beale's claim) or a plain misreading (McCasland's claim). This lesson reports all three positions without ranking them; the first problem Beale himself identifies with his own view, by his own framing (reported here secondhand), is that it requires reading a single verse against the full shape of an entire prophetic chapter rather than against that one verse alone -- a method not every reader will accept.

**What, if anything, is Matthew 2:23 quoting?**

This is the clearest case in the whole passage of a citation this lesson cannot resolve, because no source consulted resolves it either. "He will be called a Nazarene" (2:23) matches no surviving Hebrew or Greek verse. Several named proposals exist.

Jerome, reported secondhand, is credited with the oldest and most commonly repeated proposal: a wordplay on the Hebrew *netzer* ("branch" or "shoot"), as used messianically at Isaiah 11:1 ("a shoot will spring up from the stump of Jesse... a Branch will bear fruit from his roots"). The consonants of "Nazareth" and *netzer* share a middle sound in Hebrew; Craig S. Keener is reported noting that *netzer* carries messianic associations in the Dead Sea Scrolls as well. R.T. France, reported secondhand, raises a real problem for this proposal: the wordplay works only in Hebrew, and Matthew is writing in Greek, where the phonetic echo Jerome relies on does not carry over.

A second proposal reads "Nazarene" as pointing to Nazareth's own low status rather than to any specific word. Albert Barnes, reported secondhand, reads the title as having become "an insulting epithet," so that Matthew is invoking a general, widely attested Old Testament expectation that the Messiah would come from humble, even despised, origins (sometimes tied to Isaiah 53:2-3's "despised and rejected" figure) rather than quoting one specific verse about the word "Nazarene" itself. Michael Goulder, reported secondhand, is credited with a related view: that Matthew deliberately reached for the closest available wordplay to justify, after the fact, why the Messiah came from this particular town.

A third proposal, tied to the word's surface resemblance to "Nazirite" (Hebrew *nazir*, as in Judges 13:5's prophecy over Samson), is reported by more than one source as grammatically weak: Jesus is never described as a Nazirite (a person under a specific vow), and the Hebrew forms behind "Nazirite" and "Nazareth" do not match as closely as the *netzer* proposal's do.

Jared M. August ("'He Shall Be Called a Nazarene': The Non-Citation of Matthew 2:23," *Tyndale Bulletin* 69.1 (2018): 63-74, reported here only through a search-engine-generated summary; a direct PDF fetch of the article returned corrupted, unreadable text and was abandoned) proposes a different kind of answer: comparing all fourteen of Matthew's formula-citations, August argues that ten of them quote an identifiable Old Testament passage, while four (including 2:23, alongside 5:17 and 26:54, 56) instead develop a general Old Testament theme or expectation rather than citing one verse at all. On this reading, the search for a single missing verse behind 2:23 may be asking the text a question it was never trying to answer -- Matthew's "the prophets," plural, may itself be the clue that no single citation is intended.

This lesson reports all of these without selecting among them. What is not disputed, and bears repeating because it is easy to read past: no source consulted for this lesson, including the ones defending each proposal, claims to have located a surviving verse that says, in so many words, "he will be called a Nazarene."

**Is the massacre of Bethlehem's children (2:16-18) historical?**

This is argued in print by named scholars working from different starting assumptions, and this lesson reports two positions without adjudicating between them -- including the specific caution the task behind this lesson was given directly: Herod's own documented brutality (see Context, above) does not, by itself, establish that this particular episode happened, and Josephus's silence does not, by itself, establish that it did not.

The majority modern-scholarly position, reported here through more than one secondary source, holds the massacre non-historical. The central argument from this position is an argument from silence, stated carefully: Josephus, Herod's own near-contemporary and the principal source for his reign, records Herod's other documented acts of violence in detail (see Context) but never mentions a massacre at Bethlehem; no other contemporary writer, Jewish or Roman -- Philo, Tacitus, Suetonius, or Nicolaus of Damascus, who had been Herod's own friend and wrote a now-lost biography of him -- mentions it either. Andrew Lincoln (*Born of a Virgin?*, 2013, p. 44, reported secondhand) states that Matthew's episode "appears" to have been modeled on the Exodus narrative of Pharaoh's decree against Hebrew infant boys (see the connection to Exodus 1:15-22 attached to this lesson), "as told in an expanded version that was current in the 1st century." Jodi Magness, reported secondhand, states that on this view the episode "was inspired by Herod's reputation" rather than reported from a specific, remembered event.

A minority position, held by named scholars arguing for plausibility rather than certainty, holds that Josephus's silence is explainable without concluding the event did not happen. R.T. France, reported secondhand, argues that Bethlehem's own small population (France's own estimate, reported here, runs to roughly 1,000 residents) means the number of boys actually killed, if the episode is historical, would likely have been small -- France's own figure, reported here, is six to twenty children -- small enough, on this argument, that a Greco-Roman historian writing for a Greco-Roman audience, and already selecting from among Herod's many other documented atrocities, might simply not have recorded it. James Dunn, reported secondhand, is cited as finding the episode consistent with Herod's own well-documented pattern of violence, including against his own family, even without independent confirmation. Paul L. Maier is reported as a further named defender of plausibility, though this lesson's sources describe his specific arguments as unconvincing to most other scholars without detailing those arguments further.

This lesson states both positions and the shape of the argument on each side; it does not conclude which is correct, and no source consulted here claims that the question can be settled from the evidence currently available.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage states in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key: the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step, and a different passage element, situation, or response would be equally legitimate.

**Passage element.** Matthew 2:13-14, 19-21. Twice in this short passage, Joseph receives guidance through a dream and acts on it immediately and without any recorded protest, explanation, or delay: "he got up... by night" (2:14); "Joseph got up... and went" (2:21). Each time, the guidance concerns a vulnerable dependent (the Child) and a threat Joseph did not create and could not fully verify in the moment he had to act.

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text records no hesitation, no request for confirmation, and no words from Joseph at either point -- only the instruction and the compliance. Inference: for a first-century reader, a guardian who acts decisively and without delay on the best guidance available to protect someone vulnerable, in a situation later events confirm was genuinely dangerous (Herod's own documented willingness to kill; Archelaus's own documented massacre at the Temple), models a kind of responsible urgency rather than recklessness. The Context section above supplies what the text itself records (the instructions, the timing, the lack of recorded objection); this inference goes one step further, into why that pattern of behavior would have read as commendable rather than merely obedient.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When someone is responsible for a vulnerable dependent and faces a threat that is real, even if not yet fully verifiable by the time a decision must be made, prompt and decisive protective action on the best available guidance can be the right response -- and such action does not require public explanation or debate before being taken.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: decisive, protective action under incomplete information and time pressure, when the threat has a real, nameable basis (even if that basis cannot be completely verified in the moment). Not carried forward: dreams as the mechanism of guidance, the specific political history of Herod and Archelaus, or any promise that acting decisively will always turn out, in hindsight, to have been the right call. The bridge holds only where a real, nameable threat and real responsibility for someone vulnerable are both present -- not wherever someone wants to act quickly and call it faith.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy from how Joseph is shown acting, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed; nothing in 2:13-14 or 2:19-21 promises a modern reader that decisive action will always be vindicated the way Joseph's was.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family and safety. A parent receives a credible but not fully verifiable warning -- from a source they have good reason to trust, though they cannot confirm every detail before they would need to act -- that a specific, nameable danger exists in their household's current situation, and must decide whether to act on it before full verification is possible.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: protective, decisive action under incomplete information, not panic and not delay pending total certainty. One faithful response: the parent acts on the credible warning promptly -- removing the people at risk from the specific danger named -- while continuing afterward to seek verification and outside counsel, rather than treating the single warning as needing no further check at all once the immediate danger is addressed.

**Cautions.** This is not a model for acting on vague anxiety, a single unverified rumor with no credible source, or a "feeling" dressed up as guidance -- most situations that feel urgent are not. It is not license to bypass the people equipped to help assess a real threat (law enforcement, a trusted professional, extended family who can help verify) in favor of a private, unaccountable decision. And it does not fit a case where the supposed danger is actually a disagreement, a preference, or a conflict that calls for conversation rather than flight. The passage is not a personal-safety manual; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing, and a different reader may find it does not hold for their own case.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Beale reads the Hosea 11:1/Matthew 2:15 typology as already present within Hosea 11 when the whole chapter is read together; McCasland reads it as a meaning foreign to Hosea's own words. What would you need to see in Hosea 11 itself, apart from either scholar's larger argument, to judge which reading sits closer to the text?
2. Matthew's BSB wording at 2:18 reverses the order of two nouns from Jeremiah's own BSB wording at 31:15 ("weeping and great mourning" against "mourning and great weeping"). What would distinguish a deliberate change from an ordinary variation in how two versions of the same underlying text were transmitted or translated -- and does it matter, for what the citation is doing, which one it is?
3. Four named proposals are reported above for what "he will be called a Nazarene" is citing (the *netzer*/Isaiah 11:1 wordplay, the humble-origins theme, the Nazirite word-resemblance, and August's non-citation thesis). If none of them can be shown to be what Matthew had in mind, what is actually gained, and what is lost, by continuing to propose new candidates rather than treating the citation as unresolved?
4. The majority and minority positions on the massacre's historicity both argue from Josephus's silence -- one treats it as evidence against the event, the other as explainable and therefore not decisive either way. What kind of new evidence, if any were ever found, would actually move this question past "argument from silence" on either side?

A direct fetch of Beale's JETS article was not attempted after the August fetch failed in the same way; Beale's position here rests entirely on a search-engine-generated summary, which should be treated as provisional until the article itself is read.
- **McCasland's 1961 article was not located at its original citation.** The two secondary pages used here report slightly different page ranges for the original *Journal of Biblical Literature* 80 (1961) article (143-148 in one aggregated report, "144-46" in another); this lesson records the discrepancy rather than resolving it, and the quotations attributed to McCasland ("twists the Scripture"; "a meaning entirely foreign to the original") are reported through those secondary pages, not from the original article or its 1994 Baker Academic reprint.
- **France's page numbers for the Matthew 2:23 wordplay argument (1985, pp. 88-89; 2007, p. 91) are reported as given by the aggregating Wikipedia article**, not independently checked against France's own two commentaries (on Matthew, 1985 and 2007 editions are reported as separate works); the specific page numbers should be verified before being quoted as this lesson's own finding.
- **Josephus's *Antiquities of the Jews* and *The Jewish War* were not independently read for this lesson.** Every claim about Herod's documented acts of violence against his own family, and about Archelaus's Passover massacre, is reported through secondary (Wikipedia) summaries of Josephus, not from Josephus's own text in any edition.
- **The exact scale of the Bethlehem massacre, if historical, is reported only as a range (France's "six to twenty children," on a reported population estimate of roughly 1,000) and should be treated as one scholar's estimate, not a settled figure** -- Albright and Mann are reported elsewhere in this unit's predecessor lesson as estimating Bethlehem's population at roughly 300, a lower figure than the 1,000 reported for France's estimate here; this lesson did not resolve that discrepancy and reports both numbers as they were found.
- **Isaiah 11:1's "shoot" (netzer) and Isaiah 53:2-3's "despised" language were named in the Positions section above but were not independently checked against the shipped BSB corpus**, because neither is quoted in this lesson in quotation marks attributed to BSB; a future pass extending the Nazarene discussion should verify any direct quotation of either verse against the real BSB text before publishing it as a quotation.
- **Judges 13:5's Nazirite wording was named but not quoted**, for the same reason: this lesson reports the proposal and the grammatical objection to it without quoting the verse itself.
- **The connection between "the district of Galilee" (2:22) and Isaiah 9:1-2's "Galilee of the nations"** was considered during research (it is a well-known pairing in commentaries on Matthew 4:12-16, where Matthew later does cite Isaiah 9 directly) and set aside here: Matthew does not cite Isaiah 9 anywhere in 2:13-23, the citation belongs to a later passage in this unit's sequence, and authoring it here would risk the kind of range-to-argument mismatch flagged in this curriculum's Genesis 3 review. It is named instead as a candidate in Teach-Back Prompt 5.
- **Every direct quotation in this lesson attributed to "(BSB)," or presented in quotation marks as this app's own Bible text, was checked by a script reading the real shipped corpus at `web/public/bible/BSB/{2,24,28,40}.json` directly** (Exodus is book 2, Jeremiah is book 24, Hosea is book 28, Matthew is book 40), not from memory or from any other translation; the verification method and result are reported in full in this lesson's accompanying handoff.
