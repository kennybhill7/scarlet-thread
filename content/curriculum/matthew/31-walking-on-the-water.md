Matthew 14:22-36 is Matthew's second story of the disciples in a boat in a hard wind. Right after the feeding of the five thousand, Jesus sends them across the lake and stays behind to pray. Late in the night He comes to them "walking on the sea" (14:25, BSB), they think He is a ghost, and He says, "Take courage! It is I. Do not be afraid" (14:27). Peter asks to come to Him on the water, walks, sees the wind, sinks, and is caught. Back in the boat, "those who were in the boat worshiped Him, saying, 'Truly You are the Son of God!'" (14:33). The boat lands at Gennesaret, and the sick are healed by touching "the fringe of His cloak" (14:36). This lesson does three things. It checks what the text says, in this app's BSB and, where the English hides something, in the Greek. It grades the Old Testament threads readers have heard in the scene, from strongest to weakest, and declines the ones no source read here supports. And it reports three disputes without settling them: whether "It is I" echoes the name God gives Moses, whether Peter's request was faith or presumption, and how Matthew's ending fits Mark's very different one. Every BSB quotation below was checked by script against the app's own Bible files.

## Context

**What happens, in order.** Jesus sends the disciples ahead by boat and dismisses the crowds (14:22), prays alone on the mountain (14:23), and comes to the wind-beaten boat in the night (14:24-25). The disciples cry, "It's a ghost!" (14:26), and He answers them (14:27). Peter asks to come, walks, sinks and is caught (14:28-31). Then "the wind died down" (14:32), the confession follows (14:33), and they land at Gennesaret, where the sick are healed (14:34-36).

**A note for anyone reading this in their own storm.** This passage is about fear in a crisis, and some readers will come to it in one. If you are in emotional crisis or thinking of harming yourself, or are worried about someone who is, a study note is the wrong place to turn. In the United States you can call or text 988, or chat through 988lifeline.org, at any hour; anywhere, call your local emergency number if anyone is in immediate danger. A doctor, a counselor or a pastor who knows you can help in ways a lesson cannot.

**Where the passage sits.** The scene follows directly on the feeding of the five thousand (14:13-21), which itself began when Jesus heard about John the Baptist's death and "withdrew by boat privately to a solitary place" (14:13). It is Matthew's second sea story. The first, the storm of 8:23-27, was covered in this curriculum's lesson on Matthew 8:18-34, which graded a Psalm 107 thread and a Jonah 1 thread. Those connections are not repeated here. Chrysostom (Homily 50 on Matthew) sets the two scenes side by side: "whereas before they had Him in the ship when this befell them, now they were alone by themselves."

**The "fourth watch."** In the shipped BSB the phrase "fourth watch" occurs in only two verses, Matthew 14:25 and its parallel in Mark 6:48 (a script count). Commentators explain it as the Roman night of four three-hour watches. Meyer puts the fourth "from three till somewhere about six o'clock," and Poole "about three hours before the rising of the sun." Jerome, in the *Catena Aurea*, draws the consequence for the story: it "shews that they had been in danger the whole night."

**Three tellings, and one missing.** Mark 6:45-52 and John 6:15-21 tell the same crossing. Luke does not: after the feeding, Luke moves to Peter's confession (Luke 9:18-20). Augustine had already noticed this. In *The Harmony of the Gospels* (II.47) he says that Luke "makes no mention of that little ship, and of the Lord's pathway over the waters." He also says that Mark's version "is similar to Matthew's version, except that nothing is said as to Peter's walking upon the waters." A script search confirms it: the name Peter does not occur in Mark 6:45-52 or John 6:15-21. Ellicott, the Expositor's Greek Testament and the Pulpit Commentary all mark 14:28-31 as Matthew's alone. Mark alone says, "He intended to pass by them" (Mark 6:48). John alone gives the crowd's attempt to "make Him king by force" as the reason Jesus withdrew (John 6:15). The endings differ most, and Positions 3 takes them up.

**"Take courage! It is I. Do not be afraid."** In the BSB, Matthew 14:27 and Mark 6:50 are identical, and John 6:20 has "It is I; do not be afraid." The Greek is the same in Matthew and Mark in every edition Biblehub lists: *tharseite, egō eimi, mē phobeisthe*. John has only *egō eimi, mē phobeisthe*. The middle two words, *egō eimi*, literally "I am," are the subject of Positions 1. "Take courage" occurs in the BSB of Matthew only three times, at 9:2, 9:22 and 14:27 (a script count). And the Pulpit Commentary, noting that John 6:20 lacks the word, suggests that "Take courage" may be "a duplicate rendering of the Aramaic" for "Do not be afraid," because "the LXX. commonly translates 'fear ye not' by θαρσεῖτε," citing Exodus 14:13. Swete's Septuagint confirms that verse: where the BSB has Moses tell the people at the sea, "Do not be afraid. Stand firm" (Exodus 14:13), the Greek opens *Tharseite*.

**Walking on the sea, in the Greek.** Matthew says Jesus came "walking on the sea" (14:25), and the disciples saw Him "walking on the sea" (14:26). The Greek editions do not agree on the case of the noun. At 14:25, SBL, Nestle 1904, Westcott-Hort and Tischendorf read *epi tēn thalassan* (accusative). The Byzantine Majority Text, the Greek Orthodox text and both editions of the Textus Receptus read *epi tēs thalassēs* (genitive). At 14:26 the split runs the other way. Meyer, writing from the Greek, explains the difference as "walking over the sea" against "walking on the sea" and insists that "According to both readings alike, we are to understand a miraculous walking on the water, but not a walking along the shore." Mark 6:48 reads the genitive, the case Swete's Septuagint uses in Job 9:8, in every edition listed. Peter's walk uses different words: he "walked on the water" (14:29), a phrase found in no other verse of the BSB (a script search).

**"You of little faith" and "why did you doubt?"** Jesus' rebuke at 14:31 uses one Greek adjective, *oligopistos*. Thayer's lexicon lists its New Testament uses as Matthew 6:30, 8:26, 14:31 and 16:8, and Luke 12:28. A related noun, *oligopistia*, stands at Matthew 17:20 in the critical editions (Lachmann, Tischendorf, Tregelles, Westcott-Hort), where the Byzantine and Textus Receptus texts read *apistia*, "unbelief" (confirmed in Biblehub's parallel Greek of 17:20). So the BSB's "little faith" occurs in five verses of Matthew (6:30, 8:26, 14:31, 16:8, 17:20; a script count), but only four of them use the adjective. This curriculum's lesson on Matthew 6:19-34 discusses the first; the lesson on 8:18-34 discusses the second. The verb for "doubt," *distazō*, occurs in the New Testament only twice, both in Matthew: here, and at 28:17, where the eleven "worshiped Him, but some doubted" (Thayer; BSB). Ian Paul (Psephizo) notes the same pair and says the word "has a sense not of asking questions but of being in two minds."

**"Truly You are the Son of God."** In Matthew's BSB, "Son of God" occurs in eight verses (a script count). The three before 14:33 are spoken by the tempter (4:3, 4:6) and by demons (8:29). So 14:33 is the first time people in Matthew's story say it. Meyer makes the same observation: "this is, according to Matthew, the first time that Jesus was designated the Son of God by men." The exact wording "Truly You are the Son of God" occurs in no other verse of the BSB, though a near match comes at the cross, where the centurion and his men say, "Truly this was the Son of God" (27:54). In the BSB, the English word "worshiped" occurs in Matthew only at 2:11, 14:33, 28:9 and 28:17 (an English count; the Greek verb behind it was not counted across the Gospel).

**Gennesaret and "the fringe of His cloak" (14:34-36).** The boat lands at Gennesaret, the people recognize Jesus, and "all who touched Him were healed" (14:36). The phrase "fringe of His cloak" occurs in four BSB verses: Matthew 9:20, Matthew 14:36, Mark 6:56 and Luke 8:44. What the Greek word *kraspedon* names, a tassel or an edge, was handled in this curriculum's lesson on Matthew 9:18-38, with its Numbers 15 connection, and is not repeated here. Two named readers judge the crowd's request differently. Calvin thinks "they were under the influence of some superstition, when they limited the grace of Christ to a touch of his robe," though "he accommodates himself to their ignorance." Chrysostom reads the same request as showing "a more abundant faith," learned, he says, from the woman of 9:20.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 14:22-33 is built, not on what the construction is meant to teach.

**Two sea stories, side by side.** Matthew 8:23-27 and 14:22-33 share a setting and several words. Both have a boat, a hard wind, frightened disciples and a cry beginning "Lord, save": "Lord, save us! We are perishing!" (8:25) and "Lord, save me!" (14:30). In the New Testament of the BSB, only these two verses have "Lord, save" (a script search, ignoring capitals). The Old Testament's "O LORD, save" (for example Psalm 118:25) renders the divine name, and that link was not graded here. Both contain "You of little faith" (8:26; 14:31). The differences are as checkable. In chapter 8 Jesus is in the boat asleep; in chapter 14 He is on the mountain and comes to them. In chapter 8 He rebukes the wind and sea; in chapter 14 He gives no command, and "the wind died down" when He and Peter climb into the boat (14:32). And the endings differ: "What kind of man is this?" (8:27) against "Truly You are the Son of God!" (14:33). Chrysostom reads the second ending as an answer to the first: "Whereas before this they had said, What manner of man is this ... now it is not so," and "by degrees he was leading them all higher and higher." The Expositor's Greek Testament likewise calls 14:33 "a great advance" on 8:27. Matthew does not say whether the pair is meant to be read this way.

**Fear named three times, then doubt.** The disciples "cried out in fear" (14:26), Jesus says, "Do not be afraid" (14:27), Peter "was afraid" (14:30), and the rebuke asks why he doubted (14:31). Fear of what they see is answered by a word, then returns when Peter looks at the wind. In Matthew's BSB, "do not be afraid" occurs in eight verses (1:20; 10:26, 28, 31; 14:27; 17:7; 28:5, 10; a script count, ignoring capitals).

**Peter's walk repeats Jesus' walk.** Jesus "went out to them, walking on the sea" (14:25), and Peter "walked on the water, and came toward Jesus" (14:29). Peter asks for the same thing Jesus is doing, but his request is framed as coming to Jesus, not as walking: "command me to come to You on the water" (14:28). Chrysostom and the Pulpit Commentary both make this point (Positions 2).

## Positions

Three questions in this passage draw named, published disagreement. None is settled here. Each reading is given in its holders' own words where a source was read directly, and marked as secondhand where it was not.

**1. Does "It is I" (14:27) echo the name God gives Moses?** The Greek *egō eimi* is literally "I am." In Swete's Septuagint, God answers Moses at the bush, *Egō eimi ho ōn*, "I am the One who is" (Exodus 3:14; the BSB, from the Hebrew, has "I AM WHO I AM"). The Septuagint of Isaiah also puts *egō eimi* in God's mouth, for example at 43:10 and 51:12, where the BSB has "I am He" and "I, even I, am He." The question is whether Matthew's two words point to any of this.

*An echo of the divine name.* Jerome, as excerpted in the *Catena Aurea*, offers it as one of two readings: "Whereas He says, 'It is I,' without saying who, either they might be able to understand Him speaking through the darkness of night; or they might know that it was He who had spoken to Moses, 'Say unto the children of Israel, He that is has sent me unto you.'" Richard Hays argues the echo for Mark's version of the scene (Mark 6:50). His book was not read for this lesson; he is reported by Derek Rishmawy (2017), who quotes him: "Thus, when Jesus speaks this same phrase, 'I am,' in his sea-crossing epiphany, it serves to underscore the claim of divine identity" present in the story as a whole. In Rishmawy's summary, Hays treats the phrase as "a stock self-identifying phrase of Israel's God throughout Scripture," citing Deuteronomy 32:39 and Isaiah 41:4, 43:11 and 51:12. On this reading, the words come in a scene already about something the Old Testament says God does, walking on the sea (see the Job 9 thread below), and they carry more than recognition.

*Ordinary self-identification.* Ian Paul (Psephizo, 2020), writing on Matthew's version, does not settle it: "It is not very clear from Matthew's account whether we should see, in this, an echo of the 'divine name' in Exod 3.14, not least since this phrase is the natural one a person would use to give reassurance about their identity." He adds: "But of course we read these words in a different light following the resurrection and ascension than the disciples would necessarily have taken them at the time." Andrew Perriman (P.OST, 2014), reviewing Hays's argument for Mark, writes that the Job argument "does not seem too far-fetched, at least when taken in isolation," but also: "I would dismiss the references to Exodus as tenuous." Three older commentators read for this lesson hold neither position: they read the words as plain recognition without raising the question. Gill paraphrases: "it is I, your Master, Saviour, and Redeemer, and not any hurtful spirit." The Expositor's Greek Testament: "the mere sound of His voice would be enough." Calvin: "he speaks, and desires his disciples to recognize him."

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the echo reading, Paul's point applies: men who have just shouted "It's a ghost!" (14:26) would naturally be answered with "it's me." Hays's setting argument, as Rishmawy reports it, rests on three supports. Only one, the walking-on-the-sea wording of Job 9:8, is in Matthew. Mark's "pass by" (Mark 6:48, beside Job 9:11) and the Exodus 33-34 "passing by" are not, and the Exodus 3:14 echo is drawn from all three: in Rishmawy's summary, Jesus' words "should probably be heard, then," as that echo. Ian Paul likewise says Mark draws out this significance "more explicitly than Matthew." Against the ordinary reading, Hays's case rests on the setting: the phrase sits inside an act that Israel's Scriptures ascribe to God, so an ordinary phrase can carry a further sense. Jerome holds both readings open in one sentence. This lesson grades the Exodus 3 thread as a plausible allusion (see Teach-Back 5) and does not decide what Matthew intended.

**2. Was Peter's request faith or presumption (14:28-31)?** Peter says, "Lord, if it is You ... command me to come to You on the water" (14:28). Jesus says, "Come," Peter walks, then sinks, and Jesus asks, "why did you doubt?" (14:31). Readers have long divided on how to hear the request.

*An act of faith that faltered.* Chrysostom (Homily 50) reads the request as ardent faith: "He said not, Pray and entreat, but, bid. Do you see how great his ardor, how great his faith?" His next sentence qualifies it: "Yet surely he is hereby often in danger, by seeking things beyond his measure." He then adds that Peter asked "for love only, not for display," since he said "Bid me come unto You," not "Bid me walk on the water," and that "not the assault of the wind, but his want of faith had wrought his overthrow." Jerome, in the *Catena Aurea*, finds Peter "the one of the most ardent faith," believing "that by the will of his Master he will be able to do that which by nature he cannot do"; of the sinking he writes, "faith burned at his heart, but human frailty drew him into the deep." Bengel calls the word "command" "A remarkable exercise of faith." The Pulpit Commentary holds that in "if it be thou" "No doubt is implied," and that "He will only come at Christ's command."

*A rash request.* Calvin reads the condition as a sign of weak faith: "The condition which he lays down shows that his faith was not yet fully settled." He continues: "without the wings of faith, he desires to fly at will." His next sentence qualifies it: "And yet there is no room to doubt that this longing sprung from a good principle; but as it degenerates into a faulty excess, it cannot be applauded as good." He adds that "Peter immediately begins to smart for his rashness." Barnes calls the request "an instance of the characteristic ardor and rashness of Peter," who "had less real faith than he supposed," and in the same note "really attached to Jesus." Benson: "This was a rash request, proceeding from the warmth and forwardness of Peter's natural temper." Two readers are mixed. Ellicott reads "If it be Thou" as a doubt, calls Peter "Eager but not steadfast" and says "he demands a sign," but adds that "at first his faith sustains him." Augustine, as the *Catena Aurea* excerpts his Sermon 76, has Peter say, "This I am not able by myself, but in Thee I am able," and also writes, "Peter then presumed on the Lord, he tottered as man, but returned to the Lord."

*Leaning, with hesitation.* R. T. France, as Ian Paul quotes his commentary (NICNT, 2007, pp. 567-568; not read directly), writes that it "is not so clear ... whether Matthew intends us to see Peter in this incident as an example of valid faith which went wrong, or as from the beginning taking a foolhardy risk," and that the failure "perhaps suggests" a "cautionary tale." France's own summary speaks of the danger "to which he had rashly exposed himself" (p. 567, as Paul quotes it). Paul himself remains undecided. Of the popular reading of Peter as a model for stepping out in faith, says "it is not at all clear that this is how we are meant to read the episode," and suggests reading the scene with Jesus, not Peter, at its center.

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the faith reading, Calvin points to the condition "if it is You" after Jesus has already spoken, and France to the outcome. Against the rash reading, the Pulpit Commentary and Chrysostom point out that Peter waits for a command and receives one ("Come," 14:29). This lesson does not decide between them. A reader who is asking whether their own bold step was faith or rashness needs a pastor who knows them, not a study note.

**3. How does Matthew's ending fit Mark's, and what does "Son of God" mean in 14:33?** Matthew ends: "those who were in the boat worshiped Him, saying, 'Truly You are the Son of God!'" (14:33). Mark ends: "the disciples were utterly astounded, for they had not understood about the loaves, but their hearts had been hardened" (Mark 6:51-52, BSB). The difference is checkable. The explanations are not.

*Both are true of the same night.* Calvin reads Mark's charge at face value. The disciples showed "very extraordinary stupidity" in fearing Jesus after the loaves, and "Mark purposely mentions blindness, in order to inform us that it is no new thing if men have their eyes closed against the manifest works of God, till they are enlightened from above." On 14:33 he writes of "They that were in the ship," "I understand these words to refer not only to the disciples, but to the sailors and other passengers," and reads their confession as giving Jesus "the honor of the Messiah." Poole holds that the confession was real but "far from a steady faith as to that point, which the disciples yet wanted."

*The speakers may not be the disciples.* Meyer argues that the Greek phrase behind "those who were in the boat" (BSB) names "not the disciples ... but those who, besides them, were crossing in the boat, the crew and others," and Ellicott calls them "probably ... the crew of the boat, or some chance passengers." Jerome, in the *Catena Aurea*, also takes them as "the sailors and pilots." Rabanus Maurus, in the same work, leaves it open: "This may be understood either of the sailors, or of the Apostles." The Expositor's Greek Testament takes the other side: "presumably the disciples alone referred to."

*Matthew's own shaping of the story.* Meyer, while holding the scene credible, calls 14:33 "a traditional amplification" connected with the Peter episode, "but yet as containing nothing improbable." Ian Paul observes that "Matthew generally softens the failures of the disciples as a whole compared with Mark," while leaving Peter's failure "on display for all to see." On this reading, the two endings reflect how each evangelist tells the disciples' story, as with "little faith" in Matthew 8:26 against "Do you still have no faith?" in Mark 4:40 (BSB).

*What the title means.* Meyer glosses it "the Messiah," and so does Calvin. The Pulpit Commentary holds that it is more than a moral relation to God, but adds: "Observe, however, that not even so is it a profession of faith in his absolute Divinity." The *Catechism of the Catholic Church*, without naming this verse in the text read, says that "Those who called Jesus 'son of God', as the Messiah of Israel, perhaps meant nothing more than this" (§441), and goes on: "Such is not the case for Simon Peter when he confesses Jesus as 'the Christ, the Son of the living God'" (§442). On the other side, Gill reads it as "not by creation ... nor by office ... but by nature; being of the same essence, perfections, and power, with God, his Father." Ellicott says the speakers confessed "that the Prophet of Nazareth was more than man." Jerome turns the sailors' confession against Arius: "how does Arrius preach in the Church itself that He is a creature?"

**Objections each reading faces.** Against harmonizing, Meyer's "traditional amplification" grants that Matthew's ending goes beyond Mark's. Against reading the speakers as the crew, the Expositor's Greek Testament holds that the disciples alone are meant. Against the redactional reading, Calvin and Poole hold that a confession and a lingering hardness can belong to the same night. The meaning of the title divides Meyer, Calvin and the Pulpit Commentary (Messiah; the *Catechism* places such confessions generally on this side) from Gill, Ellicott and Jerome (more than a man). Augustine's *Harmony of the Gospels* (II.47), which compares the three tellings, does not take up this difference. This lesson ranks none of the readings.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, a model of the shape and not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 14:30-31: "But when he saw the strength of the wind, he was afraid, and beginning to sink, cried out, 'Lord, save me!' Immediately Jesus reached out His hand and took hold of Peter. 'You of little faith,' He said, 'why did you doubt?'" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: Peter's cry is three words, Jesus' hand comes "Immediately," and the rebuke comes after the rescue. The disciples had rowed against the wind most of the night before Jesus came (14:24-25); Chrysostom says Jesus "lets them be tempest-tost all the night." Inference (this lesson's): for disciples who had just been afraid and who would face other storms, the scene showed both that fear and doubt were real in Jesus' closest followers, and that a cry for help was answered.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** A person whose faith falters in a crisis can still cry out for help, and wavering does not put them out of reach of rescue in this story.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: honest fear, a plain cry for help, and a hand that reaches the one who is sinking. Not carried forward: walking on water as something a reader should attempt; the idea that every crisis will end quickly; or any rule that a person's suffering is caused by their doubt.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a narrated rescue of one disciple, not a command and not a general promise. Promise scope: the passage narrates what happened to Peter on one night. It does not promise that storms will stop, or stop soon. The disciples were in the wind for most of the night, and the wind dies down only at the end (14:32). Nor does it say that anyone who sinks has done so because of weak faith. Jesus' question "why did you doubt?" is addressed to Peter about this moment, and this curriculum's lesson on Matthew 6:19-34 keeps a parallel distinction about hardship and faith.

**Modern domain and situation.** Health and family. A woman's husband is in intensive care after a sudden illness. She has prayed, but she is frightened and cannot sleep, and she wonders whether she believes at all. Someone in her small group tells her that her fear is the reason he is not getting better.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: crying out honestly, and accepting help. One faithful response: she tells God plainly that she is afraid, in words as short as Peter's if that is all she has, and lets her church and friends carry meals and sit with her. She talks with the hospital chaplain or her pastor about the remark that hurt her.

**Cautions.** Several readers quoted above say Peter sank because his faith gave way at that moment; Chrysostom puts it as "not the assault of the wind, but his want of faith." That is a reading of one scene. The passage states no rule that a person's illness, loss or crisis is caused by their doubt. Calvin, who is hardest on Peter, also writes that "the Lord indulges our weakness, and stretches out his hand, that the waters may not swallow us up altogether." Nothing here suggests that anxiety, panic or depression is a lack of faith, or that it should be handled by trying harder to believe. If fear has become constant, or you have thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 in the United States, or contact your local emergency number if you are in immediate danger, and speak with a doctor or counselor. This bridge is a worked example for the learner to test, not counsel for any particular person.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Matthew's two sea stories end with a question (8:27) and a confession (14:33). What would you need to check in Matthew 8-14, and in Mark's parallels, to judge whether Matthew means the second story to answer the first, or simply tells two similar events?
2. Jesus' words in 14:27 are the same three Greek phrases in Matthew and Mark, and John lacks "Take courage." If the Exodus echo depends on the setting rather than on the two words alone, which features of the setting would have to be present for you to hear it, and which of them are in Matthew?
3. The verb "doubt" (*distazō*) appears only at 14:31 and 28:17, both times beside worship (14:33; 28:17). Is that pairing a pattern Matthew builds, or a coincidence of a rare word? What evidence would tell you?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 14:22-36 in order. (b) Quote, in the BSB's words, Jesus' answer at 14:27 and the confession at 14:33. (c) Name two checkable differences between Matthew's telling and Mark's.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) setting after the feeding, and the fourth watch; (1:00-2:00) the three tellings and what only Matthew has; (2:00-3:00) "It is I" and Positions 1, with the Exodus 3 and Job 9 threads graded; (3:00-4:00) Peter's walk and Positions 2; (4:00-5:00) Matthew 14:33 against Mark 6:51-52 (Positions 3), ending on an open question.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, one worked candidate: "Calling the Exodus 3 echo only 'plausible' undersells it; a man who walks on the sea and says 'I am' is claiming to be God." (Separate what Matthew's words show from what the setting suggests, and say which named reader argues each step.) A second runs the other way: "Even 'plausible' oversells it; the phrase is ordinary Greek for 'it's me.'" (What does Jerome's double reading allow, and why do Hays's readers point to the setting?)
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 14:22-33 does not say whether Peter's request was wise. Jesus grants it and then asks "why did you doubt?" Readers divide on whether that question bears on the request (Calvin) or only on the sinking (Pulpit). (b) The passage does not promise that storms end quickly, or that sinking is caused by weak faith (see Promise scope). (c) Matthew 14:33 does not settle what "Son of God" meant to the speakers; named readers divide between Messiah and divine nature.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words in both texts:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Job 9:8-11 | allusion | plausible | God "treads on the waves of the sea"; the Septuagint's "walking ... upon the sea" shares Matthew's verb and preposition; Hays's main extra evidence ("pass by") is Mark's, not Matthew's |
| Psalm 77:16-20 | motif | plausible | God's "path led through the sea"; a shared theme, not shared wording (BSB "through"; Septuagint "in"; neither "on"); Perriman's exodus reading rests mainly on Mark but cites Matthew's Peter episode |
| Exodus 3:13-15 | allusion | plausible | *Egō eimi* in both, but only two common words; Jerome offers it as one of two readings and Hays (via Rishmawy) argues it for Mark; Paul finds it unclear and Perriman calls it tenuous |

The Job 9, Psalm 77 and Exodus 3 rows are this lesson's Old Testament threads, and none is stronger than plausible: Matthew cites none of them. Defend one row, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`. Three candidates were considered and not authored. **Isaiah 43:10 and 51:12**, where the Septuagint has *egō eimi* in God's speech close to a call not to fear (43:5, "Do not be afraid, for I am with you"; 51:12), are in some ways nearer to 14:27 than Exodus 3:14 is. Isaiah 43:2 even promises, "When you pass through the waters, I will be with you" (BSB). But no source read for this lesson argues them for Matthew; Hays's list, as Rishmawy reports it, treats them as later uses of the same divine formula, so they stand or fall with the Exodus row. **Psalm 18:16** ("He reached down from on high and took hold of me; He drew me out of deep waters," BSB) resembles Jesus reaching out and taking hold of Peter (14:31), but the English "took hold" is a translation match that was not checked in the Greek, and no source read here makes the link. **Exodus 14:13**, where Moses' "Do not be afraid" is *Tharseite* in the Septuagint, shares one common imperative with 14:27; Perriman notes it for Mark, and one word is not enough to establish an allusion.
