Matthew 16:1-12 is two short scenes joined by a boat. In the first, "the Pharisees and Sadducees" ask Jesus "to show them a sign from heaven" (16:1, BSB). He answers with a saying about the evening and morning sky, repeats almost word for word what He said to the first request for a sign, "A wicked and adulterous generation demands a sign, but none will be given it except the sign of Jonah" (16:4), and leaves. In the second, the disciples have forgotten bread, Jesus warns them to "beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees" (16:6), they take Him literally, and He reminds them of both feedings until "they understood" that He meant "the teaching of the Pharisees and Sadducees" (16:12). This lesson checks the repeated words, a weather saying some of the oldest manuscripts lack, the two Greek words for "basket," and who the two named parties were. It then reports three disputes without settling them, and names one misuse plainly: a warning about two parties of Jesus' day has been widened into a judgment on Jewish teaching as a whole. Every BSB quotation below was checked by script against the app's own Bible files.

## Context

**What happens, in order (16:1-12).** The Pharisees and Sadducees "tested Jesus" by asking for "a sign from heaven" (16:1). He answers that they can read the red sky at evening and morning, "but not the signs of the times" (16:2-3). He repeats the refusal and the sign of Jonah, "Then He left them and went away" (16:4). Across the lake "the disciples forgot to take bread" (16:5). Jesus says, "Watch out! ... Beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees" (16:6). They conclude, "It is because we did not bring any bread" (16:7). He calls them "You of little faith" (16:8), asks whether they remember the five loaves and the seven loaves and "how many basketfuls you gathered" (16:9-10), and repeats the warning (16:11). "Then they understood" (16:12).

**Where the passage sits.** The scene follows the feeding of the four thousand and a crossing to "the region of Magadan" (15:39), a name that occurs only there in the BSB (a script search). This curriculum's lesson on Matthew 14:1-21 covers the five thousand, and its lesson on the feeding of the four thousand (Matthew 15) covers the second; 16:9-10 looks back at both. Mark tells the same two scenes in the same order (Mark 8:11-21; see Teach-Back 5).

**"A sign from heaven."** In the BSB the phrase occurs only at Matthew 16:1, Mark 8:11 and Luke 11:16 (a script search). Ellicott guesses at "a sign like Samuel's thunder from the clear blue sky" (1 Samuel 12:18) or "Elijah's fire from heaven" (1 Kings 18:38); Chrysostom (Homily 53), that He should "curb the moon, or bring down thunderbolts." The text itself does not say.

**The red sky, and the manuscripts (16:2-3).** The weather saying runs from "When evening comes" to "the signs of the times." Ellicott writes: "It is remarkable that some of the best MSS., including the Vatican and Sinaitic, omit the whole of these suggestive words." He does not think them a later addition, "looking to their singular originality of form," and suggests copyists wishing "to bring the reports in the two Gospels into closer agreement," since Mark lacks them. A note printed with Meyer's commentary lists the omitting witnesses (including B and Sinaiticus, Codex א) and adds, "Tisch. 8 encloses it in brackets." The Pulpit Commentary: "These verses are most probably genuine; and they certainly could not have been foisted into the text from Luke 12:54-56. The circumstances are too different, and the variations too marked." Luke's saying is about "a cloud rising in the west" and "the south wind" (Luke 12:54-55, BSB).

What the editions show can be checked. On Bible Hub's parallel page, all nine Greek editions print the words; only Tischendorf's eighth edition encloses them in square brackets, opening at 16:2 and closing at the end of 16:3 (saved copies, read line by line). Bible Hub's line for the Westcott-Hort/NA27 text shows no brackets there, so whether NA27 marks the passage cannot be read from that page. The BSB's own text on Bible Hub prints the verses without brackets and carries a footnote: "Several manuscripts do not include When evening comes... of the times. from verses 2 and 3." This app's shipped BSB file stores only verse text, with no footnotes, so a learner reading 16:2-3 in the app sees the words with no sign that they are uncertain.

One smaller split sits inside 16:3. The Byzantine, Greek Orthodox and both Textus Receptus editions add "Hypocrites" (*hypokritai*) before "you know how to interpret"; the SBL, Nestle, Westcott-Hort and Tischendorf texts do not, and neither does the BSB. The Pulpit Commentary notes that the word "is omitted by some uncial manuscripts, the Vulgate, etc., and many modern editors."

**The sign of Jonah again (16:4).** In the BSB, 16:4 matches 12:39 from "A wicked and adulterous generation demands a sign" to "except the sign of"; 12:39 then ends "the prophet Jonah," 16:4 "Jonah." The phrase "wicked and adulterous" occurs only in these two verses of the BSB, and "sign of Jonah" only at Matthew 16:4 and Luke 11:29 (script searches). In Greek, the sixteen words of 16:4 from *genea* to *Iona* are the same sixteen words, in the same order, as in 12:39, in every edition Bible Hub lists (a script comparison; the Greek Orthodox text writes *ei me* as one word at 12:39). The critical editions end the clause there; the Byzantine, Greek Orthodox and Textus Receptus texts add "the prophet" in 16:4 as well. This time Jesus does not explain the sign. The Pulpit Commentary: "These words our Lord had already uttered on a former occasion (Matthew 12:39), but he does not here explain them." What the sign of Jonah means, and the Jonah 1:17 background, belong to this curriculum's lesson on Matthew 12:38-50 (lesson 26), and are not repeated here. Mark's version has no Jonah: "no sign will be given to this generation" (Mark 8:12). Ellicott reads Mark's "no sign" as none "of the kind that was demanded," and the Cambridge Bible as "no such sign as they demanded."

**Two parties, named together.** In the BSB, "Pharisees and Sadducees" occurs in Matthew 3:7 and 16:1, 6, 11 and 12, and elsewhere only at Acts 23:7; no other Gospel has the pair. Matthew names the Sadducees in seven verses (3:7; 16:1, 6, 11, 12; 22:23, 34). Mark's warning names "the leaven of the Pharisees and of Herod" (Mark 8:15), and Luke's "the leaven of the Pharisees, which is hypocrisy" (Luke 12:1), in a different setting. In Matthew's Greek one article covers both groups (*tes zymes ton Pharisaion kai Saddoukaion*) in 16:6, 11 and 12 in all nine editions. The Expositor's Greek Testament reads it as "one leaven, of two parties viewed as one, hence no article before Σαδ."

The two were different groups, with different beliefs. Josephus, a first-century Jewish historian, describes them in his *Antiquities* (Whiston's translation). The Pharisees hold that "souls have an immortal rigor in them, and that under the earth there will be rewards or punishments"; and "on account of which doctrines they are able greatly to persuade the body of the people" (18.1.3). "But the doctrine of the Sadducees is this: That souls die with the bodies; nor do they regard the observation of any thing besides what the law enjoins them," and "this doctrine is received but by a few, yet by those still of the greatest dignity" (18.1.4). Elsewhere he gives the sharpest difference: "the Pharisees have delivered to the people a great many observances by succession from their fathers, which are not written in the laws of Moses; and for that reason it is that the Sadducees reject them" (13.10.6). Acts records the same divide: "the Sadducees say that there is neither a resurrection, nor angels, nor spirits, but the Pharisees acknowledge them all" (Acts 23:8, BSB).

Kaufmann Kohler, writing in *The Jewish Encyclopedia* (1901-1906), describes the Sadducees as the party representing the "interests of Temple and priesthood," taking their name from "the descendants of Zadok, the high priests." He adds a caution worth keeping: "Nor is anything definite known about the political and religious views of the Sadducees except what is recorded by their opponents in the works of Josephus, in the Talmudic literature, and in the New Testament writings." Of the Pharisees he says that "No true estimate of the character of the Pharisees can be obtained from the New Testament writings, which take a polemical attitude toward them," "nor from Josephus, who, writing for Roman readers ... represents the latter as a philosophical sect." Every source in this paragraph has a point of view, including the Gospel.

Why the two appear together is discussed in Positions 1. Ellicott notes that the Sadducees "do not elsewhere appear in our Lord's Galilean ministry," and Bengel, that "The Evangelists describe only two attempts of the Sadducees against our Lord."

**The disciples and the bread (16:5-11).** What the disciples thought Jesus meant is a guess in every commentary read. Calvin: they took Him to mean "only to withdraw them from outward intercourse," since "it was customary among the Jews not to take food in company with irreligious men." Chrysostom says "they were full of trepidation about the purifications of the Jews, and the observances of meats." Jesus' rebuke opens with "You of little faith," a phrase the BSB has in Matthew at 6:30, 8:26, 14:31 and 16:8 and once in Luke (12:28); this curriculum's lesson on Matthew 14:22-36 reports the Greek word behind it. "Do you still not understand?" occurs in the BSB at Matthew 15:16 and 16:9 and Mark 8:21 (script searches).

**Two words for "basket" (16:9-10).** The BSB renders both "basketfuls." The Greek does not. At 16:9 the word is *kophinos*, the word used for the twelve baskets of the five thousand (14:20); at 16:10 it is *spyris*, the word used for the seven baskets of the four thousand (15:37). All nine Greek editions on Bible Hub agree at each of the four verses; Westcott-Hort spells the second word *sphyris*. Mark keeps the same pair (Mark 8:19-20). Ellicott: "The distinction between the two kinds of baskets—the cophini and the spurides—is ... strictly observed here." Commentators differ on what it was. The Pulpit Commentary calls them "small baskets" and "large panniers," pointing, as the Cambridge Bible also does, to Acts 9:25, where Paul is lowered "in a basket" through the wall (BSB), a *spyris* in the Cambridge Bible's note. Meyer holds that the difference "does not lie in σπυρίς being larger," but in the fact that "κόφινος is a general term, whereas σπυρίς denotes a food-basket in particular." The Cambridge Bible on 15:37: "Why the disciples brought different kinds of baskets on the two occasions we cannot determine." The Expositor's Greek Testament reports, without adopting it, a view of Hilary and of B. F. Westcott that the second kind of basket points to Gentile recipients (secondhand; neither was read).

The basket question also bears on whether the two feedings were one event told twice; the Pulpit Commentary calls that view "wilful perversity," since Jesus here sets the two side by side. That dispute belongs to this curriculum's lessons on the feedings.

**Two small variants (16:11-12).** In 16:11 the Byzantine, Greek Orthodox and Textus Receptus texts run Jesus' words as one question, without the critical editions' second sentence ("But beware..."), which the BSB follows. In 16:12 Tischendorf's eighth edition alone reads "the leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees" where the others have "the leaven of bread." Neither changes the sense the BSB gives.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 16:1-12 is built, not on what the construction is meant to teach.

**Two scenes, each turning on a word.** In the first, the askers want "a sign"; Jesus answers that they can read "the appearance of the sky" but not "the signs of the times" (16:3). In Greek *semeion* stands three times in 16:4; Bengel calls it "A weighty repetition." In the second, the disciples hear "leaven" and think of "bread," a word the BSB uses in 16:5, 7, 8, 11 and 12. Each scene ends with the word reinterpreted: the sign is Jonah's, and the leaven is "teaching." Chrysostom asks why Jesus did not say plainly, "Beware of their teaching," and answers: "His will is to remind them of what had been done, for He knew they had forgotten."

## Positions

Three questions in this passage draw named, published disagreement. None is settled here. Each reading is given in its holders' own words where a source was read directly, and marked as secondhand where it was not.

**1. A second demand for a sign, or one demand told twice?** Matthew has two requests for a sign (12:38; 16:1) with almost the same answer. Mark has only the second (8:11-12), and Luke only the first (11:16, 29).

*Two occasions.* Heinrich Meyer, a German Lutheran exegete: "Not a duplicate of the incident recorded in Matthew 12:38 ... but a second demand for a sign, and that from heaven, in which respect it is distinguished from the first." On the alliance of the two parties, which he reports some critics found "utterly improbable," Meyer answers with Theophylact, a Byzantine commentator, whose Greek says in substance that though the Pharisees and Sadducees disagreed in their teachings, "they conspire together against Christ" (this lesson's rendering). The Pulpit Commentary: "Under similar circumstances he repeats himself." Chrysostom reads the scene the same way: the askers, "having been told this a second time, ... ought to have inquired." The Cambridge Bible says the coalition "can only be accounted for by the uniting influence of a strong common hostility against Jesus."

*One demand, doubled in tradition.* David Friedrich Strauss, in *The Life of Jesus Critically Examined* (§85, George Eliot's translation), reads 16:1-4 as Matthew's second version of one tradition. He notes that in Matthew the Pharisees come "in the unlikely companionship of Sadducees," and finds it "already improbable enough, that Jesus should have twice responded to the above requisition with the same enigmatical reference to Jonah." He suggests how the doubling arose: Matthew "may have been induced to double the demand of a sign, by the verbal variation which he met with, the required sign being at one time called simply a σημεῖον, at another a σημεῖον ἐκ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ." He concludes that "Matthew here presents us, as Luke often does elsewhere, with a fictitious introduction to a discourse of Jesus." In §79 he reports Schneckenburger's view that Matthew sets the Pharisees and Sadducees side by side "in a way that represents, not their real hostility, but their association in the memory of tradition, in which one opposite suggested another" (secondhand; Schneckenburger was not read). Kohler, from a different starting point, holds that in the Gospels "'Pharisees' was inserted ... wherever the high priests and Sadducees or Herodians were originally mentioned as the persecutors of Jesus."

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the two-occasion reading stands Strauss's objection, quoted above, to the same "enigmatical reference to Jonah" given twice. Against the one-demand reading, Meyer holds that the request "from heaven" distinguishes the second scene, and the Pulpit Commentary calls the critics' doubt about the alliance "shallow criticism," since "Nothing is more common than for persons opposed on all other subjects to coalesce." Meyer, on the weather saying, adds that "there is nothing to prevent us from supposing that Jesus may have used similar ... language on several occasions." This lesson does not decide between them; on either reading the words of 16:4 point back to 12:39.

**2. What was "the teaching of the Pharisees and Sadducees" (16:12)?** Matthew says the leaven is their "teaching" (Greek *didache*) and stops. Jesus "does not Himself interpret the parable," as Ellicott notes, and readers fill the word differently.

*Particular doctrines.* Some readers take "teaching" as specific positions. Meyer: "The allusion is to their peculiar sectarian views, in so far as they deviated from the law." Calvin, on 16:12: "the naked truth of God, and the inventions which men contrive out of their own brain, are unquestionably the two things that are contrasted." Matthew Poole lists the Pharisees' doctrine under "two heads" (justification by works, and "The obligation of the tradition of the elders"), and the Sadducees' from Acts 23:8. The Scofield Reference Notes (1917) define each: "The leaven of the Pharisees was externalism in religion," that "of the Sadducees, scepticism as to the supernatural and as to the Scriptures."

*A spirit or way of life.* Others take "teaching" in a wider sense. Ellicott: "not so much the formulated dogmas of the sect as its general drift and tendency. The leaven was (as expressly stated in Luke 12:1) 'hypocrisy,'" and that of the Sadducees "the more open form of worldliness" joined to their denial of the resurrection. The Expositor's Greek Testament: "The dogmas and opinions of the two parties in question were not the worst of them, but the spirit of their life." Bengel: "The word doctrine, in opposition to bread, is taken in a wide signification, so as to mean even hypocrisy," and in his later notes, "Of which a specimen occurs in Matthew 16:1," that is, the demand for a sign itself.

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the doctrines reading, the Pulpit Commentary points to Matthew 23:2-3, where Jesus "makes a distinction between what these teachers taught ex cathedra, and what they put forth on their own authority or what they practised themselves"; the BSB there reads, "So practice and observe everything they tell you. But do not do what they do." Against the spirit reading, Meyer notes that Luke's "hypocrisy" is a different use: "The expression is explained differently in Luke 12:1." Matthew's own word in 16:12 is "teaching," and the Expositor's Greek Testament, which prefers the wider reading, grants that "doctrine" was "in a general way the import of the ζύμη." This lesson does not choose.

**A misuse to name plainly.** Matthew's warning names two parties of Jesus' own day, which, as Josephus and Acts show, disagreed with each other on central questions. It has often been widened. Three moves can be seen in commentators read for this lesson. First, from two parties to a whole people: Calvin glosses 16:4 as meaning "that the Jews are never satisfied with any signs," though the verse speaks of the askers' "generation," and Jesus, His disciples and the askers were all Jews. Second, from the two parties' teaching to Jewish observance as such: Chrysostom says the rebuke "led them away from the Jewish observances," though 16:12 names the teaching of two parties, not the Law or the practice of Jews generally. Third, from first-century parties to later controversies: Poole and Gill describe the Pharisees' teaching in the categories of their own Protestant theology ("justification by the works of the law"), and Calvin turns the same leaven against "the inventions of the Pope." The third is a Christian argument with other Christians, but it fixes a picture of Pharisees drawn from later disputes.

Answers to this widening have come from a Jewish reference work and from the Catholic Church. Kohler holds that "the hostile attitude taken toward the Pharisaic schools by Pauline Christianity" shaped the Gospels and created "a false impression, which still prevails in Christian circles and among all Christian writers, ... concerning the Pharisees." He also points out that "Otherwise the Pharisees appear as friends of Jesus (Luke vii. 37, xiii. 31)," and that Jesus "tells his disciples to do as the Scribes and 'Pharisees who sit on Moses' seat ... bid them do'" (23:2-3). The Holy See's Commission for Religious Relations with the Jews, in its 1985 *Notes on the Correct Way to Present the Jews and Judaism in Preaching and Catechesis*, says: "An exclusively negative picture of the Pharisees is likely to be inaccurate and unjust," that "'Phariseeism' in the pejorative sense can be rife in any religion," and that "if Jesus shows himself severe towards the Pharisees, it is because he is closer to them than to other contemporary Jewish groups." It lists doctrines Jesus "shares, with the majority of Palestinian Jews of that time," including "the resurrection of the body." Several things in Matthew can be checked directly: 16:12 names "the Pharisees and Sadducees," two groups, not a people or a religion; Matthew's Jesus tells "the crowds" and "His disciples" (23:1) to "practice and observe everything they tell you" (23:3); and the Sadducees are those "who say there is no resurrection" (22:23), a belief the 1985 *Notes* count among those Jesus shared with the Pharisees. Whatever "leaven" names, this lesson reports only what its named readers have said, and none of it is a verdict on Jewish teaching or on Jewish people.

**3. Leaven for the kingdom (13:33), leaven as a danger (16:6): how do readers fit them together?** In Matthew's BSB the word "leaven" occurs only at 13:33 and in 16:6, 11 and 12 (a script search). At 13:33 "The kingdom of heaven is like leaven"; here it is something to "beware of." Lesson 28 (Matthew 13:24-43) reports the dispute over 13:33 itself; this lesson reports how readers relate the two passages.

*The same image, used two ways.* Calvin, on 16:12: "In a former passage, (Matthew 13:33,) Christ had used the word in a good sense, when he said that the Gospel resembled leaven; but for the most part this word is employed in Scripture to denote some foreign substance, by which the native purity of any thing is impaired." Meyer on 16:6: "Used differently again in Matthew 13:33." Poole on 16:12 sees one property in both: leaven "doth diffuse its quality into the whole mass," and so "Our Saviour had upon this account compared the gospel to leaven, Matthew 13:33." The Expositor's Greek Testament calls leaven here "an evil influence, working, however, after the same manner as the leaven in the parable," and on 13:33 says that "In Rabbinical theology leaven was used as an emblem of evil desire," while "Jesus had the courage to use it as an emblem of the best thing in the world." The rabbinic use can be read directly: in a prayer recorded in the Talmud, a sage says that "our will is to perform Your will, and what prevents us?" and answers, "the yeast in the dough" (Berakhot 17a, Koren-Steinsaltz; the edition's gloss explains it as "the evil inclination that is within every person").

*One meaning throughout.* The Scofield Reference Notes read 13:33 by 16:11-12. Leaven "is the principle of corruption working subtly; is invariably used in a bad sense ... and is defined by our Lord as evil doctrine," and on this reading 13:33 is "a warning that the true doctrine ... would be mingled with corrupt and corrupting false doctrine." In the same notes, "Leaven, as a symbolic or typical substance, is always mentioned in the O.T. in an evil sense," and in the New Testament "it is evil doctrine Mt 16:12 in its three-fold form of Pharisasism, Sadduceeism, Herodianism."

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the first reading, the Scofield notes say that taking leaven as good at 13:33 "does violence to the unvarying symbolical meaning of leaven, and especially to the meaning fixed by our Lord Himself," citing 16:6-12. Against the second, Ellicott on 13:33 points to "the leavened loaves that were offered on the day of Pentecost (Leviticus 23:17)," which the BSB says are "baked with leaven, as the firstfruits to the LORD," and Bengel's later notes reply that "No necessity, in fact, compels us to take the leaven in a bad sense: hence, as the word does not necessarily imply censure, bad leaven is termed the old leaven in 1 Corinthians 5:7." A reader can also check Leviticus 7:13, where a thanksgiving offering includes "cakes of leavened bread" (BSB), and weigh whether Scofield's qualifier, "as a symbolic or typical substance," sets such offerings aside. This lesson does not decide what "like leaven" carries at 13:33; the warning of 16:6 is negative on every reading.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, a model of the shape and not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 16:8-9: "Aware of their conversation, Jesus said, 'You of little faith, why are you debating among yourselves about having no bread? Do you still not understand? Do you not remember the five loaves for the five thousand, and how many basketfuls you gathered?'" (BSB)

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the disciples worry about bread right after two feedings they took part in; Jesus answers their worry with questions about memory ("Do you not remember"), and only then repeats the warning (16:11). Meyer: Jesus "simply makes use of it in the way of suggesting deeper reflection." Inference (this lesson's): for these disciples, forgetting what they had just seen made them misread what He was saying now.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Remembering what one has already seen can steady present worry and clear the way to hear what is actually being said.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the habit of recalling specific past events before reacting to a present lack. Not carried forward: a promise that any reader's bread will be multiplied, or a rebuke of ordinary planning. The passage records Jesus' questions to His disciples on one journey; it does not tell anyone not to buy food.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a rebuke and a set of questions addressed to disciples, functioning as a call to understand, not a command. Promise scope: it promises nothing about future supply. It shows Jesus pointing these disciples to two meals they had witnessed.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work and household money. A woman who runs a small cleaning business loses a large client. For a week she lies awake running the numbers, and when a friend at church says something about "trusting God," she hears only a lecture about her budget.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: remembering, then hearing, with prudence kept. One faithful response: she writes down, specifically, earlier lean months and how they were met; she goes back to her friend and asks what was meant; and she takes the practical steps the situation calls for, such as calling past clients and reviewing her costs.

**Cautions.** Real financial hardship is not a failure of memory or faith, and this passage is not a reason to skip practical help. If debt or loss of income is becoming unmanageable, a nonprofit credit counselor, a local social-services office or a trusted pastor can help in ways a study note cannot. A second caution: "Beware of the leaven" (16:6) names two parties of Jesus' day. It is not a license for any leader to forbid members to read, listen to or speak with others; if a group is using it that way, a pastor or counselor outside that group can help. This bridge is a worked example for the learner to test, not counsel for any particular person.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose.

1. Matthew 16:4 repeats 12:39 but does not explain the sign of Jonah, while 12:40 does. If you had only chapter 16, what would "the sign of Jonah" mean to you? What does the repetition add?
2. Some of the oldest manuscripts lack 16:2b-3, and the app's BSB prints it with no mark. What should a reader do with a saying whose place in the text is uncertain? Does Luke 12:54-56 change your answer?
3. Matthew calls the leaven "teaching" (16:12); Luke calls it "hypocrisy" (Luke 12:1). Are these the same warning in two words, or two warnings? What would decide it?
4. The same Gospel compares the kingdom to leaven and warns against leaven. Does a figure carry a fixed meaning across a book, or does each use take its meaning from its sentence?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 16:1-12 in order, from the request to "Then they understood." (b) Say how 16:4 differs from 12:39 in the BSB. (c) Name the two Greek words for "basket" and which feeding each belongs to.

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) the request and the weather saying, with the manuscript question; (1:00-2:00) 16:4 beside 12:39; (2:00-3:00) who the Pharisees and Sadducees were, in Josephus and Kohler; (3:00-4:00) the bread, the baskets and Positions 2, including the misuse; (4:00-5:00) Positions 3, ending on an open question.

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own and answer it. Form your own first. If you are stuck, here is one: "The warning in 16:6 is about all Jewish teaching, since the Pharisees were Judaism's teachers." (Check: whom does 16:12 name; what do Josephus and Acts 23:8 say about how the two parties differed; and what does 23:3 tell the crowds to do?) A second runs the other way: "Since Matthew 23:3 says to 'practice and observe everything they tell you,' 16:12 cannot be about doctrine at all." (Check: what do Meyer and Calvin say the teaching was, and what does 23:3 itself go on to say?)

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 16:1-12 does not say what "the teaching of the Pharisees and Sadducees" consisted of; it says only that the leaven was their teaching (16:12). (b) The passage passes no judgment on Jewish teaching or Jewish people as a whole; it names two parties (16:6, 11, 12). (c) The passage does not settle whether 16:2b-3 belongs to Matthew's original text; the BSB's own footnote says several manuscripts lack it.

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words in both texts:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matthew 12:39 | motif | strong | The same sixteen Greek words in every edition read; "wicked and adulterous" occurs only in these two verses of the BSB |
| Mark 8:11-21 | parallel | strong | The same two scenes in the same order, with Mark's "no sign," "one loaf" and "leaven of Herod," and no explanation at the end |
| Matthew 13:33 | motif | strong | "Leaven" occurs in Matthew only at 13:33 and 16:6-12; the row grades the recurrence, not the meaning |
| Exodus 12:15-20 | motif | plausible | The Septuagint's *zyme* at 12:15 and 12:19; commentators trace leaven's negative sense to the Passover rules, but Matthew 16 cites no Passover text |
| Matthew 14:17-20 | explicit_reference | explicit | Jesus names "the five loaves for the five thousand"; *kophinos* in both 14:20 and 16:9 |

The Exodus 12 row is this lesson's Old Testament thread, and it is only plausible: Matthew never mentions Passover here. Defend one row in your own words, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`. Two candidates were considered and not authored: Luke 12:54-56, a similar weather saying in another setting (a `parallel` at `plausible` at most, since 16:2-3 is itself uncertain), and 1 Corinthians 5:6-8, which shares the Passover image of leaven but not a saying.

## Sources and Limits

- **BSB.** Every BSB quotation in this lesson and its five connection files was printed by script from `web/public/bible/BSB/*.json` before drafting and re-checked afterward as a literal substring. Every count and "only" claim came from a case-insensitive script over the same files and counts English words, not Greek. The BSB footnote to 16:3 was read on Bible Hub's BSB page; the shipped JSON carries no footnotes.
- **Greek.** Edition claims rest on Bible Hub's parallel pages (Matthew 16:1-6, 16:9-12, 12:39, 13:33, 14:20, 15:37; Mark 8:15, 8:19-20) and Swete's Septuagint of Exodus 12, saved and read line by line. The 12:39/16:4 comparison was made by script. None is a critical apparatus; the manuscript evidence for 16:2b-3 and "Hypocrites" is known here only through Ellicott, the note in Meyer and the Pulpit Commentary.
- **Read directly, with raw copies saved:** Bible Hub's commentary pages on Matthew 16:1-6, 16:9, 16:12, 15:37 and 13:33 (public-domain commentaries, not checked against printed editions); Calvin's *Harmony* on Matthew 16 and the Scofield notes on Matthew 13 (Bible Hub); Chrysostom's Homily 53 (New Advent); Josephus, *Antiquities* 13.10.6 and 18.1.2-4 (Whiston); Kohler's "Sadducees" and "Pharisees" (*The Jewish Encyclopedia*); Berakhot 17a (Sefaria); Strauss, *Life of Jesus*, §§79 and 85 and a note to §102; the 1985 Vatican *Notes* (Boston College's reproduction).
- **Secondhand only:** Theophylact (through Meyer; the rendering is this lesson's); Schneckenburger (through Strauss); Hilary and Westcott (through the Expositor's Greek Testament); the critics Meyer and the Pulpit Commentary answer.
- **Limits on the two parties.** Josephus wrote for Roman readers, as Kohler notes; Kohler's articles are over a century old and argue positions of their own; and almost everything known about the Sadducees comes from their opponents. No modern historical study of either party was read.
- **Hostile language.** One derogatory line (Calvin on 16:4) is quoted, as an example of the misuse; harsher lines in Poole, Gill and Calvin are paraphrased or omitted.
- **Not covered:** no Eastern Orthodox source beyond Chrysostom and no Lutheran source beyond Meyer was located on 16:1-12; that is a sourcing gap, not a claim that those traditions are silent. Modern critical commentaries on Matthew were not consulted.
