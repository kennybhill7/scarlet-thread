Matthew 8:1-17 is the first narrative after the Sermon on the Mount, and it opens on a hinge the text marks itself: "When Jesus came down from the mountain, large crowds followed Him" (8:1, BSB). Three healings follow, each told briefly -- a man with leprosy (8:2-4), a centurion's servant (8:5-13), and Peter's mother-in-law (8:14-15) -- then an evening summary of many more (8:16), and Matthew's own comment on all of it: "This was to fulfill what was spoken through the prophet Isaiah: 'He took on our infirmities and carried our diseases'" (8:17, BSB). That last verse is the center of this lesson for two reasons. It is one of Matthew's explicit fulfillment quotations, so it can be checked word by word against Isaiah 53:4 -- a near-certain strand of the scarlet thread, since Matthew names the prophet himself. And it is the verse on which a real, high-stakes dispute rests: whether physical healing is provided for "in the atonement," and whether it is promised now. That question touches people who are sick right now, so this lesson reports it in the traditions' own words, does not decide it, and says plainly: nothing in this lesson is medical guidance.

## Context

**What the text says, in order.** *The leper (8:2-4).* "Suddenly a leper came and knelt before Him, saying, 'Lord, if You are willing, You can make me clean'" (8:2, BSB). Jesus "reached out His hand and touched the man. 'I am willing,' He said. 'Be clean!' And immediately his leprosy was cleansed" (8:3, BSB). Then a double instruction: "See that you don't tell anyone. But go, show yourself to the priest and offer the gift prescribed by Moses, as a testimony to them" (8:4, BSB). *The centurion (8:5-13).* At Capernaum "a centurion came and pleaded with Him, 'Lord, my servant lies at home, paralyzed and in terrible agony'" (8:5-6, BSB). Jesus offers to come; the centurion declines: "Lord, I am not worthy to have You come under my roof. But just say the word, and my servant will be healed" (8:8, BSB), explaining from his own chain of command (8:9). Jesus "marveled" and says, "Truly I tell you, I have not found anyone in Israel with such great faith" (8:10, BSB), adds a saying about "many" who "will come from the east and the west" (8:11-12), and dismisses the centurion: "Go! As you have believed, so will it be done for you." The servant "was healed at that very hour" (8:13, BSB). *Peter's mother-in-law (8:14-15).* Jesus "saw Peter's mother-in-law sick in bed with a fever. So He touched her hand, and the fever left her, and she got up and began to serve Him" (8:14-15, BSB). *The evening (8:16-17).* "When evening came, many who were demon-possessed were brought to Jesus, and He drove out the spirits with a word and healed all the sick" (8:16, BSB), followed by the Isaiah quotation.

**"Leprosy" is not the modern disease of that name.** The Wikipedia article on *tzaraath* used here (a tertiary source) reports that the Hebrew term is "frequently translated as leprosy (though it is not Hansen's disease, the disease known as 'leprosy' in modern times)," and covers various "disfigurative conditions of the human skin, clothing, and houses." This app's own BSB of Leviticus 13-14 reflects that, using "skin disease" (Leviticus 13:2; 14:2) where Matthew's BSB says "leper." The law also set the person apart from the community: "He must live alone in a place outside the camp" (Leviticus 13:46, BSB).

**"The gift prescribed by Moses" is Leviticus 14.** Matthew names Moses, not the chapter, but the law in question is "the law for the one afflicted with a skin disease on the day of his cleansing, when he is brought to the priest" (Leviticus 14:2, BSB). Ellicott, Barnes and Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (public-domain commentaries, via Bible Hub) all identify Leviticus 14 as the referent; the Wikipedia article on Matthew 8:4 reports Davies and Allison noting that the priest's visit was what readmitted a cleansed person to society. A wording note against common memory: the phrase "present the offering Moses prescribed" is this app's BSB wording of Mark 1:44 and Luke 5:14, not of Matthew 8:4, which reads "offer the gift prescribed by Moses." The same Wikipedia article also notes a tension worth seeing for yourself: 8:1 has "large crowds" following Jesus, yet 8:4 tells the man to tell no one.

**Two ways English Bibles punctuate 8:7.** Translations differ on whether 8:7 is a statement or a question: this app's BSB reads "I will go and heal him," a statement, while Bible Hub's parallel page shows the NIV's "Shall I come and heal him?" and the CSB's "Am I to come and heal him?" as questions. This lesson did not find a manuscript variant behind the difference; it is a punctuation choice translators make, it affects how the exchange with a Gentile officer reads, and it is left to Questions to Carry rather than decided here.

**Matthew's centurion and Luke's are told differently.** Luke 7:1-10 tells the same healing (see the connection to Luke 7 below), with differences checkable in this app's BSB. In Matthew "a centurion came and pleaded with Him" (8:5); in Luke "he sent some Jewish elders to ask Him to come" (7:3), and later "the centurion sent friends with the message" (7:6). Luke's elders tell Jesus "This man is worthy... for he loves our nation and has built our synagogue" (7:4-5), while the centurion's own message, in both Gospels, is "I am not worthy" (Matthew 8:8; Luke 7:6). Matthew's servant is "paralyzed and in terrible agony" (8:6); Luke's is "sick and about to die" (7:2). Neither Gospel says whose army the centurion served in; the Wikipedia article on the miracle reports I. Howard Marshall's view that "there were no Roman forces in Galilee prior to AD 44," so that he was "probably a member of Herod Antipas's troops," while adding that "he is clearly a gentile." Only Matthew has the east-and-west saying (8:11-12) and Jesus' direct word to the centurion (8:13); in Luke "the messengers returned to the house" and "found the servant in good health" (7:10). Two named harmonizations exist. Augustine, read directly for this lesson (*The Harmony of the Gospels* 2.20), holds that the centurion made his approach through others, and that it is "nothing out of the way" for Matthew "to have chosen to express the matter in this compendious method, There came a centurion to Him." The Wikipedia article on this miracle reports that John Chrysostom, Theophylact and Euthymius instead hold that the events in Luke came first "and then last of all the centurion came to Christ" in person. The same article reports Craig Keener's suggestion that Matthew and Luke drew on a shared source. This lesson reports these readings without choosing one.

**What Isaiah 53:4 says, in three forms.** This is the thread connection at the heart of the passage, so it is worth seeing exactly.
- *This app's BSB.* Isaiah 53:4: "Surely He took on our infirmities and carried our sorrows; yet we considered Him stricken by God, struck down and afflicted." Matthew 8:17: "He took on our infirmities and carried our diseases." The first clause is identical; the second differs ("sorrows" against "diseases"); Matthew leaves out "Surely" and the second half of the verse.
- *The Hebrew.* Bible Hub's interlinear for Isaiah 53:4 glosses the two nouns as *choli*, "our sicknesses," and *makob*, "our sorrows." Anthony Palma (an Assemblies of God scholar, read directly) argues from other uses that *choli* "clearly means sickness or disease" and that *makob* "is used for physical pain." Graham Hill, by contrast, holds that "Isaiah 53:4 is readily understood in metaphorical terms, as the next two verses clearly deal with sin, not sickness" (see Positions).
- *The Septuagint.* The older Greek translation, checked directly on Blue Letter Bible's text, reads *houtos tas hamartias hēmōn pherei* -- roughly, *this one bears our sins*, followed by *and is pained for us*.
- *Matthew's Greek.* Nestle 1904, checked on Bible Hub: *Autos tas astheneias hēmōn elaben kai tas nosous ebastasen* -- roughly, *He took our infirmities and bore our diseases*.

So Matthew's nouns line up with the Hebrew's sickness-and-pain words, not with the Septuagint's "sins." The commentators agree on this point, whatever else they disagree about: Barnes calls Matthew's Greek "an exact translation of the Hebrew"; Meyer says "The passage is quoted from the original (Hebrew) text"; the Expositor's Greek Testament contrasts the Septuagint, "making the text (Isaiah 53:4) refer to sin," with "The Hebrew refers to sicknesses and pains"; Palma calls the Septuagint's *hamartias* a rendering "for which there is no justification." That is a checkable fact about wording. It does not, by itself, settle what Matthew meant by "fulfill" here -- the question the Positions section takes up.

**Isaiah 53 has its own history of interpretation.** Matthew applies the Servant passage to Jesus, and that application is explicit in his text. It is also worth knowing, as a matter of cited fact, that the passage is read differently elsewhere. The Wikipedia article on Isaiah 53 used here (a tertiary source) reports individual, righteous-remnant and national readings of the Servant, and states that the national reading -- the Servant as "a metaphor for the entire nation of Israel" -- "became the majority position within Judaism from the medieval period until today," with Rashi, Ibn Ezra and Radak as representatives. A learner tracing the thread should know that Matthew's reading of Isaiah is a claim the New Testament makes, not the only way the chapter has been read.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 8:1-17 is built. These are observations a reader can check; what Matthew meant by the arrangement is not something this lesson decides.

**Three petitioners, three different ways of asking.** The leper asks for himself ("Lord, if You are willing," 8:2). The centurion asks for someone else ("my servant lies at home," 8:6). Peter's mother-in-law does not ask at all; in Matthew, Jesus "saw" her (8:14). That last detail is Matthew's own: in Mark, "they promptly told Jesus about her" (Mark 1:30, BSB), and in Luke, "they appealed to Jesus on her behalf" (Luke 4:38, BSB). Matthew also has her serve "Him" (8:15), where Mark 1:31 and Luke 4:39 both say "them." These are this lesson's own observations, checked against the BSB of all three Gospels, not findings attributed to a named scholar.

**Touch, word, touch, word.** Jesus touches the leper (8:3), heals the centurion's servant by a word from a distance (8:8, 8:13), touches the mother-in-law's hand (8:15), and drives out spirits "with a word" (8:16). The pattern is in the text; whether Matthew intended it as a pattern is not something the text says.

**A frame around the Sermon and these miracles.** Matthew 4:23 and 9:35 are nearly identical summaries in this app's BSB: "teaching in their synagogues, preaching the gospel of the kingdom, and healing every disease and sickness" (4:23 adds "among the people"). Between them sit the Sermon (chapters 5-7) and a run of miracle stories that begins here. This lesson notes the repeated wording as a checkable fact; it does not claim to know the author's design from it.

## Positions

Two disputes attach to this passage. The first carries real, present-day stakes for people who are sick and is given the fuller treatment. The second is narrower and reported briefly.

**Is physical healing "in the atonement" -- and if so, is it promised now?** Everyone below agrees that Matthew applies Isaiah 53:4 to Jesus' healings. They disagree about what follows: whether Isaiah 53 ties bodily healing to Jesus' death, and whether that healing can be claimed in this life. Four positions are reported here, each from its own published words; grouping them into these four is this lesson's own construction, not a classification any of the sources uses. If you or someone you love is ill, please read the note at the end of this section first; nothing here is medical guidance.

*Word of Faith teaching (Kenneth Hagin; Kenneth Copeland).* Kenneth Hagin's article "The Double Cure" (read directly on his ministry's site) states, "Our redemption in Christ is twofold. Jesus not only died for our sins on the Cross of Calvary, His blood was also shed so you and I might live free from sickness and disease." He gives a pastoral reason for the teaching: "Satan is the author of sickness, not God," and he recalls that when he lay sick "as a 15 and a 16-year-old Baptist boy," people told him God was "using sickness to teach them something" -- "I didn't learn anything. Instead, I became bitter and almost turned against God." He reads Isaiah 53:4 in parallel with 53:6: "If you believe that God laid your iniquities on Jesus for your salvation, then you should also be able to believe that God laid sickness and disease on Him so you could be healed." On symptoms that return after prayer, he writes that people "wavered in faith and believed the symptom over the truth of God's Word," and he tells readers to "quote First Peter 2:24, Matthew 8:17, and Isaiah 53:4–5." Kenneth Copeland, as quoted by Walt Russell in the *Christian Research Journal* (2004; Russell writes as a critic of the movement, and Copeland's own page was not independently located), cites this verse directly, in the King James wording: "According to Matthew 8:17, Jesus '… took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses.' He bore them in His own body so that you and I wouldn't have to." On 1 Peter 2:24 he writes: "By those stripes, you were healed — past tense. You were healed 2,000 years ago, but you have to receive it by faith today." The Wikipedia article on the movement (a tertiary source) summarizes it as teaching that "complete healing (of spirit, soul, and body) is included in Christ's atonement and therefore is available immediately to all who believe."

*Classical Pentecostal teaching (the Assemblies of God).* The Assemblies of God's Statement of Fundamental Truths, section 12, quoted in its 2010 position paper "Divine Healing" (read directly), states: "Divine healing is an integral part of the gospel. Deliverance from sickness is provided for in the Atonement, and is the privilege of all believers (Isaiah 53:4,5; Matthew 8:16,17; James 5:14–16)." The same paper sets this inside an "already and not yet" frame: "In this period of the 'already and not yet' some are healed instantly, some gradually, and others are not healed." It also says, "In humility we confess that we do not understand all that pertains to divine healing," and "We do not understand fully why some are healed and others are not," and "Belief in divine healing neither opposes nor competes with medical doctors." On 1 Peter it says that "The Isaiah context and the reference to it in 1 Peter 2:24,25 emphasize especially the healing or restoration from sin," while arguing from Isaiah 53:4's wording that the gospel includes healing "from both the spiritual and physical effects of the fall." The paper also says, without naming anyone, that "Excessive claims and unfounded judgments are offered by some." The Assemblies of God and Word of Faith positions should not be merged: the Wikipedia article on Word of Faith (a tertiary source) reports that Hagin's path ultimately "led to Hagin leaving the Assemblies of God." Anthony Palma, in an article published on the denomination's site, calls Matthew's use of Isaiah "proleptic (anticipatory) in nature," writing that "we have in Matthew 8 an anticipation of His death and its benefits," and cautions: "We ought, therefore, to be cautious about trying to formulate a detailed theology of 'healing in the Atonement.' Yet we need to be convinced that divine healing is indeed mediated to us through the Cross."

*Commentators who place full healing at the resurrection (Carson, Moo, Blomberg, Hill, Russell).* Graham Hill, in the *Pacific Journal of Baptist Research* (2013, read directly), lists four readings of Matthew 8:17 and quotes several commentators this lesson did not read in their own books. He quotes D.A. Carson (*Expositor's Bible Commentary*, 1995) saying Matthew 8:17 "cannot be used to justify healing on demand. This text and others clearly teach that there is healing in the Atonement; but similarly there is the promise of a resurrection body in the Atonement, even if believers do not inherit it until the Parousia." He quotes Douglas Moo (*Trinity Journal*, 1988): "The atoning death of Christ provides for the healing of all our diseases—but nothing in Matthew or in the NT implies that this healing will take place in this life." Hill's own conclusion is that "our interpretation" of these passages "may include physical healing, but does not guarantee it," because "thanks to the already but not yet nature of the kingdom, it is not available to all in this present life." Hill also says why he took up the question: the church he attended as a young man "taught that physical healing is guaranteed in the atonement, and that one only needs enough faith to be healed," and in his own account two church leaders he knew, who "were absolutely certain that God would heal them from their cancer in this life," died in the same year. Walt Russell, reading 1 Peter 2:24 in its paragraph (servants under unjust masters), concludes that Peter's application "is one of spiritual healing in the present time," and that the passage "offers a firm expectation of perfect physical health in the immortality of the resurrection, but it provides no basis for claiming such perfect health in the mortality of this present world."

*Calvin, and an older line of reading.* John Calvin's commentary on 8:17 (read directly, public-domain translation) begins by stating the problem: "This prediction has the appearance of being inappropriate," because "Isaiah does not there speak of miracles, but of the death of Christ, — and not of temporal benefits, but of spiritual and eternal grace." His answer is that Matthew states "the purpose for which he healed their diseases": the healings point beyond themselves, "for it would be idle to confine our view to a transitory advantage, as if the Son of God were a physician of bodies." The same tension was noticed much earlier. The Wikipedia article on Matthew 8:17 (reporting the *Catena Aurea*) quotes Chrysostom: "The Prophet seems to have meant this of sins; how then does the Evangelist explain it of bodily diseases?"

These four are not four labels for one view. All of them read Matthew as applying Isaiah 53:4 to Jesus' healings, and none of the modern sources quoted here denies that God can heal people now. They differ on two things that matter for real people. First, how Isaiah 53 joins healing to Jesus' death: as a "double cure" of sin and sickness (Hagin), as a provision of the atonement experienced now in part (the Assemblies of God, Palma), as a provision fully received at the resurrection (Carson, Moo, Hill), or as healings that signify a deeper salvation (Calvin). Second, what a sick believer may expect now: healing available to faith today (Hagin, Copeland), with Hagin tying returning symptoms to wavering faith; healing as a real but not universal grace, where the Assemblies of God paper says "We do not understand fully why some are healed and others are not"; or healing as something God may give but cannot be demanded "this side of eternity" (Blomberg, as Hill quotes him). This lesson reports each in its own words and does not decide among them.

**Please read this if you or someone you love is ill.** This lesson is a study of how a text has been read. It is not medical guidance, and nothing in it is a reason to delay, refuse, or stop medical care. A person facing illness needs a doctor and a pastor who knows them, not a study note. Some readers have been told that their illness continues because their faith is too weak. Whatever you conclude about Isaiah 53, that is not a burden to carry alone: talk to a pastor you trust, and keep talking to your doctor.

**Who are the "many" from the east and the west (8:11), and who are "the sons of the kingdom" (8:12)?** A narrower dispute. Several classic commentators read the "many" as Gentiles: Meyer glosses "from the east and west" as "from the most widely separated quarters of the world Gentiles," and Bengel calls the phrase "an euphemism for 'from the Gentiles'" (both via Bible Hub). The Wikipedia article on Matthew 8:11 reports Davies and Allison leaving the question open: "The verse is not clear if these people from the east and west are Gentiles... There were many Jews in lands to the east and west and the verse could be referring to them returning to Israel." On 8:12, the Wikipedia article on that verse reports that "sons of the kingdom" was "commonly read to imply that many Jews would be excluded from salvation," and that Davies and Allison "argue against this," noting the text "can be equally read as all or some." Because the verse has so often been read as a verdict on Jewish people as a whole, a learner's reading of it should be held with care; this lesson reports both sides and does not settle it.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key; the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step. It deliberately does not use a healing, so that no one reads it as a promise about illness.

**Passage element.** Matthew 8:8-9: "Lord, I am not worthy to have You come under my roof. But just say the word, and my servant will be healed. For I myself am a man under authority, with soldiers under me. I tell one to go, and he goes; and another to come, and he comes" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: a man with real rank asks on behalf of someone under him, declines to have Jesus come to his house, and explains his request from his own experience of giving and taking orders. Luke's version adds that local elders called him "worthy" (Luke 7:4) while he called himself "not worthy" (Luke 7:6). Inference: for a first-century hearer, a Gentile officer (whether in Roman service or, as I. Howard Marshall is reported to hold, in Herod Antipas's forces; see Context) using his authority to plead for a servant, and refusing to presume on a Jewish teacher's hospitality, would have been striking. That step goes beyond the text's wording and is this lesson's own.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Someone with authority can use it to advocate for a person under them, and can ask for help without presuming on the person they are asking.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: advocacy for someone under one's care, and asking without presuming. Not carried forward: the military setting, the healing itself, or any claim about what result advocacy will bring.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: an example drawn by analogy from a narrative character's conduct, not a command. Promise scope: none. Jesus' words "As you have believed, so will it be done for you" (8:13, BSB) were spoken to this centurion about this servant; this lesson does not read them as a promise that any request made with faith will be granted in a given form or time, and the Positions section above shows how much is disputed on exactly that point.

**Modern domain and situation.** Workplace. A shift supervisor learns that a newer employee is struggling with a schedule that conflicts with caring for a family member. The supervisor has no power to change the schedule but knows the regional manager who does.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: advocacy on someone else's behalf, made without demanding or presuming. One faithful response: the supervisor brings the request to the manager directly, explains the employee's situation plainly, and asks rather than insists -- spending some of their own standing for someone who cannot easily speak for themselves.

**Cautions.** This does not mean every request should be pushed regardless of an organization's legitimate limits, or that advocating for someone guarantees the outcome. It does not fit a case involving harassment, discrimination, or a safety issue; those need HR, a formal process, or another qualified person. The passage gives one person's example; the bridge is an analogy for the learner to test.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Matthew says Isaiah 53:4 is fulfilled in healings that happen before Jesus' death, while Isaiah 53:5-6 and 1 Peter 2:24 speak of sins and of a death. Palma calls Matthew's use "proleptic"; Calvin reads the healings as pointing to their purpose; Moo and Carson place the full benefit at the resurrection. What would you need to check about how Matthew uses "fulfill" elsewhere (for example 1:22, 2:15, 4:14, 12:17) to judge which of these accounts of "fulfill" fits his habits best?
2. In Matthew the centurion "came" himself; in Luke he sends elders and then friends. Augustine reads Matthew as describing an approach made through others; Chrysostom, Theophylact and Euthymius (as reported) read both accounts as happening in sequence. What would you need to know about how ancient writers reported speech sent through messengers before choosing between them?
3. Matthew 8:11 names "the east and the west"; Luke 13:29 says "from east and west and north and south," the same four directions in the same order as Psalm 107:3. What would it take to decide whether either Gospel is echoing a specific Old Testament text, rather than a common way of saying "everywhere"?
4. The BSB punctuates 8:7 as a statement ("I will go and heal him"); the NIV and CSB punctuate it as a question. Read 8:5-13 both ways. Does either reading change how you hear Jesus' response to a Gentile officer, and what in the context could settle it?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) name the three people healed in 8:1-15 in order, and say who asked in each case. (b) Recite Matthew 8:17 as the BSB gives it, and say which word differs from the BSB of Isaiah 53:4. (c) Say what the Septuagint has in Isaiah 53:4 where the Hebrew and Matthew have words for sickness.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the three healings and the evening summary, as the text tells them (Context); (1:00-2:00) the leper sent to the priest under Leviticus 14, and the wording difference from Mark and Luke (Context, connection); (2:00-3:00) Matthew's centurion against Luke's, stated as checkable differences, with the two named harmonizations (Context); (3:00-4:30) Isaiah 53:4 in the BSB, the Hebrew, the Septuagint and Matthew's Greek, then at least two of the four positions on healing and the atonement in their own words, without picking one, and the pastoral note (Positions); (4:30-5:00) one open question, stated on purpose as the last thing taught.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: *reporting four views on healing without choosing is cruel to a sick person who needs to know whether to expect healing.* A second candidate, running the other direction: *Matthew plainly says Isaiah 53:4 was fulfilled in physical healings; any reading that turns 8:17 mainly into a statement about sin, as Calvin's partly does, is reading against Matthew's own words.* How would Calvin's own explanation answer that?
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 8:17 says Jesus' healings that evening fulfilled Isaiah 53:4. It does not say, in its own words, whether every believer may expect healing now; that step belongs to the positions above, not to the verse. (b) Matthew 8:13 records Jesus' words to one centurion about one servant. It does not, by itself, state a rule that healing follows faith in every case, or that illness which continues shows a lack of faith; neither claim is in the text.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words in both texts:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Isaiah 53:4 | quotation | explicit | Matthew's own fulfillment formula names Isaiah; first clause identical in BSB, "sorrows" against "diseases" in the second, and Matthew follows the Hebrew rather than the Septuagint's "sins" |
| Leviticus 14 | explicit_reference | explicit | "The gift prescribed by Moses" (8:4) can only be the cleansing offerings of Leviticus 14, though Matthew names Moses, not the chapter |
| Luke 7:1-10 | parallel | strong | The centurion's speech is nearly identical, but Matthew has him come in person while Luke has him send elders and friends |
| Isaiah 25:6-8 | allusion | plausible | Shares the banquet image (named by the Pulpit Commentary) but no wording; "east and west" points to other texts |
| 1 Peter 2:24-25 | doctrinal_synthesis | strong | Both apply Isaiah 53 to Jesus, but different verses for different ends; neither cites the other |

Or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`. Two real candidates this lesson declined: Isaiah 49:12 is often listed beside Matthew 8:11, but in this app's BSB it reads "from the north and from the west, and from the land of Aswan" -- no "east," and no banquet -- so the verbal link is weaker than its reputation. And Exodus 15:26 ("I am the LORD who heals you," BSB), which the Assemblies of God paper cites, shares a theme with this passage but no wording and no citation, so it was not authored as a connection. Say what would need to be true before either could be graded higher.
