Matthew 16:13-28 turns on two questions and two answers. Jesus asks, "Who do you say I am?" (16:15, BSB), and Simon Peter answers, "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God" (16:16). Jesus answers him in turn: "you are Peter, and on this rock I will build My church" (16:18). Gregg Allison, a Baptist theologian, writes that "Few verses have caused more controversy than Matthew 16:18." Catholics, Orthodox and Protestants read "this rock" and "the keys" differently, and the Church Fathers themselves read the verse more than one way. This lesson reports each tradition from its own published words and does not choose among them. It also checks what can be checked: the wordplay on Peter's name, the keys beside Isaiah 22, the legal language of binding and loosing, and the turn from blessing to rebuke (16:23). It reports the readings of the puzzle in 16:28 without settling it. Finally, the call to "deny himself and take up his cross" (16:24) has been used to press people to stay in abusive homes. That misuse is named in Positions, with a referral. Every BSB quotation below was checked by script against this app's own Bible files.

## Context

**What happens, in order.** At Caesarea Philippi, Jesus asks who people say "the Son of Man" is (16:13). The disciples report "John the Baptist," "Elijah," and "Jeremiah or one of the prophets" (16:14). Peter confesses Him as "the Christ, the Son of the living God" (16:16). Jesus blesses him, names him, and promises the church and the keys (16:17-19), then forbids the disciples "to tell anyone that He was the Christ" (16:20). "From that time on" Jesus begins to show that He "must be killed and on the third day be raised to life" (16:21). Peter rebukes Him and is rebuked in return (16:22-23). Jesus then speaks to all the disciples about the cross and the coming of the Son of Man (16:24-28).

**What only Matthew has.** Mark (8:27-9:1) and Luke (9:18-27) tell the same scene in the same order (see the connections below). Checked in this app's BSB, only Matthew has the blessing, the rock, the church and the keys (16:17-19), and only Matthew has "the Son of the living God" in Peter's confession (Mark: "You are the Christ," 8:29; Luke: "The Christ of God," 9:20). Only Matthew adds "You are a stumbling block to Me." The Catholic NABRE notes call Jesus' reply to Peter "drawn principally from material peculiar to Matthew."

**Peter's name: Petros, petra and Kepha.** In Greek, "you are Peter" is *su ei Petros*, and "on this rock" is *epi tautē tē petra*: a masculine noun, then a related feminine one. In Aramaic the name is *Kepha*. The BSB records it at John 1:42: "You will be called Cephas" (which is translated as Peter). Paul calls Peter "Cephas" in eight further verses (checked by script). Sources on every side report the wordplay and differ on what the two Greek forms signify.

- The NABRE notes say the Aramaic would have been "You are the Rock (Kēpā') and upon this rock (kēpā') I will build my church," and that the gender difference "may be due simply to the unsuitability of using a feminine noun as the proper name of a male." The two words, it adds, "were also used interchangeably with the same meaning, 'rock.'"
- Calvin finds "no difference of meaning" between the two words "except that the former belongs to the Attic, and the latter to the ordinary dialect," but holds that "the gender of the noun was intentionally changed, to show that he was now speaking of something different."
- Thayer's lexicon (via Bible Hub) lists 16:18 under "a man like a rock, by reason of his firmness and strength of soul," and gives the other views only in a parenthesis. Some treat the classical distinction between "the massive living rock" and "a detached but large fragment" as "important for the correct understanding of this passage." For "others," the genders are "due first to the personal then to the material reference."
- The Orthodox Church in America reads the rock as Peter's confession "despite the fact that Peter/rock is a play on the word for rock in Aramaic [cephas] and Greek [petros]."

This lesson does not decide what the wordplay settles.

**"Church."** "Church" occurs in the BSB Gospels only at 16:18 and 18:17 (twice), by script. Bible Hub's Greek concordance agrees for *ekklēsia* (its next occurrence is Acts 5:11), and so do the NABRE notes: the word "occurs in the gospels only here and in Mt 18:17 (twice)." "The gates of Hades" occurs only here in the BSB. The NABRE notes picture Hades as "a walled city whose gates will not close in upon the church."

**The keys and Isaiah 22.** "Keys" occurs twice in the BSB: here, and in Revelation 1:18, where the risen Christ says, "I hold the keys of Death and of Hades." Commentators across traditions set 16:19 beside Isaiah 22. There the LORD removes Shebna, "the steward in charge of the palace" (22:15), and gives his office to Eliakim: "I will place on his shoulder the key to the house of David. What he opens no one can shut, and what he shuts no one can open" (22:22, BSB). Revelation 3:7 applies those words to Christ, "who holds the key of David." In the two online Septuagint editions saved for this lesson, the key clause stands in eBible's Greek text but is missing from Swete's Septuagint as shown on Bible Hub, which reads only that he will rule, that none will speak against him, and that he will shut (*kai arxei, kai ouk estai ho antilegōn, kai kleisei*). The connection below grades this link and reports who names it.

**Binding and loosing.** Kaufmann Kohler's *Jewish Encyclopedia* article (1906) calls the phrase a "Rabbinical term for 'forbidding and permitting'" (Hebrew *asar we-hittir*): "The various schools had the power 'to bind and to loose'; that is, to forbid and to permit." He reads 16:19 and 18:18 as Jesus using "the familiar formula," and calls the forgiving and retaining of sins in John 20:23 "Quite different from this Judaic and ancient view." Ellicott's example: Shammai's school "bound" when it "forbade divorce on any but the one ground of adultery," and Hillel's "loosed." The NABRE notes name "the giving of authoritative teaching, and the lifting or imposing of the ban of excommunication." "Bound in heaven" and "loosed in heaven" occur in the BSB only at 16:19 and 18:18 (see the connection below). How binding relates to forgiving sins is part of the dispute in Positions.

**The first passion prediction.** "From that time on Jesus began" occurs in the BSB only at 4:17 and 16:21. The NABRE notes say Matthew's phrase marks "a new phase of the gospel." The prediction is the first of three that end with Jesus "raised to life" on "the third day" (16:21; 17:22-23; 20:18-19). Peter answers with a rebuke of his own: "Far be it from You, Lord! ... This shall never happen to You!" (16:22).

**"Get behind Me, Satan!" (16:23).** In the BSB, Jesus dismisses the tempter at 4:10 with "Away from Me, Satan!" and Peter with "Get behind Me, Satan!" The Greek differs too. At 16:23 every edition on Bible Hub reads *Hypage opisō mou, Satana*. At 4:10 the Nestle 1904, Westcott-Hort, Tischendorf and Textus Receptus editions read only *Hypage, Satana*, while the Byzantine Majority and Greek Orthodox Church texts add *opisō mou*, "behind me." The NABRE notes still hear 4:10 in 16:23. Chrysostom says Christ "said not, Satan spoke by you, but, Get behind me, Satan," because "it was a desire of the adversary that Christ should not suffer." "Stumbling block" in the singular occurs in the BSB Gospels only here; the plural occurs at 18:7 and Luke 17:1.

**The cross saying (16:24-25).** "If anyone wants to come after Me, he must deny himself and take up his cross and follow Me" (16:24, BSB). Its core repeats 10:38-39, a recurrence lesson 21 already traces (conn-matt10-cross-matt16, listed above, not duplicated). New here is "deny himself," found in the BSB only in this saying, its parallels (Mark 8:34; Luke 9:23), Isaiah 58:5 and 2 Timothy 2:13. The NABRE notes explain: "to deny oneself is to disown oneself as the center of one's existence." In Matthew the saying follows directly on the prediction that Jesus "must be killed" (16:21).

## Literary Design

Notes on how 16:13-28 is built, not on what the arrangement is meant to teach.

**Blessing, then rebuke.** Two questions open the scene, one about "people" (16:13) and one about "you" (16:15). Then Peter speaks twice, and Jesus answers twice. The first answer begins "Blessed are you" and gives the source of Peter's words: "this was not revealed to you by flesh and blood, but by My Father in heaven" (16:17). The second begins "Get behind Me, Satan!" and gives a source too: "you do not have in mind the things of God, but the things of men" (16:23). The two verses pair God against human beings. Ellicott sees a further contrast with the rock: "Peter is still a stone, but it is as 'a stone of stumbling and a rock of offence' (Isaiah 8:14; 1Peter 2:8)." That pairing is Ellicott's. The English "rock" (16:18) and "stumbling block" (16:23) do not share a word.

## Positions

Three disputes attach to this passage: what the rock and keys are, what 16:28 refers to, and a misuse of 16:24, named with a referral.

**What is "this rock," and what are "the keys" (16:18-19)?** Each tradition is reported in its own published words.

*The Fathers read the verse more than one way.* Late in life, Augustine reviewed his own books in the *Retractations* (Latin text, book 1, chapter 21.1; numbered 20.1 in the 1968 *Fathers of the Church* translation). He says he once wrote of Peter "that in him, as in a rock, the Church was founded," a sense "also sung by the mouths of many in the verses of most blessed Ambrose." Afterward, he writes, he "very often" explained the verse so that "upon this rock" meant "upon Him whom Peter confessed," with Peter, named from this rock, representing the Church, which "received the keys of the kingdom of heaven." His reason: "For it was not said to him, 'You are *petra*,' but 'You are *Petrus*.' But the rock was Christ." He ends: "Of these two opinions, let the reader choose which is the more probable" (the lesson's own translations of the Latin). Chrysostom glosses "upon this rock" as "on the faith of his confession" and says Christ "makes him a shepherd." Later in the homily, arguing the Son's equal dignity, he says that "to a mortal man He entrusted the authority over all things in Heaven, giving him the keys." Cyprian's *On the Unity of the Church*, chapter 4, survives in two forms, both printed by the Benedictine scholar John Chapman in the Catholic Encyclopedia (1908). The form "found in most manuscripts" says that "The other Apostles were indeed what Peter was, endowed with a like fellowship both of honour and of power, but the commencement proceeds from one." The other adds, "but the primacy is given to Peter," and asks, "He who deserts the chair of Peter, upon whom the Church is founded, is he confident that he is in the Church?" A combined version, Chapman writes, "has played a large part in controversy with Protestants" and is "spurious in this conflated form." Of the second form he reports that "it seems to be now generally admitted" that it is "an alteration made by the author himself," and that "The old contention that it is a Roman forgery is at all events quite out of the question." He adds that "The 'one chair' is always in Cyprian the episcopal chair" and that "The assertion of the equality of the Apostles as Apostles remains."

*Roman Catholic.* The Catechism teaches that "Simon Peter holds the first place in the college of the Twelve," and that "Christ, the 'living Stone', thus assures his Church, built on Peter, of victory over the powers of death. Because of the faith he confessed Peter will remain the unshakable rock of the Church" (§552). "The 'power of the keys' designates authority to govern the house of God, which is the Church," and binding and loosing "connotes the authority to absolve sins, to pronounce doctrinal judgments, and to make disciplinary decisions" (§553). "The Lord made Simon alone, whom he named Peter, the 'rock' of his Church," and "This pastoral office of Peter and the other apostles belongs to the Church's very foundation and is continued by the bishops under the primacy of the Pope" (§881). It also says, "On the rock of this faith confessed by St. Peter, Christ built his Church" (§424). The First Vatican Council (*Pastor Aeternus*, 1870) teaches that "It was to Simon alone, to whom he had already said You shall be called Cephas," that the Lord spoke 16:17-19, and that "whoever succeeds to the chair of Peter obtains by the institution of Christ himself, the primacy of Peter over the whole church." The NABRE notes describe Peter's foundational role as "his being witness to Jesus as the Messiah, the Son of the living God."

*Eastern Orthodox.* The Encyclical of the Eastern Patriarchs (1848), issued by the patriarchs of Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem with their synods, holds that the Church "was founded upon the immovable rock of St. Peter's Confession... (which was the answer of the Apostles in common...), as the Fathers, both Eastern and Western, interpret the passage to us." Of "Feed my sheep" (John 21) it says that the command "implied no prerogative in St. Peter over the other Apostles, least of all in his successors. It was a simple restoration to his Apostleship, from which he had fallen by his thrice-repeated denial." The Orthodox Church in America likewise answers that "the word 'rock' refers to Peter's confession of faith, and not to Peter himself," and that "It is on Jesus Christ, the Rock, that the Church's unchanging faith and confession is firmly rooted." On the keys, Philaret's Longer Catechism (approved by the Russian Holy Synod) reads binding and loosing as the power to forgive sins given to the Apostles together: "The Apostles were promised by Jesus Christ power to forgive sins, when he said, Whatsoever ye shall bind on earth, shall be bound in heaven" (Q352, citing Matthew 18:18). Fr. Andrew Stephen Damick, an Antiochian priest, adds that "the Orthodox have traditionally followed St. Cyprian of Carthage (third century) in insisting that all true bishops sit upon the chair of Peter and that none has an exclusive right to his authority."

*Protestant: the confession, or Christ.* Calvin honors Peter personally, "as a living stone (1 Peter 2:5) in the temple of God," adding that "This extends, no doubt, to all believers," though "it denotes also the distinguished excellence of Peter above the rest." The rock is the common faith, by which Christ "intended to associate with Peter all the godly that would ever exist in the world." The keys are "the office of teaching," and Peter "does not receive permission to give any thing to his successors." Matthew Henry holds that "Christ himself is the Rock," and "Peter's confession is this rock as to doctrine." Ellicott calls Peter's identity with the rock "a question on which men may legitimately differ," granting "the probability that in the Aramaic... there would be no difference between the words." Then, "On the assumption of a distinction," he weighs Peter's faith, the truth confessed, and Christ, and finds that "the balance seems to incline in favour of the last view."

*Protestant: Peter himself, without successors.* Meyer (19th-century German Lutheran) holds that "the demonstrative expression... can only point to the apostle himself," adding, "it being understood, of course, that it was in consideration of Peter's faith that the Lord declared him to be a foundation of rock." Peter's primacy among the apostles "must be impartially conceded," but "Peter's successors are not for a moment thought of by Jesus." Barnes calls Peter-as-rock "the obvious meaning of the passage." The Expositor's Greek Testament: "Peter, believing that truth, is the foundation, and the building is to be of a piece with the foundation." Gregg Allison (2020) concludes, "The rock is Peter by virtue of his confession," and holds that the verse "doesn't support the Roman Catholic interpretation that Peter is the first pope."

*The Isaiah 22 argument, from both sides.* Catholic Answers (2019) argues that Jesus, as king, gives the keys to Peter, "thus making Peter the messianic equivalent of Eliakim," and answers the appeal to Revelation 3:7: "In Isaiah 22 Eliakim is master of the palace–the king isn't." John Oakes (Evidence for Christianity, 2026) grants "some interesting parallels." Of "some sort of double-prophecy here about Jesus, but also about Peter," he writes, "I would not rule that out." He still holds that Isaiah 22:20-24 "is principally a messianic foreshadow, not a papal or a Peter foreshadow."

*Objections each reading faces, from named sources.* The Catholic reading meets Calvin and Meyer, quoted above: the verse names no successors. The Holy Synod of the Moscow Patriarchate (2013) states that "The Orthodox Church rejected the doctrine of the Roman Church on papal primacy and the divine origin of the power of the first bishop in the Universal Church." It quotes Cyprian's Council of Carthage (256): "neither does any one of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops." Metropolitan Elpidophoros Lambriniadis of the Ecumenical Patriarchate contested other parts of that statement, on primacy within Orthodoxy, in "First without equals" (2014). The reading of the rock as Peter's confession meets Meyer's objection that the words can only point to Peter; he calls it "incorrect." Allison adds that it tends "to drive a wedge between the person of Peter and his divinely revealed confession." The reading of the rock as Christ meets Allison's objection that "in this context Jesus affirms that he is the builder of his church, not its foundation," and Kirsch's: "the Saviour cannot have meant Himself." The Orthodox reading of Peter's chair as shared by all bishops meets *Pastor Aeternus*, which opposes those who deny that Peter, "in preference to the rest of the apostles, taken singly or collectively, was endowed by Christ with a true and proper primacy of jurisdiction." The Protestant reading of Peter without successors meets the council's second chapter, quoted above, and Kirsch: "This foundation created for the Church by its Founder could not disappear with the person of Peter." *Pastor Aeternus* ends each of its four chapters with an anathema. Neither those canons nor the polemic in Calvin and the 1848 Encyclical is reproduced here.

This lesson does not decide among these readings. They disagree about who or what the foundation is, and whether the keys pass to anyone after Peter. The official Catholic-Orthodox Joint Commission's Chieti document (2016) records the difference in words both sides agreed. It says that in the West the primacy of the bishop of Rome "was gradually interpreted as a prerogative that was his because he was successor of Peter." It continues: "This understanding was not adopted in the East, which had a different interpretation of the Scriptures and the Fathers on this point. Our dialogue may return to this matter in the future." A reader weighing these readings for their own church life needs a pastor or priest who knows them, not a study note.

**What does 16:28 refer to?** "Truly I tell you, some who are standing here will not taste death before they see the Son of Man coming in His kingdom" (BSB). The Expositor's Greek Testament calls it a "crux interpretum," and the Pulpit Commentary says it "has always been a crux to commentators." Four readings are reported here.

*The Transfiguration.* Chrysostom, preaching on 16:28 and 17:1-8 together, says that having spoken of "hell, and of the kingdom," Christ "shows in the vision" the kingdom. In Matthew the next verse begins "After six days," and three of those "standing here" see Jesus transfigured (17:1-2).

*The resurrection, ascension and Pentecost.* Poole calls this "the most generally received opinion": "his resurrection from the dead. His ascension into heaven, and sending the Holy Spirit." The Catholic NABRE notes read "the manifestation of Jesus' rule after his resurrection." Barnes reads it as the church "greatly enlarged, established, and spreading," and names "the wonders of the day of Pentecost" among what the apostles lived to see.

*The fall of Jerusalem (AD 70).* The Cambridge Bible lists the Transfiguration, Pentecost and the fall of Jerusalem, and judges that "The last best fulfils the conditions." The Pulpit Commentary agrees: "This advent is doubtless the destruction of Jerusalem." It adds that "There is some truth in all the views," quoting Wordsworth that the prophecy "unfolded itself by degrees." Ellicott ties the saying to Matthew 24:34, quoted in his own older wording: "This generation shall not pass away till all be fulfilled."

*An expectation that did not come true.* Albert Schweitzer (*The Quest of the Historical Jesus*, English 1910) argued that Jesus expected the end within his hearers' lifetime. Of the parallel saying at 10:23 he wrote, "this prediction was not fulfilled," calling it "the first postponement of the Parousia." He read Mark's form of the cross saying and the promise that follows (Mark 8:34-9:1) inside that same expectation, after relocating that section from Caesarea Philippi to an earlier stay at Bethsaida. Summarizing Hermann Reimarus (18th century), Schweitzer writes of 16:28: "It simply means that Jesus promises the fulfilment of all Messianic hopes before the end of the existing generation." He reports D. F. Strauss as judging that sayings like 10:23 and 16:28 "rather suggest" a coming within Jesus' lifetime. Schweitzer's own reconstruction also held that Jesus faced His death believing it would bring the Kingdom.

*Objections each reading faces.* Against the Transfiguration, the Pulpit Commentary asks, "How could Christ assert in the most solemn manner... an event which was to occur only a week hence?" Ellicott makes the same point. Against the resurrection and Pentecost, the Pulpit Commentary says those events came "only half a year after this time" and could not have been foretold "in the terms here used." Meyer calls the Pentecost reading "entirely out of place" given "some who are standing here." Against the fall of Jerusalem, Meyer calls a "figurative coming in the destruction of Jerusalem" a way "to explain it away in a manner no less arbitrary." Against the reading of a failed expectation, Meyer answers Strauss that "theoretical error" in this prediction "would necessarily" compromise "the ethical side" of Jesus' ministry. (Meyer himself explains the delay by how the disciples merged several senses of Christ's coming.) The Expositor's Greek Testament offers its own mediating view, that Jesus' words "provide for two possible alternatives: for a near advent and for an indefinitely postponed advent." This lesson does not choose among the readings.

**A misuse of "take up his cross" (16:24).** In its own setting, the saying asks a disciple to follow Jesus, who "must be killed" (16:21), and to lose his life "for My sake" (16:25). It has also been turned against people who are being harmed at home. Mariz Tadros (Institute of Development Studies, University of Sussex), writing in *Public Orthodoxy* (2019), documents clergy pressing abused women to return to violent husbands with "one common message: this is your cross to bear." She writes that this "is not specific to the Coptic Orthodox faith." She links similar reports from Russian Orthodox, Catholic and Church of England-linked sources, and a Greek Orthodox priest's denunciation of the practice. She names the verse, in her wording "Take up your cross and follow Me" (Matthew 16:24; Mark 8:34; Luke 9:23), as the one appropriated, and quotes a Coptic counselor, Dr. Nabil Baki: "This is a wrong counsel." She calls for "a church-wide acknowledgement that domestic violence is not a cross to bear." The U.S. Catholic bishops' statement *When I Call for Help* (2002) says: "As bishops, we condemn the use of the Bible to support abusive behavior in any form," and "no person is expected to stay in an abusive marriage." The text itself bears on this. In 16:24 the cross is taken up by the one who chooses to "come after Me." Nothing in 16:21-28 tells anyone to accept harm from another person or to stay where they are being hurt.

*A pastoral note.* If someone is hurting you at home, or if a church leader has told you that enduring it is your cross, you deserve safety and real help. A study note is not that help. In the United States, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is at 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788. If you are in immediate danger, call your local emergency number. A pastor or counselor you trust can also walk with you, ideally someone outside any group that is pressing you.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key. Your own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 16:26: "What will it profit a man if he gains the whole world, yet forfeits his soul? Or what can a man give in exchange for his soul?" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the question follows the call to "deny himself and take up his cross" (16:24) and the saying that whoever "wants to save his life will lose it" (16:25). "Soul" here and "life" in 16:25 render the same Greek word (*psychē*, Nestle 1904 and Westcott-Hort on Bible Hub). Inference, the lesson's own: for disciples who had just heard Peter try to talk Jesus out of suffering, the question asked what following Him is worth when it costs something.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** No gain is worth trading oneself away for, and following Jesus may cost something that looks like loss.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the weighing of gain against the self; the call to follow when it costs. Not carried forward: the disciples' road to Jerusalem, or any claim that suffering itself is the goal. The cross is taken up "for My sake" (16:25), not handed to someone by a person harming them.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a question and a principle spoken to disciples, carried over by analogy. Promise scope: 16:27 speaks of the Son of Man repaying "each one according to what he has done." The passage promises no earthly reward for choosing integrity, and none is claimed here.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. A project manager is offered a large bonus if she signs off on a safety inspection report she knows is incomplete. Signing would also secure her promotion.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: integrity at a cost. One faithful response: she declines to sign until the inspection is complete. She states the gap to her supervisor in writing, learns the company's reporting process, and accepts that the bonus and the promotion may be lost.

**Cautions.** This is not legal advice. Refusing to sign or reporting a safety problem can carry legal and financial consequences; a person in this position should talk with an employment lawyer, a union representative or the appropriate regulator. "Deny himself" here means refusing a gain that would cost her integrity, not accepting mistreatment. A person being harassed or threatened at work is not asked by this verse to endure it.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Peter is called blessed (16:17) and, six verses later, "Satan" and "a stumbling block" (16:23). Does the rebuke weigh for or against any reading of 16:18 in Positions, or is it neutral?
2. Matthew 16:19 is addressed to one person and 18:18 to a group, in the same English words. What in Matthew 18:15-20 would help you judge whether the change in Greek number matters?
3. Matthew 16:28 is followed at once by "After six days" (17:1); Mark's form is followed by the same words (Mark 9:2). Does the placement favor the Transfiguration reading, or is it simply the order in which events happened?
4. Why might Jesus forbid the disciples "to tell anyone" (16:20) a confession He has just said His Father revealed? How does 16:21-23 bear on your answer?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 16:13-28 in order, from the two questions to 16:28. (b) Quote 16:18 in the BSB's own words. (c) Name what only Matthew has, compared with Mark and Luke.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the scene and its parallels; (1:00-2:00) Petros, petra and Kepha, with sources from more than one side; (2:00-3:30) the rock and the keys: the Fathers, Catholic, Orthodox and two Protestant readings in their own words, with one objection each; (3:30-4:15) binding and loosing, and the 16:19-18:18 recurrence; (4:15-5:00) the readings of 16:28, and the misuse of 16:24 with its answer.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: "Augustine's 'let the reader choose' shows that 'this rock' was never settled, so no church can claim this verse." Before answering, note that Chrysostom reads the rock as Peter's faith and also says Christ "makes him a shepherd," and that the Catechism holds person and faith together (§§424, 552). A second candidate, running the other way: "Calling the rock 'the confession' is a modern Protestant dodge." Before answering, look again at what Chrysostom and Augustine, both writing more than a thousand years before the Reformation, say in Positions.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 16:13-28 does not mention Rome, bishops or successors (none of these words occurs in BSB Matthew, by script). Whether the promise implies successors is the dispute in Positions, which the traditions answer from other texts and from history. (b) Matthew 16:24 does not tell anyone to stay where they are being harmed. The cross is taken up by the one who chooses to follow, "for My sake." (c) Matthew 16:28 does not name the event it foretells.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words of both texts. The five attached to this lesson:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mark 8:27-9:1 | parallel | strong | Same scene in the same order; "Get behind Me, Satan!" identical in the BSB; Mark lacks 16:17-19 |
| Luke 9:18-27 | parallel | strong | Same question, confession and cross saying; no rock, keys or rebuke; "take up his cross daily" |
| Isaiah 22:20-22 | allusion | strong | A king gives the key of his house to his steward; "key" shared, but the verbs differ, there is no formula, and Swete's Septuagint lacks the clause; named by Catholic and Protestant commentators |
| Matthew 18:17-18 | motif | strong | "Bound in heaven... loosed in heaven" only here and at 16:19 in the BSB; singular in 16:19, plural in 18:18 in Greek |
| Matthew 10:38-39 → 16:24-25 | motif | strong | Authored with lesson 21; "take up his cross and follow Me" recurs in the BSB |

The Isaiah row is this lesson's thread connection to the Old Testament. Matthew gives it no formula, which is why it is an allusion; the label rests on the shared noun and image and on commentators of different traditions who name it. What the link implies for succession is the Positions dispute, not the grade. Two candidates were considered and not authored. **Isaiah 28:16 and 1 Peter 2:8**, which Barnes cites for the reading that Christ is the rock ("Christ is called a rock"), were considered. Matthew 16:18 points to neither, so linking them would argue one reading of the verse. **Isaiah 38:10** ("the gates of Sheol") shares the phrase Ellicott hears in 16:18. Meyer lists other places with the same picture of gates, among them Job 38:17 and Psalm 107:18 ("the gates of death," BSB), so a link to one verse is not warranted.
