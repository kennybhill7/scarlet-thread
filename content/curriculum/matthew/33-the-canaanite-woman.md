Matthew 15:21-39 has two scenes. In the first, Jesus withdraws "to the district of Tyre and Sidon" (15:21, BSB), and a woman of that region cries after Him for her daughter. He is silent; then He says, "I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (15:24); then, "It is not right to take the children's bread and toss it to the dogs" (15:26). She answers, "Yes, Lord ... even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their master's table" (15:27), and He says, "O woman ... your faith is great!" (15:28). In the second scene, back beside the Sea of Galilee, crowds bring the lame, blind, crippled and mute, and Jesus feeds "four thousand men ... in addition to women and children" with seven loaves and a few fish (15:38). This lesson practices reading a hard saying honestly: checking the words, in this app's BSB and in Greek, before the explanations; laying Matthew beside Mark; and reporting what named readers make of the saying about dogs, each at comparable strength and with the objection it faces. The lesson also names how readings of this scene have been used against Jews, and it reports, without deciding, whether the second feeding is a second event and who was fed.

**A note before reading.** This passage centers on a parent pleading for a child who is suffering: "My daughter is miserably possessed by a demon" (15:22). Christian traditions understand possession in different ways, and this lesson neither equates it with illness nor denies it. If you are caring for someone in mental or emotional crisis, or are in crisis yourself, a study note is not the right support. In the United States you can call, text or chat with the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (988, or 988lifeline.org) at any hour, and call your local emergency number if anyone is in immediate danger. A doctor who knows the person, and a pastor, can help in ways a lesson cannot.

## Context

**The shape of the passage.** After the dispute over hand-washing (15:1-20), Jesus "withdrew to the district of Tyre and Sidon" (15:21). The woman cries out (15:22); He does not answer, and the disciples urge, "Send her away, for she keeps crying out after us" (15:23). He answers, the woman kneels and says, "Lord, help me!" (15:25), and the exchange about bread and dogs follows (15:26-27). Her daughter is "healed from that very hour" (15:28). Then Jesus goes along the Sea of Galilee, up a mountain, heals many (15:29-31), and feeds the four thousand (15:32-38) before sailing "to the region of Magadan" (15:39).

**Tyre and Sidon.** In Matthew's BSB, "Tyre" and "Sidon" occur only at 11:21-22 and 15:21 (a script search). At 11:21-22 the two cities stand for outsiders who would have repented: "if the miracles that were performed in you had been performed in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago." Genesis 10:15 names Sidon as Canaan's firstborn: "Canaan was the father of Sidon his firstborn" (BSB).

**"Canaanite": one word, once.** The BSB calls the woman "a Canaanite woman" (15:22). A script search finds the word "Canaanite" (or "Canaanites") nowhere else in this app's New Testament. In Greek the adjective *Chananaia* occurs only here (Bible Hub's concordance lists one occurrence). The King James Version's "Simon the Canaanite" (Matthew 10:4; Mark 3:18) is a different Greek word, *Kananaios*, which the BSB renders "Zealot"; Mookgo Kgatle (*Stellenbosch Theological Journal*, 2018) notes the same distinction. The land, "Canaan," appears twice, at Acts 7:11 and 13:19. Mark calls the same woman "a Greek woman of Syrophoenician origin" (Mark 7:26).

Why Matthew uses the old name is not stated in the text. Named readers tie it to Israel's history with Canaan, and they draw different things from that tie.
- John Chrysostom (*Homily 52 on Matthew*) says that when you hear "of a Canaanitish woman, you should call to mind those wicked nations, who overset from their foundations the very laws of nature," so as to consider "the power of Christ's advent."
- John Calvin suggests the name was a Jewish usage for the region's people: "The Jews disdainfully gave the name of Canaanites to all the inhabitants of that district; and it is probable that the majority of them were descended from the tribes of Canaan."
- Marilyn Salmon (Working Preacher, 2008) writes: "There were no Canaanites living in the first century, so the label does not describe present-day encounters. The label evokes historical conflicts and thus defines the woman in terms of age-old prejudices a first-century Jewish audience would understand."
- Amy-Jill Levine ("Matthew and Anti-Judaism," 2007) reads the name through Matthew's own genealogy: it "reminds readers of Canaanite women in the genealogy, Tamar and Rahab, who proved more faithful than the men with whom they are associated." (Genesis 38 does not itself state Tamar's people.)
- Elaine Wainwright (*HTS Teologiese Studies*, 2014) reports Mark Nanos's suggestion that the woman "may be a descendent of the Northern tribes who were deported, intermarried or were amongst others sent to repopulate the region." Nanos is reported here only through Wainwright.

**Matthew and Mark side by side.** The two accounts share the setting, the request, the saying and the reply. The differences, checked in the BSB:

| | Matthew 15:21-28 | Mark 7:24-30 |
|---|---|---|
| Where | "the district of Tyre and Sidon"; no house | "the region of Tyre"; "He entered a house" |
| The woman | "a Canaanite woman" | "a Greek woman of Syrophoenician origin" |
| Her first words | "Lord, Son of David, have mercy on me!" | none quoted |
| Silence, disciples | "Jesus did not answer a word"; "Send her away" | not present |
| "Sent only" | "I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" | not present |
| The saying | "It is not right to take the children's bread and toss it to the dogs" | the same, preceded by "First let the children have their fill" |
| Her reply | "even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their master's table" | "even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs" |
| Jesus' answer | "O woman ... your faith is great!" | "Because of this answer, you may go" |
| The outcome | "healed from that very hour" | "she went home and found her child lying on the bed" |

A script search finds no "faith" anywhere in Mark 7. Augustine (*Harmony of the Gospels* 2.49) treats the accounts as one event and harmonizes the house: Jesus, during "that silence which He maintained," went out of the house.

**"Sent only to the lost sheep."** Matthew 15:24 repeats the phrase of 10:6. Lesson 20 of this course reports the Greek (the same in both verses), the Jeremiah 50:6 background, and four named readings of how 10:5-6 and 15:24 fit "make disciples of all nations" (28:19). That material is not repeated here; see Lesson 20, Positions 1.

**"Dogs" and "little dogs."** "Dog" or "dogs" occurs in nine verses of this app's New Testament ("dogs" in eight) (a script search). The Greek behind 15:26-27 is *kynaria*, a diminutive of the ordinary word for dog. Bible Hub's concordance lists four uses in the New Testament, all in this story (Matthew 15:26-27; Mark 7:27-28). Elsewhere Matthew uses the ordinary word: "Do not give dogs what is holy" (7:6). In the Greek editions on Bible Hub, all read "it is not good" (*ouk estin kalon*) at 15:26 except Tischendorf's eighth edition, which reads "it is not lawful" (*ouk exestin*); the Pulpit Commentary calls that reading one "of less authority." What the diminutive does to the saying is disputed; see Positions 1.

**The daughter.** Matthew says only that the daughter is "miserably possessed by a demon" (15:22) and that she was healed (15:28); she never appears. Chrysostom dwells on the mother's suffering, explaining why she says "Have mercy on me": "she indeed is insensible of her disease, but it is I that suffer her innumerable woes." Calvin's comment does not discuss the daughter's condition. Matthew Henry (Concise) applies the verse to children "grievously vexed" in soul. YongHan Chung (*HTS Teologiese Studies*, 2025) reports that "Scholars often interpret these demonic forces in Matthew as symbolic of political oppression and colonial domination," citing Warren Carter. Wainwright hears in the mother's cry that of families "separated by migration or asylum seeking." This lesson reports these readings without choosing.

**The second scene (15:29-39).** Jesus goes "along the Sea of Galilee" and "up on a mountain" (15:29); Matthew names no region. Mark's parallel says Jesus went "through Sidon to the Sea of Galilee and into the region of the Decapolis" (Mark 7:31), and tells one healing, of a man "deaf and hardly able to speak" (7:32), where Matthew gives a summary of many (15:30-31). The crowd "glorified the God of Israel" (15:31). That phrase occurs in this app's New Testament only here and at Luke 1:68 (a script search). For the feeding, lesson 30 already covers the two basket words (*kophinos*, *spyris*), "in addition to women and children" (14:21; 15:38), and "gave thanks" (15:36) beside "spoke a blessing" (14:19). Two points are new. First, Jesus, not the disciples, raises the need: "I have compassion for this crowd, because they have already been with Me three days and have nothing to eat" (15:32). Second, Matthew's landing place, "Magadan" (15:39), is Mark's "Dalmanutha" (Mark 8:10). Augustine (*Harmony* 2.51) and Calvin both take the two names as one place or district; Augustine adds that "most codices, even of Mark's Gospel, give no other reading than that of Magedan."

## Literary Design

Notes on how 15:21-39 is put together. They are claims about the text's shape, not about what that shape is meant to teach.

**Three appeals, three answers.** The woman speaks three times, and each time she says "Lord": "Lord, Son of David, have mercy on me!" (15:22), "Lord, help me!" (15:25), "Yes, Lord" (15:27). Jesus' responses move from silence (15:23), to a word spoken to the disciples about His mission (15:24), to a word spoken to her (15:26). Chrysostom traces the same steps: "when He vouchsafed her a word, then He smote her more sharply than by His silence." Eunyung Lim (Working Preacher, 2026) notes that Matthew has her say "Lord" "each time she speaks," and that Greek *kyrios* ("lord") "sounds similar to, yet contrasts in meaning with" *kynarion* ("dog"). She also notes that the woman's last word turns on "their masters' [lords'] table" (15:27).

**Two "great" faiths.** In Matthew's BSB, "great faith" occurs only at 8:10, of the centurion ("I have not found anyone in Israel with such great faith"), and "your faith is great" only at 15:28. Both are commendations of people from outside Israel who ask on behalf of someone else, and both healings happen at a distance. The Greek wording differs (*tosautēn pistin* at 8:10; *megalē sou hē pistis* at 15:28), so the English match is partly the translation's.

**Bread between two feedings.** The woman's exchange is about "the children's bread" and "crumbs" (15:26-27), and it sits between a feeding with "twelve basketfuls" left over (14:20) and one with "seven basketfuls" left over (15:37). No source read here argues that Matthew arranged this on purpose; it is offered as an observation only.

## Positions

Three questions in this passage draw named, published disagreement. None is settled here. Each reading is given in its holders' own words.

**1. "It is not right to take the children's bread and toss it to the dogs" (15:26): how is the saying to be heard?** The words are not in dispute. What they were for, and what they say about Jesus, is. Five readings are reported, followed by the points where their holders explicitly disagree.

*A test of her faith.* Chrysostom: "With this intent did Christ put her off, for He knew she would say this; for this did He deny the grant, that He might exhibit her high self-command." He adds, "Not in insult then were His words spoken, but calling her forth, and revealing the treasure laid up in her." Calvin calls the reply "harsher than ever," and says Christ "disdainfully compares the woman herself to a dog," and writes, "His chief design was, to make trial of the woman's faith"; the same sentence goes on to say that Jesus also pointed to judgment on the Jews who rejected Him (see the caution below). Barnes holds that Jesus "meant to try her faith," and adds that "he cannot be understood as intending to justify or sanction the use of such terms."

*A softened word: "little dogs."* Ellicott says the diminutive "pointed not to the wild, unclean beasts that haunt the streets of an Eastern city ... but to the tamer animals that were bred in the house." He adds that the answer "has, even taking this into account, a somewhat harsh sound," and calls it "probably but a common proverb." (Ellicott also holds reading 4.) The Expositor's Greek Testament: "seemingly a hard word, but not so hard as it seems," with *kynaria* containing "a loophole." Meyer says the diminutive "is intended to mitigate the harshness of the expression." Bengel: the word "is a diminutive, and Jesus thereby gives a handle to the woman to take hold of Him."

*A literary convention, and "to the Jew first."* Amy-Jill Levine reads the scene as a known story-shape: "By initially refusing the woman, the Matthean Jesus follows a literary convention, known from both Roman and Jewish sources, in which someone in authority is humbled by his social inferior." She continues: "Thus Matthew instructs ecclesial leaders to follow Jesus in attending to the 'least,' even if doing so is not part of their job description." And: "The point is not Jewish exclusivism; it is rather history, and salvation history."

*Jesus changed, or the woman is the model.* Ellicott rejects "the supposition of a simulated harshness" and sees "the prevailing power of prayer working on the sympathy of Christ, and leading Him to pass beyond the ordinary limits of His appointed work." On 15:28 he writes: "The answer of the woman changed the conditions of the problem, and therefore, we may reverently add, changed the purpose which depended on them." Eunyung Lim (2026): "This woman's respectful persistence, wisdom, and endurance in the face of silence, rejection, and humiliation prompt Jesus to change his mind and praise her great faith." She sets this within the history of Matthew's community (Positions 2). Richard Ward (Working Preacher, 2023) writes that at first Jesus "reacts to the woman's request as they would expect of a rabbi in those days," and that at her answer "Jesus' tradition-shaped heart breaks open." Marilyn Salmon (2008): "quite frankly, Jesus does not come off well in this encounter," and "The Canaanite woman models the most admirable human behavior, not Jesus." She also writes, "Of course the story is about Jesus. We see a very human Jesus," and that "Jesus has the last word."

*A postcolonial feminist reading of her agency.* YongHan Chung (2025) shifts attention from Jesus' intention to the woman's act. Her reply "reframes the relationship between the 'children' and the 'dogs'." Chung writes that the woman's faith "proves persuasive even to Jesus," while declining a larger claim: "Although it may be an overstatement to claim that the woman unveils a new dimension of Jesus' mission to the Gentiles, it is plausible to contend that she prompts him to reaffirm the inclusive nature of divine grace." Elaine Wainwright (2014), reading the scene alongside today's migrants, writes that "This verse and this attitude of Jesus need to be engaged with critically."

*Where the readings disagree.* Against the test reading, Ellicott rejects "the supposition of a simulated harshness," and MacLaren writes that "His refusal was a real refusal, founded on the divine decree." Chung objects that the test reading rests on "the assumption that Jesus possesses omniscient control over every situation" and reduces the woman "to a mere instrument for unveiling Jesus' salvific plan." Salmon names the temptation "to justify Jesus' unseemly behavior or pretend we did not see it." Against readings that hear contempt in the saying, MacLaren writes that "the woman seized His intention much better than later critics who find 'national scorn' in the words." Against the softened word, the Pulpit Commentary calls *kynaria* "A contemptuous diminutive": "Scripture gives no warrant for thinking that the Hebrews ever kept dogs as friends and companions, in our modern fashion; and our Lord adopts the language of his countrymen, to put the woman in her right position, as one with whom Jews could have no fellowship." The Geneva note on Mark 7:27 says Jesus used the small-dog word "that he may seem to speak more reproachfully." Against the changed-Jesus reading, Chrysostom holds that Jesus "knew she would say this." Levine objects to readings that "highlight Jesus' Jewish ethnocentrism" and to the idea "that in heeding the woman Jesus also overcame his Jewish misogynism": "None of this is helpful or necessary." Of the holders above, Ward comes closest to what she describes; Salmon makes a similar correction herself (see the caution below), and Lim locates the change in the history of Matthew's community. This lesson reports all five readings and ranks none. If the saying troubles you in your own faith, that is worth taking to a pastor or a teacher you trust, not settling from a study note.

**2. Is this scene a turn toward the Gentiles?** Lesson 20 reports four readings of how 10:5-6 and 15:24 fit 28:19. The question here is narrower: does this scene mark a turn?

*An early sign.* Calvin: "In this miracle we are informed in what manner the grace of Christ began to flow to the Gentiles; for, though the full time was not yet come ... yet he intended to give some early manifestations of the common mercy which was at length offered indiscriminately to Jews and Gentiles after his resurrection." Chrysostom says Jesus, having set the disciples "free from the observance of meats" in 15:1-20, "then to the Gentiles also He goes on to open a door," and points ahead to Peter, who is "sent to Cornelius." Carla Works (Working Preacher, 2014): "The encounter with the Canaanite woman prepares the reader for Jesus' great commission to go and to make disciples of all the nations". Lim reads the story as reflecting how Matthew's community "came to open its doors to the gentiles."

*Not yet a turn.* Levine: "The Gentile mission begins only with the 'Great Commission' (28:16-20)." Chrysostom himself, answering the objection that Jesus forbade the disciples the Gentiles' road, says "that not in order to preach did He depart." The text can be checked on one point. Matthew has already told of a Gentile commended for faith (8:5-13), so this scene is not the first such encounter in the Gospel. These readings overlap: Calvin, Works and Levine all place the full mission to the nations after the resurrection. They differ on whether this scene foreshadows it. This lesson does not decide.

**3. The four thousand: a second event or a second telling, and who was fed?** The two feedings share their shape. Two answers to whether they are two events, and a middle answer, are on record.

*Two events.* Augustine (*Harmony* 2.50): since the evangelists who tell of the seven loaves "have also not failed to mention the other miracle of the five loaves, no difficulty can be felt by any one, and all can see that both works were really wrought." Chrysostom suggests, as one explanation, that the basket counts differ so that "by the variation they might be reminded of both one and the other." Ellicott lists the "notes of distinctness" (numbers, three days, loaves, baskets, the two basket words). He also points to 16:9-10, where Jesus speaks of "the five loaves for the five thousand" and "the seven loaves for the four thousand" (BSB), as "decisive as proving that there had been two events."

*One event told twice.* David Friedrich Strauss (*Life of Jesus*, section 102) reports and defends the opinion of "later critics" that "here one and the same fact has been doubled, through a mistake of the first Evangelist." His chief reason is the disciples' question in 15:33: it is hard to conceive that they, "after they had themselves witnessed how Jesus was able to feed a great multitude ... should nevertheless on a second occasion of the same kind, have totally forgotten the first." Meyer lists eighteen critics who held it, Strauss among them; the others were not read here.

*Two events, told alike.* Meyer, citing Bleek, takes "that there were actually two instances of miraculous feeding of a substantially similar character, but that ... in the early traditions the accounts came to assume pretty much the same shape."

*Objections each reading faces.* Against two events, Strauss presses the disciples' forgetfulness and calls one proposed explanation "pure fictions." Against one event, Ellicott and Meyer point to 16:9-10. Meyer notes that "As a consequence of this duplicate-hypothesis, it has been found necessary to question the authenticity of Matthew 16:9 f." Strauss answers that the two basket words, kept "in the subsequent allusion to them," fit the evangelist's copying two traditions "just as he read or heard them." Against the middle view, Strauss argues that even without the disciples' question it is "sufficiently improbable that the scene should have been repeated in so entirely similar a manner." All the voices above are from before 1900. No modern study of the question was read; see Sources and Limits.

*Who was fed?* Matthew names no region, but readers place the scene in Gentile country. The Expositor's Greek Testament says "glorified the God of Israel" (15:31) "suggests a non-Israelite crowd and seems to hint that after all for our evangelist Jesus is on the east side and in heathen territory." In the same comment it allows, citing Weiss-Meyer, that the phrase "may point back to Matthew 15:24 and mean the God who conferred such favours on Israel as distinct from the heathen." On 15:37 the same commentary reports that "Hilary contends for Gentile recipients of the second blessing, with whom Westcott ... agrees" (both secondhand). The Cambridge Bible on Mark 8:1 calls the district (not the crowd) "the half-pagan district of Decapolis." Others read a Jewish crowd. MacLaren speaks of "Jewish sufferers" on "the half-Gentilised eastern shore of the lake." The Pulpit Commentary on 15:30: "The fame of Jesus attracted the Jews settled in this semi-Gentile district." Meyer on 15:31 says "Israel" is added "in the consciousness of the advantages they possessed over the neighbouring Gentiles." The text itself states only the crowd's praise of "the God of Israel"; it does not state who was in the crowd. This lesson does not decide.

**A caution on how this passage has been used.** Readings of this scene have been turned against Jews. Calvin writes that the woman's faith is presented "for the purpose of instructing us by means of comparison, that the Jews were justly deprived of the promised redemption." Chrysostom sets her humility against "the proud language of the Jews," and says the Canaanites "appeared so much better disposed than the Jews ... while those were driving Him away." Meyer, explaining why Jesus spoke of dogs, cites among his authorities Eisenmenger's *Entdecktes Judenthum*. Wikipedia (a tertiary source) describes that work as "an early example of modern antisemitism." Modern readings can do something similar more quietly, as Levine warns: when interpreters "highlight Jesus' Jewish ethnocentrism," or say that in heeding the woman He "overcame his Jewish misogynism." Salmon makes a related correction. The view that the label shows Jewish antipathy to all Gentiles "implies that all Jews loathed and avoided all non-Jews. First-century Jews were diverse in their attitudes toward gentiles" and in their dealings with them. Several nineteenth-century commentators cited above (Barnes, the Cambridge Bible, the Pulpit Commentary, Meyer), and Calvin on the name "Canaanites," explain the saying by claiming that Jews generally despised Gentiles; the Pulpit's line about the woman, quoted in Positions 1, is one example. Salmon's correction applies to that claim. Barnes and the Pulpit also compare the word to Muslim or Turkish usage of their own day: the Pulpit sets it beside "Turks nowadays" calling Christians dogs, and adds that later, "by a curious inversion," Jews were called dogs. No source read for this lesson documents this verse itself being used against Gentiles as a group, so no such claim is made. What the text says can be checked. The saying is spoken once, to one woman. Jesus grants her request and commends her faith (15:28). The Gospel that records it ends with the sending to "all nations" (28:19). Nothing in 15:21-28 states a verdict on Jews or on Gentiles as peoples.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, a model of the shape and not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** "And a Canaanite woman from that region came to Him, crying out, 'Lord, Son of David, have mercy on me! My daughter is miserably possessed by a demon.' ... The woman came and knelt before Him. 'Lord, help me!' she said" (Matthew 15:22, 25, BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the woman asks for her daughter, who is not present. She keeps asking through silence and through words that seem to refuse her, and she speaks respectfully each time ("Lord"). Inference (this lesson's own): whatever reading of 15:26 one holds, the scene presents a parent who keeps speaking up for a child who cannot speak up for herself.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Speaking up, respectfully and repeatedly, for someone in your care who cannot speak for themselves is worth doing even when the first answers are silence or no.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: persistent, respectful advocacy on behalf of another. Not carried forward: any reading of Positions 1, any claim about what possession is, any expectation that persistence guarantees a particular outcome, and any idea that a refusal or an insult from someone in authority is a test to be endured.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a narrated encounter, carried by analogy; it is not a command to the reader. Promise scope: "Let it be done for you as you desire" (15:28) is spoken to one woman about one request. It is not a promise to the reader that a request will be granted.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family and caregiving. A parent's teenage son has been withdrawn and struggling for weeks. The parent has raised it at a clinic and at school and has been told to wait for the next opening.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: advocacy for a dependent. One faithful response: the parent keeps the son's appointments on the calendar, writes down what has changed and when, asks the doctor directly about next steps, and keeps asking politely until someone answers.

**Cautions.** This example is not counsel for any particular family. If anyone is thinking of suicide or self-harm, or is in danger, do not wait for an appointment: in the United States, call, text or chat with 988 at any hour, and call your local emergency number if there is immediate danger. A doctor should evaluate any change in a person's mental health. A pastor can walk with a family, but should not be asked to replace medical care. Whichever reading of 15:26 one holds, this passage should never be used to tell someone that harsh treatment from a person in authority is a test they must endure, or that a parent's faith was too small if a child is not healed.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Matthew alone has the silence, the disciples' "Send her away," and "I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (15:23-24). Mark alone has "First let the children have their fill" (Mark 7:27). How does each Gospel's version change the way the saying about dogs sounds? Which of the five readings in Positions 1 depends most on one Gospel's wording?
2. Ellicott, the Expositor's Greek Testament and Meyer hear the diminutive *kynaria* as softening; the Pulpit Commentary hears it as contemptuous. What evidence about Greek diminutives, or about dogs in first-century households, would you need to decide between them?
3. If the four thousand were Gentiles, the passage moves from one Gentile woman to a Gentile crowd. If they were Jews, it moves back from the edge to Israel. Which details in 15:29-39 would you weigh, and which does Matthew leave out that Mark includes?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 15:21-28 in order, naming Jesus' three responses before He grants the request. (b) Quote the saying of 15:26 and the woman's reply, as the BSB has them. (c) Name three things Matthew has that Mark 7:24-30 does not.

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) the scene and its setting, with Tyre and Sidon at 11:21-22; (1:00-2:00) "Canaanite" and what named readers make of it; (2:00-3:30) the saying, with at least three of the five readings in Positions 1 in their own words, and one objection; (3:30-4:15) the caution on misuse, and what the text does and does not say; (4:15-5:00) the four thousand: two events or one, and who was fed, ending on an open question.

3. **Likely objection.** Form your own objection to one reading first. If you are stuck, here is one: "Calling the saying a test makes Jesus play a game with a desperate mother." (Read Chrysostom's full reason, and then Chung's objection, and ask what each assumes about what Jesus knew.) A second runs the other way: "Saying Jesus changed his mind makes Him the problem the story solves." (Read Levine's objection, and then Lim's and Ward's own words, and ask whether each is claiming something about Jesus, about Matthew's community, or about the reader.)

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 15:21-28 does not state a verdict on Jews or Gentiles as peoples; the saying is spoken to one woman, whose request is granted (15:28). (b) Matthew 15:22 does not describe the daughter's condition beyond "miserably possessed by a demon," and the text does not equate possession with any illness. (c) Matthew 15:29-39 does not name the region or the makeup of the crowd; "Decapolis" is Mark's word (Mark 7:31).

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a row and defend its type and label from the words in both texts:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mark 7:24-30 | parallel | strong | Same scene and saying, in nearly the same Greek words; Matthew alone has "Canaanite," "Son of David," "sent only" and "faith" |
| Mark 8:1-10 | parallel | strong | Same feeding in much the same wording; spyris in both; Magadan and Dalmanutha |
| Isaiah 35:5-6 | allusion | plausible | Blind, mute or deaf, and lame share Greek nouns with the Septuagint; no formula; a shared verb (akouō) only in the Greek Orthodox Church text |
| Joshua 2:8-14 | motif | plausible | A woman of Canaan confesses Israel's God and asks for her family; the link runs through Levine's reading of "Canaanite" and Matthew 1:5 |

Or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`. The main candidate not authored is Elijah and the widow of Zarephath (1 Kings 17:8-24), a mother near Sidon whose son is restored. Luke names that story (Luke 4:26); Matthew 15 does not, and shares no wording with 1 Kings 17. The only link found is Meyer's report that Volkmar called Matthew's scene "an imitation" of it, a claim about origins, not a connection Matthew draws. A second candidate is the conquest background of "Canaanite" (Deuteronomy 7:1-2; Genesis 10:15-19). It was declined because no source read here argues that Matthew points to those texts, and authoring it would build one of the readings of "Canaanite" into the thread itself. Say what new evidence would justify authoring either.

## Sources and Limits

- **Every BSB quotation in this lesson and its four connection files was checked by script against this app's own corpus** (`web/public/bible/BSB/*.json`), before and after drafting.
- **Every count or "only" claim was produced by a case-insensitive script over the shipped BSB**, on word boundaries: "Canaanite(s)" in the New Testament, "Canaan," "Syrophoenician," "Tyre," "Sidon," "dogs," "faith" in Mark 7, "great faith" and "faith is great," "God of Israel," "O woman," "Decapolis," "Magadan," "Dalmanutha" and "Rahab." A first pass counted "O woman" twice because it matched inside "no woman"; the word-boundary recount found one. The script and its output are kept with this lesson's working files. These counts apply to this app's English text, not to the Greek.
- **The Greek rests on Bible Hub's parallel-text and concordance pages**, saved as raw text: *Chananaia* (one occurrence), *Kananaios*, *Chanaan*, *kynarion* (four occurrences), and the Tischendorf reading at 15:26. These are not critical editions.
- **Read directly:** Chrysostom's Homilies 52 and 53 on Matthew and Augustine's *Harmony of the Gospels* 2.49-2.51 (New Advent); Calvin's *Harmony* on Matthew 15 (via Bible Hub); Strauss, *Life of Jesus Critically Examined*, section 102 (George Eliot's translation); Levine (2007); Chung (2025), Wainwright (2014) and Kgatle (2018), open-access journals; and Working Preacher commentaries by Salmon (2008), Works (2014), Ward (2023) and Lim (2026).
- **Read through the Bible Hub aggregator:** Ellicott, Matthew Henry (Concise), Barnes, Poole, Gill, Meyer, Bengel and his editors, the Expositor's Greek Testament, the Cambridge Bible, the Pulpit Commentary, Vincent and the Geneva notes.
- **Reported secondhand, not read:** Mark Nanos (through Wainwright); Warren Carter (through Chung); Volkmar, Bleek, Hilary and Westcott, and the critics Meyer lists on the second feeding (through Meyer and the Expositor's Greek Testament); Eisenmenger (through Meyer, with Wikipedia, a tertiary source, for the book's reception).
- **Feminist and postcolonial readings** are represented by Chung (a postcolonial feminist reading, by its own title), with Wainwright and Lim, read directly. Book-length studies (Sharon Ringe, Musa Dube, Elaine Wainwright's 1991 monograph, Gail O'Day's 2001 essay) were not read; Lim and Chung cite some of them. The Working Preacher pieces are short commentaries for preachers, not peer-reviewed scholarship.
- **Not consulted:** modern critical commentaries on Matthew (Davies and Allison, Luz), and any modern study of whether the two feedings are one event. Positions 3 therefore rests on Augustine, Chrysostom and nineteenth-century writers. No Eastern Orthodox source beyond Chrysostom, no Catholic magisterial text, and no Jewish source on 15:29-39 was located. That is a sourcing gap, not a claim that those traditions are silent.
- **One quoted source contains an evident slip that this lesson does not rely on.** Works's comment gives the great commission as "(28:20)," where the command to make disciples is 28:19.
- **Crisis resources were checked on the organization's own page** on the access date: 988lifeline.org ("Call, text, or chat"). No other routes are given.
