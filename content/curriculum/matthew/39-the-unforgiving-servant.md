Matthew 18:15-35 closes Jesus' fourth discourse with two scenes about wrongs between disciples. In the first, Jesus gives steps for a brother who sins: "go and confront him privately" (18:15, BSB), then "take one or two others along" (18:16), then "tell it to the church" (18:17). If he will not listen even then, "regard him as you would a pagan or a tax collector." In the second, Peter asks, "Up to seven times?" (18:21), and Jesus answers, "not just seven times, but seventy-seven times!" (18:22), then tells of a servant forgiven "ten thousand talents" (18:24) who will not forgive "a hundred denarii" (18:28). This lesson checks two words some manuscripts lack, a line the BSB marks as from Deuteronomy, a number that can be read two ways, and the size of the debt. It reports four disputes without settling them and names one documented misuse plainly. Every BSB quotation below was checked by script against this app's own Bible files.

*A note before starting.* Parts of this passage, about confronting someone who has wronged you and about forgiving without limit, have been pressed on people who are being harmed at home or in a church. If someone is hurting you, a study note is not the help you need. In the United States, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is free and confidential: call 1-800-799-7233, text START to 88788, or visit thehotline.org. If you are in immediate danger, call 911 or your local emergency number. A pastor or counselor you trust, ideally someone outside any group that is pressing you, can also help.

## Context

**What happens, in order (18:15-35).** Jesus gives three steps (18:15-17), then sayings on binding and loosing (18:18), on two who "agree about anything you ask for" (18:19), and on His presence "where two or three gather together in My name" (18:20). Peter asks how often to forgive (18:21-22). In the parable a servant begs, "Have patience with me" (18:26), and the master "forgave his debt" (18:27); the same servant has a fellow servant "thrown into prison" (18:30) and is handed "over to the jailers to be tortured, until he should repay all that he owed" (18:34). Jesus closes: "That is how My heavenly Father will treat each of you unless you forgive your brother from your heart" (18:35).

**Where it sits.** The chapter opens with "Who then is the greatest" (18:1) and warns against causing "one of these little ones who believe in Me to stumble" (18:6); this curriculum's lesson on Matthew 18:1-14 covers that half. The NABRE notes say the chapter "is often called the 'church order' discourse, but it lacks most of the considerations usually connected with church order."

**"Against you" (18:15).** The BSB prints "If your brother sins against you," and its footnote on Bible Hub reads, "NE and WH do not include against you," that is, the Nestle and Westcott-Hort editions. On Bible Hub's parallel Greek page, the SBL text, the Byzantine Majority text, the Greek Orthodox text and both Textus Receptus editions read *eis se*, "against you"; the Nestle 1904, Westcott-Hort and Tischendorf texts omit it; the line labeled "Westcott and Hort / [NA27 variants]" prints it in brackets. The NABRE, which also brackets the words, notes that they "are widely attested but they are not in the important codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus or in some other textual witnesses. Their omission broadens the type of sin in question." The Pulpit Commentary reports that some editors omit them "on the ground that it is a gloss derived from Peter's question (ver. 21)," while "The words are retained by the Vulgate and other high authorities." Retaining them, it says, "seems to suit the context best." Meyer, who also keeps them, reports that Ch. W. Müller and Julius Müller "reject the reading," and asks, "How can it be supposed that the procedure here inculcated was intended to apply to every sin without any limitation whatever?" Calvin, who reads the words, takes them another way: "against thee" "does not denote an injury committed against any one, but distinguishes between secret and open sins." The app's shipped BSB file stores verse text only, so a learner reading 18:15 in the app sees "against you" with no sign that editions differ. Luke 17:3 has a similar split (see the connections below).

**"Two or three witnesses" (18:16).** The BSB puts the line in quotation marks, "‘every matter may be established by the testimony of two or three witnesses,’" with a footnote to Deuteronomy 19:15, which ends, "A matter must be established by the testimony of two or three witnesses" (BSB). Matthew's Greek is a shortened form of the Septuagint's (see the connection below). In Deuteronomy the rule is for a court: "A lone witness is not sufficient to establish any wrongdoing or sin against a man" (19:15). Poole: what was "the law of God in civil and judicial causes" God "would have observed in ecclesiastical causes." Chrysostom: "His meaning is, that you have done all your part." A script search finds "two or three witnesses" in six verses of the BSB: Deuteronomy 17:6 and 19:15, Matthew 18:16, 2 Corinthians 13:1, 1 Timothy 5:19 and Hebrews 10:28. Paul's form (2 Corinthians 13:1) is also in quotation marks in the BSB, and in Greek keeps the Septuagint's "and" (*kai*) and future verb, which Matthew lacks. In Matthew, "two or three" occurs only at 18:16 and 18:20.

**"Tell it to the church" (18:17).** "Church" occurs in the BSB Gospels three times in two verses, at 16:18 and twice at 18:17 (a script search). The NABRE notes call this "the second of the only two instances of this word in the gospels," and say that here "it refers not to the entire church of Jesus, as in Mt 16:18, but to the local congregation." Readers differ on what the first hearers pictured. Chrysostom: "that is, to the rulers of it." The Cambridge Bible: "either (1) the assembly or congregation of the Jewish synagogue, or rather, (2) the ruling body of the synagogue," adding, "But what was spoken of the Jewish Church was naturally soon applied to the Christian Church." Meyer rejects the synagogue reading and takes it as "the community of believers on Jesus." What "regard him as you would a pagan or a tax collector" requires is reported in Positions 1. In Matthew's BSB, "tax collector" or "tax collectors" occurs in eight verses (5:46; 9:10, 11; 10:3; 11:19; 18:17; 21:31, 32), and "pagan" or "pagans" in two (6:7; 18:17).

**Binding and loosing (18:18).** "Bound in heaven" occurs in the BSB only at 16:19 and 18:18 (a script search). This curriculum's lesson "Peter's Confession; the Way of the Cross" reports the rabbinic background of the phrase and how the traditions read it; that is not repeated here. The NABRE note on 18:18: "Except for the plural of the verbs bind and loose, this verse is practically identical with Mt 16:19b and many scholars understand it as granting to all the disciples what was previously given to Peter alone. For a different view, based on the different contexts of the two verses, see note on Mt 16:19." Chrysostom applies it to this context: "He did not say to the ruler of the church, Bind such a man, but, If you bind, committing the whole matter to the person himself, who is aggrieved."

**"Where two or three gather together in My name" (18:20).** A named parallel stands in the Mishnah. Pirkei Avot 3:2 (Sefaria's numbering) gives a saying of Rabbi Hananiah ben Teradion: "if two sit together and there are words of Torah [spoken] between them, then the Shekhinah abides among them," citing Malachi 3:16 (Kulp's translation). Avot 3:6 gives Rabbi Halafta's version, descending from ten to five, three, two and one: "when ten sit together and occupy themselves with Torah, the Shechinah abides among them," and then "How do we know that the same is true even of two?" Commentators on Matthew link the two. Gill quotes both sayings, citing "Pirke Abot, c. 3. sect. 6" and "Ib. sect. 2." The NABRE note numbers the saying "Pirqê ’Abôt 3, 3" and attributes it to "a rabbi executed in A.D. 135 at the time of the second Jewish revolt." Neither text cites the other. They share the small number and a promised presence; they differ on what gathers the two ("words of Torah"; "My name") and on who is present (the Shekhinah; "I"). The NABRE note adds that whether the prayer of 18:19 "has anything to do with the preceding judgment is uncertain."

**Peter's question and the number (18:21-22).** In the BSB, "seventy-seven" occurs in three verses: Genesis 4:24 ("seventy-sevenfold"), Judges 8:14 (a count of leaders) and Matthew 18:22. "Seventy times seven" does not occur in the BSB text; it is in the BSB footnote, "Or seventy times seven; see Genesis 4:24 LXX." The Greek is *hebdomekontakis hepta* in every edition Bible Hub lists. How to render it is Positions 2. The link to Lamech's boast, "If Cain is avenged sevenfold, then Lamech seventy-sevenfold" (Genesis 4:24), is already an authored connection in this curriculum (conn-gen4-lamech-matt18). The NABRE note: "the Greek corresponds exactly to the LXX of Gn 4:24. There is probably an allusion, by contrast, to the limitless vengeance of Lamech." It adds that the parable "really does not deal with repeated forgiveness, which is the point of Peter's question," which suggests to the note's authors "That the parable did not originally belong to this context."

**How large was the debt (18:24, 28)?** The BSB footnotes say: "A talent was worth about twenty years' wages for a laborer," and "A denarius was customarily a day's wage for a laborer." The NABRE calls the talent "a unit of coinage of high but varying value depending on its metal (gold, silver, copper) and its place of origin." Older commentators priced the sum in the money of their own day. Ellicott reckons "about £2,500,000 sterling"; the Cambridge Bible, "at least two million pounds of our money," adding, "It was probably more than the whole annual revenue of Palestine at this time" (citing Josephus, *Antiquities* 12.4.4, not read here). Bengel notes that "The Greek language cannot express by two words, as a distinct and continuous quantity, a larger sum than this," and that "A talent contains about six thousand denarii." On Bengel's figure, a reader can work out that the first debt is six hundred thousand times the second. That is this lesson's arithmetic, not his. The Expositor's Greek Testament warns that "exact calculations" are "idle or pedantic." Chrysostom reads the gap as that between sins "against man and against God." In the New Testament BSB, "talent" or "talents" occurs only at Matthew 18:24 and in Matthew 25:15-28 (a script search).

**"From your heart" (18:35).** In Matthew's BSB, forms of "forgive" (forgive, forgave, forgiven, forgiveness) occur 18 times in 13 verses. Four of them are in this passage (18:21, 27, 32, 35) and three in 6:12-15 (a script search). The Byzantine, Greek Orthodox and Textus Receptus texts end 18:35 with "their trespasses" (*ta paraptomata auton*), which the SBL, Nestle, Westcott-Hort and Tischendorf texts lack. The Cambridge Bible: "The MS. authority is against these words." Ellicott: they "have probably been added to make the verse correspond with Matthew 6:14-15" (see the connection below).

## Literary Design

Notes on how 18:15-35 is built, not on what the construction teaches.

**A widening circle.** The first scene moves outward in three steps: "privately," "one or two others," "the church" (18:15-17). Bengel: "The church is opposed to two or three in about the same proportion as two or three are to one." Its sayings then return to small numbers: "two of you" (18:19), "two or three" (18:20).

**A parable in three scenes, with one line repeated.** King and servant (18:23-27), servant and fellow servant (18:28-30), king and servant again (18:31-34). The plea "Have patience with me" occurs in the BSB only at 18:26 and 18:29 (a script search). The responses run in opposite directions: the master "had compassion on him" (18:27); the servant "refused" (18:30). The master's question, "Shouldn't you have had mercy on your fellow servant, just as I had on you?" (18:33), sets the two side by side.

## Positions

Four questions draw named, published disagreement. None is settled here; each reading is given in its holders' own words.

**1. What does "regard him as you would a pagan or a tax collector" require (18:17)?**

*Exclusion from the community.* The NABRE notes: "just as the observant Jew avoided the company of Gentiles and tax collectors, so must the congregation of Christian disciples separate itself from the arrogantly sinful member who refuses to repent." Such a one "is to be set outside the fellowship of the community," and "Paul makes a similar demand for excommunication in 1 Cor 5:1–13." Calvin: "the meaning is, that we ought to have no intercourse with the despisers of the Church till they repent." He adds that Christ "did not intend to enjoin them to avoid the society of heathens, of whom the Church was afterwards composed," and borrowed the phrase "from what was then customary among his nation." The Pulpit Commentary: "The obdurate offender must be deprived of Church membership," but "even in this extreme case charity will not regard the sinner as hopelessly lost; it will seek his salvation by prayer and entreaty." The Jehovah's Witnesses' study edition of the New World Translation glosses the phrase as "those with whom Jews had no unnecessary dealings," and cross-references 1 Corinthians 5:11, "With such a man do not even eat" (BSB).

*Withdrawal of fellowship, not a formal church sentence.* The Expositor's Greek Testament: the offender "puts himself outside the society," and to treat him as a heathen "does not mean with indifference or abhorrence, but carefully avoiding fellowship with him in sin, and seeking his good only as one without. There is no reference in this passage to ecclesiastical discipline and Church censures." Gill: "This is not a form of excommunication to be used among Christians," but "advice to the offended person, how to behave towards the offender"; he concludes that "their company is to be shunned, and intimate friendship with them to be avoided." Meyer: "Christ says nothing, as yet, about formal excommunication on the part of the church," though it was "a fair and necessary deduction from what he did say." Barnes: "cease to have religious contact with him, or to acknowledge him as a Christian brother," but "It does not mean that we should cease to show kindness to him and aid him in affliction or trial, for that is required toward all people." Ellicott: the offender "practically excommunicates himself," yet "they were still men, and, as such, entitled to courtesy and all kindly offices."

*Treat him as Jesus treated tax collectors.* Jesus ate with tax collectors (9:10-11) and was called "a friend of tax collectors and sinners" (11:19). The Cambridge Bible notes the irony without drawing a rule from it: "Jesus, the friend of publicans and sinners, uses the phrase of His contemporaries. What Jesus says, Matthew the publican records." A critic of the Witnesses' practice, writing as Meleti Vivlon on the Beroean Pickets site (2023), draws the rule: "The Pharisees shunned tax collectors. Jesus ate with them so as to win them over to God." He distinguishes removing a sinner "to protect the congregation from the leaven of sin" from what he calls "quite another thing": "to totally shun that person to the point of cutting them off from all social interaction," which he says is "something Jesus never taught."

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the exclusion reading, the Expositor's Greek Testament says there is "no reference" here to "Church censures," and Gill that it "is not a form of excommunication." Against the withdrawal reading, Meyer reports that "Calovius, in common with the majority of the older expositors, asserts that the institution of excommunication is, in the present passage, already expressly declared," and the NABRE says the sinner "is to be expelled." Against the third reading, Chrysostom observes that "everywhere He puts the publican for an example of the greatest wickedness," citing 5:46 and 21:31. This lesson does not decide among them.

**2. "Seventy-seven" or "seventy times seven" (18:22)?**

*Seventy-seven.* The BSB text and the NABRE read "seventy-seven times." Meyer: "seeing that we have ἑπτά, and not ἑπτάκις again, the rendering should simply be: till seventy-seven times." The phrasing follows "the LXX. Genesis 4:24," and he counts "Origen, Augustine, Bengel, Ewald, Hilgenfeld, Keim" on this side. Bengel: "The termination κις makes the whole number seventy-seven."

*Seventy times seven.* The BSB footnote gives "Or seventy times seven," which the Chrysostom and Calvin translations read here. The Expositor's Greek Testament decides "in favour of the latter as giving a number (490) practically equal to infinitude." It notes that "The majority adopt the opposite view" from Bengel's, "among whom may be named Grotius and Fritzsche, who cite the Syriac version in support." The Pulpit Commentary reports the Lamech reading, then: "But the genius of the language supports the rendering of the Authorized Version." Meyer lists "Jerome, Theophylact, Erasmus, Luther, Grotius, de Wette, Bleek" on this side.

**Objections each reading faces.** The Expositor's Greek Testament puts both at once: "On either view there is inexactness in the expression. Seventy times seven requires the termination -κις at both words. Seventy-seven times requires the -κις at the end of the second word rather than at end of first." Both sides agree on the point. Chrysostom: "not setting a number here, but what is infinite and perpetual and forever." The Cambridge Bible: "There is no limit to forgiveness." This lesson does not choose a rendering.

**3. Does 18:35 mean God's forgiveness can be withdrawn?**

*The forgiveness given can be revoked.* The NABRE note on 18:35: "The Father's forgiveness, already given, will be withdrawn at the final judgment for those who have not imitated his forgiveness by their own." Ellicott: "The parable teaches that the debt may come back. If faith does not work by love, it ceases to justify."

*The unforgiving show they were never truly forgiven.* Matthew Henry: "We are not to suppose that God actually forgives men, and afterwards reckons their guilt to them to condemn them; but this latter part of the parable shows the false conclusions many draw as to their sins being pardoned, though their after-conduct shows that they never entered into the spirit, or experienced the sanctifying grace of the gospel." Poole: those who do not forgive "may justly question whether God hath forgiven them," and their failure is "an ill evidence to our souls, that God hath not indeed forgiven us, as well as a bar against such forgiveness."

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the first, Henry's own sentence: "We are not to suppose that God actually forgives men, and afterwards reckons their guilt." Against the second, Ellicott writes that the words "cut through the meshes of many theological systems by which men have deceived themselves," among them "the self-assurance of justification." Both sides also meet a caution about pressing a parable's details. Poole: "similitudes run not on four feet." Calvin: "It would be an idle exercise of ingenuity to examine here every minute clause." This lesson does not decide what 18:35 implies about God's forgiveness.

**4. Does forgiving require reconciling, or repentance first?**

The passage says "forgive" and "from your heart" (18:35); Luke's parallel says "if he repents, forgive him" (Luke 17:3). Named readers separate questions the text leaves together.

*Two kinds of forgiving.* Calvin distinguishes them. In the first, "laying aside the desire of revenge, do not cease to love him," and "so far are we from having any right to wait till he who has offended shall return of his own accord to be reconciled to us." The second "is, when we receive a brother into favor." Repentance, he adds, "is a sacred thing, and therefore needs careful examination." Poole: "our Saviour by this precept doth not oblige any to take his enemy into his bosom, and make him his intimate or confidant again; but only to lay aside all malice, all thoughts and desires of revenge towards him."

*Forgiveness answering a request.* Barnes on 18:22: "As often as a brother injures us and asks forgiveness, we are to forgive him. It is, indeed, his duty to ask forgiveness, Luke 17:4." He adds: "If he does not ask us to forgive him, yet we are not at liberty to follow him with revenge and malice." Maimonides, writing for Jews in the *Mishneh Torah* (Laws of Repentance 2:9-10, Touger's translation), places the burden on the one who did wrong: he "must appease him and ask him to forgive him." If refused, he returns with "a group of three of his friends," up to three times; after that, "the person who refuses to grant forgiveness is the one considered as the sinner." The wronged person, in turn, "should be easily pacified, but hard to anger. When the person who wronged him asks for forgiveness, he should forgive him with a complete heart and a willing spirit." In the next sentence Maimonides contrasts this with gentiles, whom he describes as unforgiving; that ruling addresses his own community.

*A change of heart.* The Catechism of the Catholic Church quotes 18:35 and says: "It is not in our power not to feel or to forget an offense; but the heart that offers itself to the Holy Spirit turns injury into compassion and purifies the memory in transforming the hurt into intercession" (§2843).

*Forgiveness without reconciliation where there is abuse.* The National Domestic Violence Hotline, a secular service, asks, "Does forgiveness mean I have to see the abuser or reconcile with them?" and answers, "No, it does not." It says that "a survivor is not going to be in a place to forgive abuse if they are still in danger," and that "When abuse is involved, though, a face-to-face reconciliation is likely NOT safe." The U.S. Catholic bishops' *When I Call for Help* (2002) says: "Forgiveness, however, does not mean forgetting the abuse or pretending that it did not happen," and "Forgiveness is not permission to repeat the abuse."

**Objections each reading faces.** Against forgiving only on request, Calvin holds that "we ought to love those who deliberately provoke us, who spurn reconciliation." Against forgiving without repentance, Barnes cites Luke 17:4 and says it is the offender's "duty to ask forgiveness," and Maimonides frames forgiveness as the answer to a request. Against treating forgiveness as restored closeness, Poole says the precept does not require anyone to "take his enemy into his bosom." Against keeping the two wholly apart, Calvin's second kind is to "receive a brother into favor" once there is "probable evidence of conversion." This lesson does not decide among them.

**A misuse to name plainly.** Two parts of this passage have been turned against people being harmed. First, the call to forgive. *When I Call for Help* says: "Men who batter also cite Scripture to insist that their victims forgive them (see, for example, Mt 6:9-15). A victim then feels guilty if she cannot do so." The bishops cite Matthew 6, which this lesson connects to 18:35. They also say, "As bishops, we condemn the use of the Bible to support abusive behavior in any form," and "no person is expected to stay in an abusive marriage." Second, the steps of 18:15. Barrett Gritters, a professor at the Protestant Reformed Theological Seminary, writes in *The Standard Bearer* (2022): "It is a wrong, dangerous, and maybe fatal application of Matthew 18 to require one who has been abused sexually to confront the abuser privately before reporting the sin to anyone else, if at all." Lance Ford, a pastor writing in *The Roys Report* (2025), cites that outlet's earlier reporting: when a woman brought accusations against the founder of the International House of Prayer Kansas City, the ministry's first move was "to require her husband to confront Bickle privately, one-on-one, using Matthew 18." Ford calls this "a misapplication of Scripture that retraumatizes the wounded and shields the powerful."

Both writers answer it from the text. Gritters reads the chapter's first concern as "the calling to protect the sheep (the 'little ones' referred to in the earlier part of the chapter)," and argues that a victim cannot do "the main thing to be done: gain the brother," which "takes the work of the consistory and many others." Ford distinguishes 18:15-17, for "private offenses that can be resolved in quiet humility," from 1 Timothy 5:19-20, for sin "connected to leadership failure or abuse." Several things in the text can be checked directly. Matthew 18 opens by warning anyone who "causes one of these little ones who believe in Me to stumble" (18:6). The steps in 18:15-17 begin with the wronged person told to "go," and say nothing about staying in a place of harm. In the parable, the servant who will not forgive is the one who uses force: he "grabbed him and began to choke him" (18:28). None of the readings reported in Positions 1-4 is a misuse; holding any of them is different from using the passage to keep someone in danger or silent.

A reader working any of these questions through in their own life needs a pastor or counselor, or where there is danger the Hotline (above), not a study note.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, a model of the shape and not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 18:15: "If your brother sins against you, go and confront him privately. If he listens to you, you have won your brother over." (BSB)

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the first step is private, and the aim is gain, "you have won your brother over." Calvin reads it as "enjoining us to cover the faults of brethren, as far as lies in our power." Inference (this lesson's): for these disciples, the first move after a wrong between them was a private conversation aimed at restoring the relationship, before anyone else was told.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Where it is safe, an ordinary wrong between peers is first raised directly and privately with the person, with restoration as the goal.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: speaking to the person before speaking about the person, and aiming at repair. Not carried forward: any rule that a person harmed by abuse, crime or someone with power over them must confront that person alone, or must not report it.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: instruction to disciples about wrongs between them, functioning as a pattern for ordinary disputes. Promise scope: 18:15 promises nothing about the outcome; "If he listens" is a condition.

**Modern domain and situation.** Volunteer work. Two members of a church's food-pantry team are equals. One keeps reassigning the other's shifts without asking, then mentions it in the group chat as if it were agreed.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: a private conversation first. One faithful response: the volunteer asks the other for coffee, names the specific shifts, and listens; if nothing changes, they ask the coordinator to join a second conversation.

**Cautions.** This applies to ordinary disputes between peers. It does not apply to abuse, threats, crime or harm to a child. In those cases the right steps are safety, reporting to the proper authorities and outside help. In the United States, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is at 1-800-799-7233 (text START to 88788). If you are in immediate danger, call 911. This bridge is a worked example for the learner to test, not counsel for any particular person.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose.

1. With "against you," 18:15 concerns wrongs done to the reader; without it, any sin of a brother. Which reading fits better with Peter's "sins against me" in 18:21, and what would count as evidence either way?
2. Jesus says "regard him as you would a pagan or a tax collector," and Matthew, a tax collector, records it (10:3). How much should 9:10-11 and 11:19 shape the reading of 18:17?
3. The parable does not concern repeated forgiveness, as the NABRE notes. What does it add to Peter's question that "seventy-seven times" alone does not?
4. Calvin distinguishes laying aside revenge from receiving someone back into favor. Is that distinction in the text, or brought to it?
