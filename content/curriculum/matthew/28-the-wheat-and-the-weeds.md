Matthew 13:24-43 continues the day of parables by the sea: weeds sown among wheat, a mustard seed that becomes a tree, and leaven worked through flour. Matthew then adds a fulfillment formula and a line from Psalm 78, "I will open My mouth in parables; I will utter things hidden since the foundation of the world" (13:35, BSB), and in the house Jesus explains the weeds to His disciples. This lesson follows three threads. The first is the Psalm 78 quotation, checked line by line: Matthew follows the Greek Septuagint in one line and leaves it in the next. The second is a set of echoes without formulas: Daniel's and Ezekiel's great trees behind the mustard plant, and Daniel 12:3 behind "the righteous will shine like the sun" (13:43). The third is a dispute. "Let both grow together until the harvest" (13:30) has been read since at least the late fourth century (Chrysostom's homilies on Matthew; Augustine's letters, 402-412) as a ruling about who belongs in the church and about what may be done to dissenters, and it was cited both to defend and to condemn putting heretics to death. One note before starting: this passage speaks of burning and of "weeping and gnashing of teeth" (13:42), and its history includes coercion. Its words "Let both grow together" can also be pressed on someone as a reason to say nothing about harm. If you are being harmed by someone in a church or a family, the parable is not the place to settle what to do. The National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788) and the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline (1-800-422-4453) can help, and so can a pastor or counselor you trust.

## Context

**What happens, in order (13:24-43).** A man sows good seed, "his enemy came and sowed weeds among the wheat" (13:25, BSB), and the servants offer to pull them up. The owner answers, "'No,' he said, 'if you pull the weeds now, you might uproot the wheat with them. Let both grow together until the harvest'" (13:29-30). The mustard seed (13:31-32) and the leaven (13:33) follow, then Matthew's summary, "He did not tell them anything without using a parable" (13:34), and the quotation (13:35). Jesus "went into the house," and the disciples ask, "Explain to us the parable of the weeds in the field" (13:36). He explains it (13:37-43) and closes, "He who has ears, let him hear" (13:43), a call that occurs in Matthew's BSB at 11:15, 13:9 and 13:43 (a script search). The previous lesson, on 13:1-23, reports the dispute over why Jesus speaks in parables; it is not repeated here.

**The weeds and the leaven.** Ellicott identifies the weeds (*zizania*) as darnel, which "grew up at first with stalk and blade like the wheat; and it was not till fructification began that the difference was easily detected." The parable says the same: "When the wheat sprouted and bore grain, then the weeds also appeared" (13:26). Luke 13:18-21 has the mustard seed and the leaven together, and Mark 4:30-32 the mustard seed alone. Where the BSB says the woman "mixed" the leaven into the flour (13:33), the verb in the Nestle 1904 Greek is *enekrypsen*, "hid in." Chrysostom noticed it: "He said not, put, simply, but hid."

**The birds of the air (13:32), and three Old Testament trees.** The mustard plant "becomes a tree, so that the birds of the air come and nest in its branches" (13:32, BSB). Three Old Testament passages have a great tree with birds in its branches. In Nebuchadnezzar's dream, "in its branches the birds of the air nested" (Daniel 4:12, BSB), repeated at 4:21, where Daniel tells the king, "you, O king, are that tree!" (4:22). In Ezekiel 17:22-24 the Lord plants "a tender sprig" that becomes "a majestic cedar," and "Birds of every kind will nest under it, taking shelter in the shade of its branches" (17:23). In Ezekiel 31, Assyria is a cedar where "All the birds of the air nested in its branches" (31:6). In the BSB the form "nested" occurs only in Daniel 4:12 and 4:21, Ezekiel 31:6 and 31:13, and Luke 13:19 (a script search).

The Greek is mixed. The Septuagint text Bible Hub serves under Swete's name has Matthew's "the birds of the air" (*ta peteina tou ouranou*) at Daniel 4:12, 4:21 and Ezekiel 31:6, with a different verb for nesting. The Greek Daniel served by Blue Letter Bible has Matthew's verb (*kateskenoun*) and "in its branches" at 4:21, with another word for birds. In Ezekiel 17:23 the birds "rest" (*anapausetai*). The Pulpit Commentary says to compare Daniel 4:12, 21 and Ezekiel 17:23 "for both thought and language"; Bengel cites Ezekiel 17:23; Ellicott, "the imagery of Ezekiel 31:3; Daniel 4:10"; Scofield's notes say the figure "is drawn from Dan 4:20-22." What follows from the echo is disputed (Positions 2). The connections grade Daniel 4 and Ezekiel 17 as plausible allusions, because the image belongs to three passages and the wording cannot single out one.

**Psalm 78:2 at Matthew 13:35, line by line.** The formula is "So was fulfilled what was spoken through the prophet" (13:35, BSB). In Matthew's BSB, "spoken through the prophet" occurs eight times, and "So was fulfilled" only here and at 2:23 (a script search).

| | Matthew 13:35 (BSB) | Psalm 78:2 (BSB) | The Greek and Hebrew |
|---|---|---|---|
| Line 1 | "I will open My mouth in parables" | "I will open my mouth in parables" | Matthew's Greek, *anoixo en parabolais to stoma mou*, matches the Septuagint (Swete) word for word. The Hebrew has the singular *bemashal*, "in a parable" (NASB gloss on Bible Hub). |
| Line 2 | "I will utter things hidden since the foundation of the world" | "I will utter things hidden from the beginning" | The Septuagint's *phthenxomai problemata ap' arches* ("I will utter riddles from the beginning," this lesson's rendering) shares only the preposition *apo* (shortened to *ap'* in the Septuagint) with Matthew's *ereuxomai kekrymmena apo kataboles*; the verb, the noun and the time phrase all differ. The Hebrew noun is *chidot*, glossed "a riddle, an enigmatic, perplexing saying." |

The commentators on Bible Hub agree that line 2 comes from the Hebrew; they divide over line 1. The Pulpit Commentary: "The first clause of the quotation is verbally the same as the LXX." and "The second clause is different from the LXX., the first verb being a literal translation from the Hebrew, and the rest a paraphrase." Meyer: "the first half being according to the LXX., the second a free rendering of the Hebrew text." The Expositor's Greek Testament calls the second clause a "free translation from Hebrew." The Cambridge Bible dissents on line 1: "The quotation does not agree verbally with the LXX. It is a direct translation of the Hebrew," which does not fit line 1 in the Greek texts checked here. The phrase "things hidden" matches neither the Hebrew "riddles" nor the Greek *problemata*; the Pulpit Commentary suggests Matthew "has modified its language" to speak of what was hidden and is now told to the disciples.

**Two readings the Greek editions split on.** First, "of the world." The BSB's "since the foundation of the world" follows texts ending with *kosmou*: on Bible Hub's parallel page, the Byzantine Majority, Greek Orthodox and both Textus Receptus editions. The Nestle 1904, Westcott-Hort and Tischendorf texts end at *kataboles*, "since the foundation," and the NA27/UBS4 line brackets *kosmou*. Second, "Isaiah." Tischendorf's eighth edition alone on that page reads "through the prophet Isaiah" (*dia tou prophetou Esaiou*). Ellicott reports the reading in the Sinaitic manuscript; he judges "there is no reason for receiving it as the original reading" and suspects a copyist "misled by the word 'prophet,'" but adds, "If the mistake had been St. Matthew's, it would stand on the same footing as the substitution of Jeremiah for Zechariah in Matthew 27:9." The Pulpit Commentary leans the other way and quotes the Westcott-Hort Appendix: "It is difficult not to think Ἠσαίου genuine." (It prints the name as "Dr. Herr"; in context it is evidently Hort.) This lesson reports both splits and settles neither.

**Which prophet, and what kind of fulfillment?** The psalm's heading is "A Maskil of Asaph" (78:1). The BSB calls him "Asaph the seer" (2 Chronicles 29:30), and there the Septuagint (Swete) reads *Asaph tou prophetou*, "Asaph the prophet," as Meyer notes. Gill adds 1 Chronicles 25, where Asaph "prophesied under the direction of the king" (25:2, BSB), and concludes he "is very rightly called a prophet by the evangelist." The psalm itself retells Israel's history from Egypt to David. Ellicott says "The Psalm itself was in no sense predictive," and that "the occurrence of the word was enough" for Matthew. Poole: "Nor was it fulfilled as the type in the antitype, but as a thing of the same nature was done." Calvin: Matthew "does not mean, that the psalm, which he quotes, is a prediction which relates peculiarly to Christ, but that, as the majesty of the Spirit was displayed in the discourse of the Prophet, in the same manner was his power manifested in the discourse of Christ." Meyer: "The passage, however, is not a prophecy so far as its historical meaning is concerned, but only according to the typical reference which the evangelist discerns in it"; Matthew, he adds, uses "in parables" "in a special sense, and one that is foreign to the original Hebrew." They agree that Matthew is quoting the psalm and differ over what "fulfilled" means. They also name the "things hidden" differently: for Poole, "the great and mysterious things of the gospel"; for the Pulpit Commentary, the truths of the parables just told and their private explanation to the disciples. The verse itself does not say.

**The explanation (13:36-43).** "The field is the world" (13:38); "The harvest is the end of the age, and the harvesters are angels" (13:39). The angels "will weed out of His kingdom every cause of sin and all who practice lawlessness" (13:41). On who the weeds are, Ellicott writes: "It was, perhaps, natural that theologians, who saw in heresy the greatest of all evils, should identify the tares with heretics. So far as heresy rises from the spirit of self-will, or antagonism to righteousness, we may admit that they are included in the class, but the true definition is that given in Matthew 13:41." Zephaniah 1:3 is discussed under Teach-Back 5.

**"the fiery furnace, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth" (13:42).** In the BSB, "fiery furnace" occurs only in Psalm 21:9, Daniel 3 (eight verses) and Matthew 13:42 and 13:50, and "weeping and gnashing of teeth" six times in Matthew and once in Luke (a script search). Meyer: "The furnace (Daniel 3:6) represents Gehenna." The Catechism of the Catholic Church quotes 13:41-42 in its teaching on hell (§1034). What the fire means for the final state of the lost is a named dispute, reported in this curriculum's Matthew 10:16-42 lesson and not argued again here. That lesson's pastoral word applies: a reader who meets these verses with grief or fear should talk with a pastor who knows them, and with a licensed counselor as well if the fear persists.

**"the righteous will shine like the sun" (13:43).** Daniel 12:3 reads, "Then the wise will shine like the brightness of the heavens" (BSB), right after "some to everlasting life, but others to shame and everlasting contempt" (12:2). Calvin: "our Lord unquestionably refers here to a passage in Daniel." The Pulpit Commentary: "An undoubted reference to the substance of Daniel 12:3." Matthew's verb, *eklampsousin*, stands in the Greek Daniel served by Blue Letter Bible, though not in Bible Hub's Swete text (*phanousin*); Bengel notes the verb is "employed by the LXX. in Daniel 12:3." Its presence in only one Greek form of Daniel leaves open which text Matthew knew. Ellicott is more cautious ("The imagery is so natural that we hardly need to look for any reference to older teaching") before naming Daniel 12:3, and MacLaren sets Judges 5:31 ("shine like the sun" in the BSB) and Daniel 12:3 beside 13:43 as instances of a common metaphor.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 13:24-43 is built, not on what the construction is meant to teach.

**Crowd, then house.** Three parables go to the crowd (13:24-33), a summary and the quotation close the public part (13:34-35), and the explanation is given in "the house" (13:36) to the disciples alone, so the weeds parable and its explanation are split. Chrysostom first lays down that "the parables must not be explained throughout word for word, since many absurdities will follow," and then points to this explanation as an example: Jesus "says not at all who the servants are."

## Positions

Two disputes are reported in their holders' own words and not resolved, with the history of the first.

**1. "Let both grow together until the harvest" (13:30): the church, the world and the sword.** Readers divide over two questions. Is the field the church, so that the parable describes a mixed church? Or is it the world, so that the church may still separate from open sinners while the world is left to God's harvest? And what does "Let both grow" forbid: separation from the church, the killing of heretics, any coercion, or civil punishment for religion at all? Readings are given in historical order.

*John Chrysostom (Homily 46 on Matthew).* Chrysostom reads the weeds as heretics: "here He means the societies of the heretics." He holds that the parable forbids killing them: "For it is not right to put a heretic to death, since an implacable war would be brought into the world." He names "two reasons": "one, that the wheat be not hurt; another, that punishment will surely overtake them, if incurably diseased." His next sentence limits the ruling: "He does not therefore forbid our checking heretics, and stopping their mouths, and taking away their freedom of speech, and breaking up their assemblies and confederacies, but our killing and slaying them."

*Augustine against the Donatists (Letters 76, 93 and 133).* The Donatists were a church in North Africa that had separated from the wider church and held that the true church survived in their communion. Augustine answered with this parable. In Letter 76 (402), addressed to them, he writes, "For if you were wheat, you would bear with the tares, and not separate yourselves from that which is growing in Christ's field." He turns 13:38 against their claim: "He said, The field is the world; He said not, The field is Africa." In Letter 93 (408) he says the church is the good seed Christ "has foretold that it should grow among the tares until the harvest." The same letter defends imperial pressure on the Donatists, and Augustine explains his change of view: "originally my opinion was, that no one should be coerced into the unity of Christ, that we must act only by words, fight only by arguments," but "this opinion of mine was overcome... by the conclusive instances to which they could point." In Letter 133 (412), which does not cite the parable, he asks that Donatists who had confessed to killing a Catholic priest not be put to death in retaliation ("we do not wish to have the sufferings of the servants of God avenged by... retaliation"), while granting that he "might silently pass over the execution of criminals" tried on others' charges, and still wanting them restrained "by such coercive measures as may be in accordance with the laws."

*John Calvin (Reformed; Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists).* Calvin reads the field as the church: "though Christ afterwards adds that the field is the world, yet he undoubtedly intended to apply this designation, in a peculiar manner, to the Church." Its purpose, he says, is "that the children of God may be armed with patience" while "bad men and hypocrites will mingle" in the church. He does not take it as a bar on discipline: "Pastors ought to labor strenuously to purify the Church," though they "will not succeed in such a manner as to purify the Church entirely from every defilement." He restricts the toleration to morals: "we are not at liberty to extend such a toleration to wicked errors, which corrupt the purity of faith." He also rejects one use of the passage: "This passage has been most improperly abused by the Anabaptists, and by others like them, to take from the Church the power of the sword." He asks, "why may not godly magistrates, when necessity calls for it, use the sword against wicked men?" and answers the Anabaptist rejoinder: "They reply that, when the punishment is not capital, there is room allowed for repentance; as if the thief on the cross (Luke 23:42) did not find the means of salvation." His next sentence narrows the point: "I shall satisfy myself with replying, that Christ does not now speak of the office of pastors or of magistrates."

*The Catholic Church (Catechism §827; Dignitatis Humanae, 1965).* The Catechism applies the parable to sin in every member of the church: "All members of the Church, including her ministers, must acknowledge that they are sinners. In everyone, the weeds of sin will still be mixed with the good wheat of the Gospel until the end of time." On coercion, the Second Vatican Council's declaration on religious freedom cites the parable in support of that freedom: Christ, "noting that the cockle had been sown amid the wheat, gave orders that both should be allowed to grow until the harvest time" (§11, citing Matthew 13:30 and 40-42). In §12 it says that "the doctrine of the Church that no one is to be coerced into faith has always stood firm." The sentence before that one acknowledges that "there has at times appeared a way of acting that was hardly in accord with the spirit of the Gospel or even opposed to it."

*An Anabaptist reading (Menno Simons, apparently from his reply to Gellius Faber, 1554).* Menno answers a Reformed opponent who had cited the parable for a mixed church. He insists on the explanation in 13:38: "Christ says, It is the world, and not the church, as Gellius claims." On his reading, wheat and weeds "grow together in the same field, namely, in the world," and the owner "will not have them destroyed by rooting them up." The net and the other mixed parables, he grants, were spoken "in allusion to the church, yet they were not spoken for the purpose that the church should knowingly and willfully accept and suffer open transgressors," but "because many intermix with the christians, in semblance only." He adds a complaint from experience: if the preachers understood the parable, "they would not cry so loudly against us, who, alas, are every where called tares, heretics."

*A Separatist reading (Roger Williams, The Bloudy Tenent, 1644).* Williams prints and defends a paper by a prisoner in Newgate, who argued that Christ "commandeth, that the tares and wheat... should be let alone in the world, and not plucked up until the harvest, which is the end of the world." The prisoner's reason: "they who now are tares, may hereafter become wheat." Williams's opponent, John Cotton, had replied that the tares are hypocrites and errors that "come very near the truth," and that only in such cases does Christ call "for toleration, not for penal prosecution." Williams answers under the heading "The field by most, generally, but falsely, interpreted the church." For Williams the tares are false worshippers in the world, whom Christ "commands a permission of... in the world, until the time of the end of the world." Offenders against "the good and peace" of the civil state, such as murderers and thieves, are not "to be let alone" but "seasonably to be suppressed."

**The history: the parable for and against the stake.** The same verses were cited on both sides of the question of executing heretics.

- *For.* Thomas Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 11, a. 3) raises the parable as an objection to his own view: "the Master commanded his servants (Matthew 13:30) to suffer the cockle 'to grow until the harvest'... Therefore heretics should be tolerated." He answers that heretics may be "not only excommunicated but even put to death," and that this "is not contrary to Our Lord's command, which is to be understood as referring to the case when the cockle cannot be plucked up without plucking up the wheat." In the body of the article he adds, "On the part of the Church, however, there is mercy which looks to the conversion of the wanderer, wherefore she condemns not at once, but 'after the first and second admonition.'" Protestant tribunals also sentenced heretics to death, and Catholics and Protestants alike persecuted the Anabaptists (World History Encyclopedia, tertiary).
- *Against.* In 1524, the year before he joined the Anabaptists, Balthasar Hubmaier quoted "Let both grow together till the harvest" in *Concerning Heretics and Those Who Burn Them* (Vedder's translation) and concluded: "Hence it follows that the Inquisitors are the greatest heretics of all, since, against the doctrine and example of Christ, they condemn heretics to fire, and before the time of harvest root up the wheat with the tares." Hubmaier was burned for heresy in 1528 (as World History Encyclopedia reports). Menno and Williams belong to the same side of this argument.
- *Later Catholic teaching.* *Dignitatis Humanae* (1965), quoted above, reads the parable for religious freedom. In 2000 the International Theological Commission's *Memory and Reconciliation* quoted Pope John Paul II's apostolic letter *Tertio millennio adveniente* (§35) on "the acquiescence given, especially in certain centuries, to intolerance and even the use of force in the service of truth," and included "all forms of force used in the repression and correction of errors" (§5.3).

Calvin's passage defends the magistrate's sword, capital cases included, against the Anabaptist use of the parable, though he says the parable does not address it.

*Where the readings divide.* On the field, Calvin reads the church, the Catechism speaks of the weeds of sin in every member, Menno and Williams insist on the world, and Augustine uses both (the church grows "among the tares" in "this world"). On the command, Chrysostom forbids killing but allows silencing; Augustine accepts coercion and, in one case, pleads against execution; Calvin keeps the magistrate's sword and says the parable does not address it; Williams excludes civil punishment for religion but not for civil crimes; the Second Vatican Council reads the parable for religious freedom. This lesson does not decide among them.

**2. The mustard tree and the leaven: growth of the kingdom, or growth of corruption?** Some readers take both parables as pictures of the kingdom's growth from small beginnings; others read them as warnings.

*The growth reading.* Chrysostom: "For as this converts the large quantity of meal into its own quality, even so shall you convert the whole world." Calvin says Christ "encourages his disciples not to be offended and turn back on account of the mean beginnings of the Gospel." He meets the other uses of leaven directly: "The word leaven is sometimes taken in a bad sense," citing 16:11 and 1 Corinthians 5:6, "But here the term must be understood simply as applying to the present subject." Ellicott grants that leaven is "commonly, as in the Passover ritual, the symbol of malice and wickedness," but says here it is "the type of influence for good as well as evil," and points to "the leavened loaves that were offered on the day of Pentecost (Leviticus 23:17)." On the birds, Ellicott calls them "no longer, as before, the emblems of evil," and the Pulpit Commentary says, "There is not necessarily any connotation of evil about these (cf. vers. 4, 19)."

*The warning reading.* The Scofield Reference Notes argue that leaven "is the principle of corruption working subtly; is invariably used in a bad sense... and is defined by our Lord as evil doctrine," citing 16:11-12 and Mark 8:15. On this reading the parable "constitutes a warning that the true doctrine... would be mingled with corrupt and corrupting false doctrine." Scofield reads the mustard tree as "the rapid but unsubstantial growth" of the kingdom's outward form, and of the birds' shelter, drawn from Daniel 4, says, "How insecure was such a refuge the context in Daniel shows." Arno Gaebelein describes the two parables as foretelling "the external and internal development of the kingdom of the heavens," a progress that "is an unnatural and evil progress." He notes that he stands against the majority: "All the leading commentators of the Bible have accepted" the other explanation.

*Objections each reading faces.* Against the warning reading, Ellicott cites Leviticus 23:17, where the firstfruits loaves are "baked with leaven" (BSB), and Calvin holds that the figure takes its sense from "the present subject." Against the growth reading, Scofield raises three objections. Such a reading "does violence to the unvarying symbolical meaning of leaven," pointing to Matthew 16:6-12. And a world wholly leavened "is explicitly contradicted by our Lord's interpretation of the parables of the Wheat and Tares, and of the Net," which picture good and bad together until the end. And the kingdom's method of extension "is by sowing seed, not by mingling leaven." This lesson does not decide what "like leaven" carries.

**A pastoral note.** Some readers belong to a church in conflict and hear "Let both grow together" pressed into an argument about a particular person. The readers above disagree about church discipline, and none of their words is a ruling on anyone in your congregation; a pastor who knows the situation is the right person to talk with. If someone in a church is harming you or another person, that is a matter for the civil authorities and qualified help, whatever one thinks the parable says about the church, and the hotlines named at the start of this lesson are there for that.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, a model of the shape and not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 13:28-29: "So the servants asked him, 'Do you want us to go and pull them up?' 'No,' he said, 'if you pull the weeds now, you might uproot the wheat with them.'" (BSB)

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: by the time the servants ask (13:27-28), the weeds have "appeared" and can be told apart; they ask before acting, and the owner gives a reason, the risk to the wheat (13:29). Inference (this lesson's): for hearers who knew fields, the picture was of a removal whose cost to the wheat the owner judged too high before the harvest.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Zeal to remove what looks wrong can damage what is good, and the cost to others belongs in the reckoning before acting.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the servants' readiness to ask, and the owner's reason. Not carried forward: any rule about church discipline or civil law, which is the dispute in Positions 1, and any claim that harmful conduct should be left alone. The owner's reason concerns damage to the crop, and the parable says nothing about protecting people from harm.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: an explained parable, functioning as a description of the present age and an answer to the servants' question. It is not a general command never to act. Promise scope: the parable describes a harvest at "the end of the age" (13:39). It does not promise that anyone who now looks like wheat or weeds will stay so.

**Modern domain and situation.** Church and community life. A volunteer coordinator learns that a newer member, eager to serve, has a rough past. Another volunteer urges her to keep him off every team because his faith "can't be real."

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: restraint, weighing the cost to others, with ordinary responsibility kept. One faithful response: she does not act on the rumor or pronounce on his heart, since excluding him on that basis could damage him and the team. She applies the same standards to him that she applies to everyone, including any screening her church requires for work with children, and she deals with conduct she can actually see through her church's normal process.

**Cautions.** This parable is not a reason to ignore safety rules or to keep quiet about abuse; screening and reporting are not "pulling up weeds," and if a child or adult may be at risk, the civil authorities and the hotlines above come first. Nor is it a license to treat a person as "weeds" on rumor. This bridge is a worked example for the learner to test, not counsel for any particular person.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose.

1. Matthew's first line of Psalm 78:2 matches the Greek word for word, but his second line matches neither the Greek nor, on some commentators' view, the Hebrew closely. If Matthew shaped the second line himself, what would "fulfilled" have to mean? Compare Calvin, Poole and Meyer.
2. "The field is the world" (13:38). Augustine used that sentence against the Donatists, and Menno and Williams used it against the reading of the field as the church. What else in 13:36-43 (for example "weed out of His kingdom," 13:41) would you weigh before deciding what the field is?
3. Chrysostom forbids killing heretics but allows "stopping their mouths." Williams forbids civil punishment for religion but keeps it for theft and murder. Where would you draw the line, and on what words in 13:28-30?
4. Daniel's tree is cut down; Ezekiel's cedar is planted by God. If the birds in 13:32 echo one of them, does the echo bring that passage's meaning with it, or only its picture?
