Matthew 17:14-27 follows Jesus down from the mountain of the Transfiguration. At its foot a father kneels and begs, "Lord, have mercy on my son" (17:15, BSB), whom the disciples "could not heal" (17:16). Jesus heals the boy, and when the disciples ask privately why they failed, he answers, "Because you have so little faith" (17:20). In Galilee he foretells his death a second time, and "the disciples were deeply grieved" (17:23). In Capernaum collectors ask Peter whether his teacher pays "the two drachmas" (17:24), and the scene ends with a coin in a fish's mouth. This lesson checks what the text says, in this app's BSB and, where the English hides something, in the Greek; grades five threads to other passages; and reports four disputes without settling them. Two of them touch people today, those who live with epilepsy and those who prayed for healing that did not come, and the lesson names how this passage has been used against both.

## Context

**A note before reading, for anyone who lives with seizures.** This passage describes a child with seizures who falls into fire and water, and some readers will come to it with a diagnosis of their own or a child's. Nothing in this lesson suggests that epilepsy, or any seizure disorder, is a sign of demonic activity or a lack of faith. If someone is having a seizure and is in danger, or a seizure does not stop, call 911 in the United States or your local emergency number. The Epilepsy Foundation's helpline answers questions about epilepsy and seizures at 1-800-332-1000 (English) or 1-866-748-8008 (Spanish), weekdays 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. Eastern, as its own site gives the hours. If you are in emotional crisis, you can call or text 988 at any hour in the United States. A doctor, a counselor or a pastor who knows you can help in ways a study note cannot.

**"He has seizures" (17:15).** The BSB's "He has seizures" renders one Greek verb, *selēniazetai*, built on *selēnē*, "moon." All nine editions on Bible Hub's parallel page read it. Thayer's lexicon gives the literal sense, "to be moon-struck (cf. lunatic)," and the meaning "to be epileptic (epilepsy being supposed to return and increase with the increase of the moon)," and lists only two New Testament uses, Matthew 4:24 and 17:15. A case-insensitive script over this app's BSB likewise finds "seizures" in only those two verses. At 4:24 the BSB lists "the demon-possessed, those having seizures, and the paralyzed" as separate groups, as the Greek does. Older English kept the moon ("he is lunatick," KJV, as Bible Hub prints it).

**What Matthew's own words say about the cause.** Two statements stand in Matthew's text. The father uses the seizure word (17:15). The narrator says that "Jesus rebuked the demon, and it came out of the boy" (17:18). Matthew's telling is the shortest of the three. Mark's father speaks of "a spirit that makes him mute" and says the boy "foams at the mouth, gnashes his teeth, and becomes rigid" (Mark 9:17-18, BSB); Luke's says, "A spirit keeps seizing him" (Luke 9:39, BSB). How readers relate the seizures to the demon is Position 1.

**The disciples' failure.** "Little faith" occurs in five verses of Matthew in this app's BSB (6:30, 8:26, 14:31, 16:8, 17:20; a case-insensitive script), and this curriculum's lesson on Matthew 14:22-36 discussed the word. At 17:20 the Greek editions split: SBL, Nestle 1904, Westcott-Hort and Tischendorf read *oligopistian*, "little faith," and the Byzantine, Greek Orthodox and Textus Receptus editions read *apistian*, "unbelief" (Bible Hub's parallel page). Ellicott, following "unbelief," calls "little faith" "an example of a tendency to tone down the apparent severity of our Lord's words." The Expositor's Greek Testament prefers it as "a word coined to express the fact exactly: too little faith for the occasion."

**A mustard seed and a mountain (17:20).** "Mustard seed" occurs in Matthew only here and at 13:31 (a script; see the connection). Luke 17:6 has faith "the size of a mustard seed" telling "this mulberry tree" to be uprooted, and Matthew 21:21 has a second mountain saying: "even if you say to this mountain, 'Be lifted up and thrown into the sea,' it will happen" (BSB). Calvin calls the saying "a hyperbolical mode of expression" and adds, "He does not mean that God will give us every thing that we may mention." The Cambridge Bible: "To 'remove mountains' is to make difficulties vanish." Paul's "if I have absolute faith so as to move mountains, but have not love, I am nothing" (1 Corinthians 13:2, BSB) uses the same picture; Origen thought Paul was "taking... his starting-point from this place."

**A verse the app's Bible leaves empty (17:21).** In this app's BSB file, Matthew 17:21 is an empty string (checked by script), so a learner sees the verse numbers jump from 20 to 22. The app stores no footnotes. Bible Hub's printing of the BSB gives one: "BYZ and TR include 21But this kind does not come out except by prayer and fasting; see Mark 9:29." Bible Hub's parallel Greek page prints the verse in the Byzantine, Greek Orthodox and both Textus Receptus editions, and the SBL, Nestle 1904, Westcott-Hort and Tischendorf editions omit it. Mark 9:29 splits the same way: the BSB reads "except by prayer," and the same four editions add *kai nēsteia*, "and fasting." Position 3 takes up the question.

**The second prediction (17:22-23).** "The Son of Man is about to be delivered into the hands of men. They will kill Him, and on the third day He will be raised to life" (17:22-23). This is the second of three such sayings (16:21; 17:22-23; 20:18-19; see the connection on 16:21). Mark's version ends "after three days He will rise" and adds, "But they did not understand this statement, and they were afraid to ask Him about it" (Mark 9:31-32, BSB). Matthew alone says, "the disciples were deeply grieved" (17:23); the phrase recurs in Matthew only at the Last Supper (26:22). Chrysostom explains the grief by partial understanding: "that He was to die they knew," but not "what this resurrection might be." Calvin says "the immoderate dread of the cross" "shut the door against the consolation which was immediately added."

**The two-drachma tax (17:24).** The BSB's "two-drachma tax" translates *ta didrachma*, as its footnote says; the phrase occurs nowhere else in the BSB, and Matthew does not explain it. Named readers identify it as the yearly half-shekel for the temple and trace it to Exodus 30, where each man counted "must pay a half shekel, according to the sanctuary shekel," as "a ransom for his life" (30:12-13, BSB). Josephus says the shekel "is equal to four Athenian drachmae" (*Antiquities* 3.8.2), so a half-shekel was two. Later texts speak of "the tax imposed by Moses the servant of God on Israel in the wilderness" (2 Chronicles 24:9, BSB) and of "a third of a shekel yearly" (Nehemiah 10:32, BSB). Readers differ on the history. Gill holds that Exodus 30 "does not appear to have been designed for a perpetual law." Chrysostom traces the payment instead to the redemption of the firstborn. Calvin agrees on Exodus 30 but holds that the Jews by then "paid to tyrants what they were formerly in the habit of paying to God alone." Meyer, the Pulpit Commentary and Jamieson-Fausset-Brown hold that it is the temple tax, not a Roman one; the Pulpit Commentary notes that the collectors "are not τελῶναι, publicans."

**After the temple fell.** Josephus reports that Vespasian "laid a tribute upon the Jews wheresoever they were, and enjoined every one of them to bring two drachmae every year into the Capitol, as they used to pay the same to the temple at Jerusalem" (*Jewish War* 7.6.6). Ellicott, Meyer and the Cambridge Bible cite this at 17:24. The Cambridge Bible leaves a question open: "It would be interesting to know whether the Jewish Christians continued to pay the Temple-tax in accordance with this precedent." Whether Matthew's first readers heard the scene against the Roman tax is not argued in any source read here, and the lesson leaves it open.

**The fish that is never caught (17:27).** Peter is told he "will find a four-drachma coin" in the first fish's mouth (17:27); the BSB footnote gives the Greek, "a stater; that is, a silver coin worth approximately one shekel," enough for two. Then the chapter ends. Matthew does not say that Peter went, caught a fish or found a coin. Ellicott writes, "There is no actual record that a miracle was wrought at all. We expect the narrative to end with the words, 'and he went and found as it had been said unto him,' but we do not find them." He reports both responses: some read the words as "figurative language" for catching and selling a fish, but "Most interpreters, however, have been content to take our Lord's words in their literal sense, and to believe that they were literally fulfilled." The Expositor's Greek Testament concludes, "In any case the miracle, not being reported as having happened, cannot have been the important point for the evangelist." Meyer holds that "the incident must continue to be regarded as in every way as historical as the evangelist meant it to be," and Chrysostom reads it as showing "the sea also to be under His rule." This lesson does not decide whether the silence matters.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 17:14-27 is built, not on what the construction is meant to teach.

**Down from the mountain.** The unit begins as Jesus comes down from the mountain (17:9) "to the crowd" (17:14), and six verses later he speaks of "this mountain" (17:20). Ellicott suggests the words were "dramatised by a gesture pointing to the mountain from which our Lord and the three disciples had descended"; the text names no mountain. Bengel, noting that "a more grievous instance of human unbelief and misery" met Jesus below, writes "cf. Exodus 32:19," where Moses comes down to the golden calf (see Teach-Back 5).

**Three predictions, three reactions.** Peter's rebuke follows the first prediction (16:22), grief the second (17:23), and the request of the mother of James and John the third (20:20-21). Setting the reactions side by side is this lesson's observation; Matthew does not comment on them.

**From "sons" to "greatest."** The chapter ends with "the sons" (17:26), and the next verse asks, "Who then is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven?" (18:1, BSB). Chrysostom links them: the question came because the disciples saw Peter honored in the words "for me and you."

## Positions

Four questions here draw named, published disagreement. None is settled here. Readings are given in their holders' own words where read directly, and marked secondhand where not.

**1. How do the seizures and the demon relate (17:15-18)?** The commentators below all accept Matthew's account as a real healing. They differ over whether the condition was demonic only, a natural disease with a demonic element, or a disease people of the time attributed to a demon.

*A demon that used the moon's name.* Chrysostom (Homily 57) treats the condition as possession, and the moon-word as a mistake that the demon itself encouraged: "if he call him a lunatic, trouble not yourself at all, for it is the father of the possessed who speaks the word." The evil spirit, "to bring a reproach upon nature, both attacks them that are seized, and lets them go, according to the courses of the moon; not as though that were the worker of it." Origen (*Commentary on Matthew* XIII.6) reports the medical view of his own day and rejects it: "Let physicians then, discuss the physiology of the matter, inasmuch as they think that there is no impure spirit in the case, but a bodily disorder." Of himself and those who "believe the Gospel," he says the sickness is "effected by an impure dumb and deaf spirit," which "watches certain configurations of the moon."

*A natural disease with a demonic element.* Calvin rejects Chrysostom's explanation: "I do not admit the fanciful notion of Chrysostom, that the word lunatic was invented by a trick of Satan... for we learn from undoubted experience, that the course of the moon affects the increase or decline of these diseases." He continues, "And yet this does not prevent Satan from mixing up his attacks with natural means," and concludes that, "as the weakness of his brain and nerves made him liable to epilepsy, Satan availed himself of this for aggravating the disease." The Pulpit Commentary: "It was in truth epilepsy accompanied by or occasioned by demoniacal possession."

*Epilepsy described in the terms of its time.* Meyer writes that the boy, "whose malady was regarded as the result of demoniacal possession," "was evidently suffering from epilepsy." The Expositor's Greek Testament says the symptoms "are those of epilepsy, which were supposed to become aggravated with the phases of the moon." Neither, in the notes read, says whether a demon was also present. Ellicott identifies the symptoms as epilepsy and says that whether there was "something more than disease, viz., a distinct possession by a supernatural force, is a question which belongs to the general subject of the 'demoniacs'"; he adds that Jesus' words at 17:21 "assume... the reality of the possession," a point that depends on Position 3. The only voices read for this lesson who hold that no spirit was involved at all are the physicians Origen describes, and they are known only through him.

**Objections each reading faces.** Against possession only, Calvin takes the moon-word as an observation of a real natural disease. Against Calvin's mixed reading, Origen holds that the moon "has no power to originate such a disorder." Against reading it as epilepsy in the terms of its time, Matthew's narrator, not only the father, says Jesus "rebuked the demon, and it came out" (17:18). This lesson does not decide among these readings.

**A misuse to name.** Whatever a reader concludes about this boy, the passage has a history outside the commentaries. Lauren Gravitz's survey in *Nature* (2014) says that "in almost every culture, epilepsy has been viewed as something to be feared, avoided and concealed," mentions this healing alongside an ancient Babylonian text that blamed seizures on "ghosts and demons," and quotes the neurologist Gregory Bergey: "That's what led, centuries ago, to the idea that someone was possessed." Such beliefs persist. In some low-income settings, the neurologist Gretchen Birbeck says, when someone "has a seizure and falls to the floor, they tend to think there's a possession or that the person's been cursed." Gravitz reports that "feelings of shame have been strongly associated with inadequate seizure control, as well as with increased depression, anxiety and isolation." The Epilepsy Foundation lists as a myth that "Epilepsy has a spiritual–not a physical cause," and answers, "Epilepsy is a medical condition and seizures are the outward sign." No source read here documents a particular writer using Matthew 17 itself this way, so the lesson names the pattern only as these sources describe it. Every reader quoted above writes about one boy in one Gospel scene; even Calvin, who sees a demon at work here, ascribes the liability to epilepsy to "the weakness of his brain and nerves." None of these readings is a diagnosis of anyone today. If you or someone you love has seizures, a doctor is the person to see.

**2. Who is the "unbelieving and perverse generation" (17:17), and whose faith fell short?** In Matthew, the explanation of the failure is given privately to the disciples, in answer to their own question (17:19-20). Whom the exclamation at 17:17 addresses is not stated, and readers divide.

*The disciples.* Meyer holds that Jesus "is aiming at His disciples," because 17:20 blames their faith, and not at the father, who "invoked the help of Jesus because he had faith in Him." He notes that some who refer the words to the father and the people go so far as to say the disciples are "exculpated, and the blame of the failure imputed to the father himself." Bengel: "By a severe rebuke the disciples are reckoned as a part of the multitude."

*The father, the crowd or the scribes.* Gill holds the words are spoken "Not to the disciples, but to the father of the child... and those that were with him, and the Scribes that were present," since "faithless" is "a way of speaking, which is never used of the disciples." Calvin refers them to the scribes, "for it is certain that the reproof is directed, not against ignorant and weak persons, but against those who, through inveterate malice, obstinately resist God." Chrysostom (Homily 57) reads the father, from Mark's account, as "exceedingly weak in faith," but asks in the next sentence, "Yet if his unbelief was the cause... why does He blame the disciples?" and answers that the disciples "might in many instances work a cure" even "without persons to bring the sick in faith."

*Everyone present.* The Expositor's Greek Testament: "Probably more or less all present: parent, disciples, scribes." The Pulpit Commentary: "Want of faith appertained to all." Ellicott says the words "were obviously addressed both to the scribes and the disciples."

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the disciples reading, Gill notes that "faithless" is not used of them elsewhere. Against the father-and-crowd reading, Meyer notes that 17:20 puts the failure on the disciples and that the father came because he believed. Against the all-present reading, Calvin holds that the rebuke fits the malicious, not "ignorant and weak persons." This lesson does not decide whom the exclamation addressed.

**A misuse to name.** Holding that healing comes through faith is not the same as misusing this passage, and the traditions that hold it most strongly say so. The Assemblies of God's position paper *Divine Healing* (2010) cites this scene: "unbelief hindered its reception at Nazareth (Mark 6:5,6) and at the foot of the Mount of Transfiguration (Matthew 17:14–20)." The paper's next sentences add: "But Jesus did not turn away from those who had little faith or who did not seem to express any faith at all. Those who are sick often find it is not easy to express faith." Later it says that "the amount of faith is not always... determinative" and that "Excessive claims and unfounded judgments are offered by some." The misuse is the step from saying that faith matters to telling a sick person, or a parent, that the illness continues because their faith failed. Graham Hill (*Pacific Journal of Baptist Research*, 2013) writes that the Pentecostal church of his youth "taught that physical healing is guaranteed in the atonement, and that one only needs enough faith to be healed," and that two leaders he knew died of cancer while "absolutely certain that God would heal them," leaving their congregations to "make sense of their deaths." Kenneth Kantzer's 1985 *Christianity Today* editorial quotes the faith healer Hobart Freeman, "If we feel the need of anything in addition to faith, then we do not have faith to be healed," reports an Associated Press count of Freeman's followers who died after refusing medicine, and warns that such promises lead "in the end to false guilt and despair." Of these sources, only the Assemblies of God paper cites this passage, with the cautions above. In Matthew's text the "little faith" answer is given to the disciples about their own failure (17:19-20), not to the father or the boy. If you have been told that an illness, yours or a child's, continues because of weak faith, a pastor or chaplain who knows you is the right person to talk to; nothing in this lesson supports that claim.

**3. Does 17:21 belong to Matthew?** The verse is in four of the nine Greek editions on Bible Hub and missing from five (see Context). Two named views are reported here.

*An addition taken from Mark.* Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary*, as quoted in a 2022 article on the Updated American Standard Version's site (not read directly), rates the omission {A}, which the article glosses as "the text is certain." It reasons that there is no satisfactory explanation for the verse's absence from many witnesses if it was original, that "copyists frequently inserted material derived from another Gospel," and that "most manuscripts have been assimilated to the parallel in" Mark 9:29. The same article quotes Philip Comfort, who adds that the witnesses with the verse are largely those that read "and fasting" at Mark 9:29. Wikipedia's list (a tertiary source) names Codex Sinaiticus (first hand), Codex Vaticanus and Codex Koridethi among the witnesses that lack it, and Codex Ephraemi and Codex Bezae among those that have it. The Pulpit Commentary says it is "omitted in many good manuscripts... it being considered to have been introduced from the parallel passage of St. Mark."

*An original verse that was dropped.* James Snapp Jr. (*The Text of the Gospels*, 2024), drawing on Jonathan Borland, argues that "Matthew 17:21 should be regarded as an authentic part of the Gospel of Matthew." He reports that the large majority of Greek manuscripts have it and cites early writers who quote the saying, among them Origen (*Commentary on Matthew* XIII.7, read directly here: "This kind goes not out save by prayer and fasting"). He objects that Matthew's wording matches Mark's in only nine of twelve words, so the supposed copying "doesn't yield a tight harmony," and proposes that an early scribe dropped the words "out of concern that readers might think that the ability of the Son of God was limited depending on whether he fasted or not."

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the addition view, Snapp points to the early quotations and the imperfect match with Mark. Against the original view, Metzger, as quoted, finds no satisfactory reason for so wide an omission, and Comfort notes that the same manuscripts carry "and fasting" in both places. This lesson does not decide the question, but notes what depends on it: Ellicott, Calvin and the Pulpit Commentary build part of their reading of "this kind" on 17:21, and Ellicott's remark in Position 1 rests on it too.

**4. "Then the sons are exempt" (17:26): who are the sons, and how serious is the argument?** Kings collect taxes "from their own sons, or from others?" Peter answers, "From others" (17:25-26). Readers agree that a royal household is being compared with God's, and differ over whom "the sons" includes.

*Jesus alone, as the Son.* Chrysostom (Homily 58) paraphrases: "much more ought I to be freed from this demand, I who am Son, not of an earthly king, but of the King of Heaven." Meyer: "Therefore I, as the Son of God, am exempt from the tax which is payable to Jehovah," with the plural "the sons" taken as a general rule whose application "must be made to Jesus only, not to Peter as well." Jamieson-Fausset-Brown and the Pulpit Commentary read it the same way, and Calvin holds that "Christ does not say any thing about a privilege common to believers."

*Jesus and those who belong to him.* Ellicott thinks the range "is, it is believed, wider," because the plural, the "we" of "so that we may not offend them," and the payment for Peter "all indicate that we are dealing with a general truth of wide application." Bengel: "They who pertain to Jesus, possess also the right of Jesus." Meyer names others of this view (Olshausen, Ewald, Lange, Keim), who are reported here only through him.

*A light word, not a serious claim.* The Expositor's Greek Testament calls the reply "a jeu d'esprit," a playful remark: "Christ's purpose is not seriously to argue for exemption, but to prepare the way for a moral lesson," namely the payment "so that we may not offend them" (17:27).

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the Jesus-alone reading, Ellicott points to the plural and to Peter's share in the payment. Against the wider reading, Meyer notes that believers were still bound to the temple at the time, and Jamieson-Fausset-Brown that it would teach "the right of believers to exemption from the dues required for sacred services." Against the playful reading, Meyer and Chrysostom treat the argument as a real claim about sonship. This lesson does not decide among them.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, a model of the shape and not an answer key. Your own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 17:26-27: "Then the sons are exempt," Jesus declared. "But so that we may not offend them, go to the sea, cast a hook, and take the first fish you catch" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: Jesus states a reason he need not pay (17:26), then pays anyway, and gives his reason: "so that we may not offend them" (17:27). Inference: for disciples who had just failed to heal a boy and would soon ask who was greatest (18:1), the scene showed a claim to freedom held together with a choice not to use it.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** A person may sometimes set aside something they are entitled to, so that others are not needlessly put off.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a real entitlement, a choice not to press it, and a reason that looks to other people. Not carried forward: the temple tax itself, the fish, Jesus' unique sonship, or any rule that rights should always be surrendered.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a narrated choice carried by analogy, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none. The passage does not promise that giving way will prevent offense, or that money will be provided.

**Modern domain and situation.** Community life. A volunteer youth-league coach has her own children's registration fees waived under the league's rules. Some parents, not knowing the rule, have complained about favoritism. She can afford the fee.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: setting aside an entitlement for others' sake. One faithful response: she pays the fee this season without making a point of it, and asks the league board to explain the rule in its newsletter so the next coach does not face the same suspicion.

**Cautions.** This bridge is not about paying a demand that is unjust, coerced or beyond one's means. Nothing in the passage tells anyone to give money to a person or group pressuring them; if that is happening to you, a trusted advisor outside the situation should hear about it. Nor does it say every entitlement should be given up; Jesus states his plainly first. The bridge is an analogy to test, not a rule.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Matthew's father uses the seizure word (17:15), and Matthew's narrator says a demon came out (17:18). If you had only Matthew and not Mark or Luke, what would you conclude the father believed about his son's condition, and what would you conclude Matthew believed? Are those the same question?
2. In Matthew, the disciples' failure is explained to them privately, as "little faith" (17:20), and the next verse in some manuscripts adds "prayer and fasting." If 17:21 was not part of Matthew's text, how does the answer read without it? If it was, how does it change the answer?
3. Matthew alone has both the grief of 17:23 and the tax scene of 17:24-27, and the chapter ends before the fish is caught. What, if anything, connects a prediction of death to a question about who is exempt from the temple tax, and what should a careful reader do with an ending the text leaves untold?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 17:14-27 in order, from the kneeling father to the coin. (b) Quote, in the BSB's words, Jesus' answer at 17:20 and his statement at 17:26. (c) Name two checkable differences between Matthew's account of the boy and Mark's.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) the descent, and the Greek behind "He has seizures"; (1:00-2:00) little faith, the mustard seed, and the empty 17:21; (2:00-3:00) the second prediction beside 16:21 and 20:18-19; (3:00-4:00) the two-drachma tax, Exodus 30, Josephus, and the untold ending; (4:00-5:00) one Position, with two named sources and the misuse its note names.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then answer it. Form your own first. If you are stuck: "Matthew says plainly that Jesus 'rebuked the demon' (17:18). Calling the condition epilepsy explains the text away." (Separate what the narrator says from what the father says, and say what Calvin, the Pulpit Commentary and Meyer do with both.) A second runs the other way: "The Greek word is 'moonstruck,' and the symptoms are a seizure. Reading a demon into it repeats an ancient mistake." (Say what 17:18 states, and what Origen and Chrysostom say about the moon-word.)
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 17:14-20 does not say that the father's faith, or the boy's, caused the failure. The explanation in 17:20 is given to the disciples about themselves; Mark's "help my unbelief" (Mark 9:24) is not in Matthew. (b) The passage does not say that a seizure disorder in anyone today is demonic. It narrates one boy in one scene. (c) Matthew 17:27 does not say the fish was caught or the coin found.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words in both texts:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mark 9:14-29 | parallel | strong | Same scene and sequence; Mark has a mute spirit, "help my unbelief," and "except by prayer"; Matthew has "seizures" and the mustard-seed answer |
| Deuteronomy 32:5-20 | allusion | plausible | "Perverse... generation" shared with the Song of Moses, two Greek words in the Septuagint; Meyer, Pulpit and Gill cite it; no formula |
| Exodus 30:11-16 | motif | strong | The two-drachma tax identified by named readers and Josephus as the half-shekel of Exodus 30; Matthew names neither Moses nor the temple |
| Matthew 16:21 | motif | strong | The second of three predictions ending "on the third day" (16:21; 17:23; 20:19), verbatim in Greek, different in detail |
| Matthew 13:31-32 | motif | strong | "Mustard seed" only here and 13:31 in Matthew, same Greek; readers differ on whether the point is smallness or growth |

The Exodus 30 row is this lesson's main Old Testament thread, and it is a thread of practice, not prophecy: Matthew neither quotes nor echoes Exodus, and the link is drawn by readers and Josephus. Two candidates were considered and not authored. **Exodus 32:15-19**, Moses coming down to the people's unfaithfulness "at the base of the mountain" (32:19, BSB): Bengel writes "cf. Exodus 32:19" at 17:17, but one commentator's cross-reference, with no shared wording, does not show that Matthew evoked it. **2 Kings 4:29-31**, where Gehazi lays Elisha's staff on a boy's face but "there was no sound or response" (4:31, BSB): Meyer reports that Strauss found the story's origin there and calls that "unwarrantable," and no source read argues it as an echo.
