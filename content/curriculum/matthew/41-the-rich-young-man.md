Matthew 19:16-30 opens with a question, "Teacher, what good thing must I do to obtain eternal life?" (19:16, BSB), and ends with a saying that turns rank upside down: "But many who are first will be last, and the last will be first" (19:30). Between them a man is told to keep the commandments, then to "go, sell your possessions and give to the poor" (19:21), and he goes away "in sorrow, because he had great wealth" (19:22). This lesson checks the Greek editions on Bible Hub where Matthew's Byzantine text differs from the critical text the BSB follows, compares Mark 10 and Luke 18, and grades the commandments Jesus quotes against Exodus 20 and Leviticus 19:18. It reports, without ranking them, how named traditions read the command to sell and give, and how the promise of "a hundredfold" (19:29) has been used to raise money.

## Context

**What happens, in order (19:16-30).** The question and reply (19:16-17); the commandments (19:18-19); "What do I still lack?" and the command to sell, give and follow (19:20-21); the man's departure (19:22); the camel saying and "Who then can be saved?" (19:23-25); "With man this is impossible, but with God all things are possible" (19:26); Peter's question, the twelve thrones and the hundredfold (19:27-29); first and last (19:30).

**Who the man is.** Matthew calls him "the young man" (19:20, 19:22), the only two uses of "young man" in Matthew's BSB. Mark says "a man" who "ran up and knelt" (Mark 10:17); Luke calls him "a certain ruler" (Luke 18:18). The familiar "rich young ruler" combines Matthew and Luke. Mark's "from my youth" (Mark 10:20) is absent from the SBL, Nestle, Westcott-Hort and Tischendorf texts of Matthew 19:20; the Byzantine, Greek Orthodox and both Textus Receptus texts have it.

**The question and the reply (19:16-17), and the Greek.** In the BSB the man says "Teacher," and Jesus answers, "Why do you ask Me about what is good?" ... "There is only One who is good. If you want to enter life, keep the commandments." In Mark and Luke he says "Good Teacher" and hears "Why do you call Me good? ... No one is good except God alone" (Mark 10:17-18; Luke 18:18-19); in this app's BSB "Good Teacher" occurs only in those two verses. Matthew's Greek is divided. The SBL, Nestle 1904, Westcott-Hort and Tischendorf editions read *Didaskale* ("Teacher") in 19:16 and *Ti me erōtas peri tou agathou? heis estin ho agathos* ("Why do you ask Me about the good? One is the good") in 19:17. The RP Byzantine, Greek Orthodox, Scrivener and Stephanus editions read *Didaskale agathe* ("Good Teacher") and *Ti me legeis agathon? Oudeis agathos ei mē heis, ho theos* ("Why do you call Me good? No one is good but one, God"), the wording of Mark and Luke. How each reading has been explained, and what each says about who Jesus is, is the first question in Positions.

**The commandments (19:17-19).** "Which ones?" Jesus answers: "'Do not murder, do not commit adultery, do not steal, do not bear false witness, honor your father and mother, and love your neighbor as yourself'" (19:18-19). The first five come from the Decalogue (Exodus 20:12-16; Deuteronomy 5:16-20); the last is Leviticus 19:18, "love your neighbor as yourself" (BSB). The NABRE note says Matthew "omits Mark's 'you shall not defraud'" and "adds Lv 19:18." In Greek, Matthew's list uses the Septuagint's future forms (*Ou phoneuseis*, "You shall not murder") in every edition Bible Hub lists, and its last line matches the Septuagint of Leviticus 19:18 word for word. Both quotations are graded in their own connections, where Calvin's comment on the choice of commands is reported.

**"If you want to be perfect" (19:21).** Only Matthew has these words; Mark and Luke have "one thing you lack" (Mark 10:21; Luke 18:22). In Matthew's BSB "perfect" occurs only here and at 5:48, "Be perfect, therefore, as your heavenly Father is perfect." The NABRE note on 19:21 draws that link: "to be perfect is demanded of all Christians; see Mt 5:48." Chrysostom (Homily 63) notes that Jesus "sets forth the recompenses" before the demand, and "leaves it all to his own will." What the command asks, and of whom, is the second question in Positions.

**Hard, then impossible (19:23-26).** "it is hard for a rich man to enter the kingdom of heaven" (19:23) becomes the camel saying (19:24). In Matthew's BSB "rich" occurs only at 19:23, 19:24 and 27:57, where Joseph of Arimathea is "a rich man ... who himself was a disciple of Jesus"; the NABRE note on 19:16-30 cites him. Verse 24 is one of five verses in Matthew's BSB with "kingdom of God" rather than "kingdom of heaven" (6:33, 12:28, 19:24, 21:31, 21:43). At Mark 10:24 the Byzantine, Greek Orthodox and both Textus Receptus texts speak of those who trust in riches; the critical texts and the BSB do not.

Readers differ on how strongly to take 19:24. Chrysostom says Jesus "shows that it is even impossible, and not merely impossible, but even in the highest degree impossible." The Expositor's Greek Testament, on 19:26, says Jesus "did not mean that it was as impossible for a rich man to be saved as for a camel to pass through a needle-eye, but that the tendency of wealth was to act powerfully as an obstructive to the spiritual life."

**The camel and the needle: three explanations, and who says what.**

*A gate called "the needle's eye."* Agnieszka Zieminska (*New Testament Studies*, 2022) writes that "Today, most biblical scholars are convinced that this theory is incorrect," and that "There is no ancient evidence of the use of the phrase 'eye of a needle' as the name of a gate in the wall of Jerusalem in Jesus' time or earlier." She traces the earliest known mention to an anonymous gloss in Thomas Aquinas's *Catena aurea*, "later attributed to Anselm of Canterbury (1033–1109)," and rejects the common claim that Theophylact first mentioned the gate: he "only writes that 'some say that "camel" is not the animal, but the thick cable used by sailors.'" If an earlier source exists, she adds, "perhaps a future generation of scholars will discover it." Ellicott reports a "needle's eye" foot-gate in "some modern Syrian cities" but adds, "It is not, however, necessary." The NABRE note says the comparison "should not be mitigated by such suppositions as that the eye of a needle means a low or narrow gate."

*A rope, not a camel.* Greek *kamēlos* is "camel"; *kamilos* is a rare word for "rope." Calvin preferred the rope: "The word camel denotes, I think, a rope used by sailors, rather than the animal so named." His English translator's note answers that Calvin adopted "the notion equally unfounded, that Greek writers sometimes used kamelos, in the sense of kamilos." The older commentators disagree about the manuscripts: Meyer writes of *kamilon* "as in several cursive manuscripts," while Ellicott says, "Not a single MS., however, gives that reading." Both reject the rope: Meyer because *kamēlos* "never has any other meaning than that of a camel," Ellicott because *kamilos* "is supposed by the best scholars (e.g., Liddell and Scott) to have been invented for the sake of explaining this passage." All eight Greek editions of 19:24 on Bible Hub read *kamēlon*.

*A proverb of impossibility.* Several commentators compare a Talmudic idiom. In Berakhot 55b (William Davidson translation, Sefaria), Rava says that "one is neither shown a golden palm tree nor an elephant going through the eye of a needle in a dream," since dreams show "the thoughts of his heart." In Bava Metzia 38b, Rav Sheshet answers a strained argument: "Perhaps you are from Pumbedita, where people pass an elephant through the eye of a needle." These sayings, recorded in a compilation completed centuries after Matthew, show the image in later Jewish usage, not its source. Meyer calls the elephant "quite analogous." The Expositor's Greek Testament: "The figure is to be taken as it stands, and not to be 'civilised.'"

**"With man this is impossible, but with God all things are possible" (19:26).** The Greek, *para anthrōpois touto adunaton estin, para de theō panta dunata*, shares the root "impossible" and the phrase "with God" with the Septuagint of Genesis 18:14, *mē adunatei para tō theō rhēma*, "Is anything too difficult for the LORD?" (BSB). The NABRE cross-references on 19:26 list Genesis 18:14, Job 42:2 and Luke 1:37. The Genesis link is graded as a motif in its own file.

**Peter's question and the twelve thrones (19:27-28).** "Look," Peter says, "we have left everything to follow You. What then will there be for us?" Jesus promises that "in the renewal of all things, when the Son of Man sits on His glorious throne," the Twelve "will also sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel" (19:28). The phrase "renewal of all things" renders one Greek word, *palingenesia*, literally "new birth"; the NABRE note says it "occurs in the New Testament only here and in Ti 3:5," as Ellicott and Barnes also say. (At Titus 3:5 the BSB renders it "new birth.") Readers place the renewal differently. Barnes takes it as "that universal new birth which will occur when the dead shall rise"; Calvin explains it "as referring to the first coming of Christ; for then the world began to be renewed." The NABRE note allows that it could mean the time after the resurrection, when the Twelve "will govern the true Israel, i.e., the church of Jesus," but judges it "more likely" that they are promised a share in the final judgment, since Matthew uses "glorious throne" language "in Mt 25:31 for the time of final judgment."

Readers also differ on "judging." The NABRE note mentions "judge" in the sense of "govern." Calvin calls the Twelve "assessors," whose number signified that God "purposed to collect the remnant of his people which was scattered." Chrysostom reads "condemning them," those in Israel who did not believe, and holds that the thrones signify "honor and glory unspeakable," since "He alone is the one that shall sit and judge." Matthew's word is "judging" (*krinontes*); the passage does not say which of these the Twelve will do. Luke has a related saying at the Last Supper (Luke 22:28-30).

**The hundredfold (19:29).** "And everyone who has left houses or brothers or sisters or father or mother or wife or children or fields for the sake of My name will receive a hundredfold and will inherit eternal life." Here the BSB follows the SBL edition; the Nestle, Westcott-Hort and Tischendorf texts lack "or wife" and read "manifold" (*pollaplasiona*) for "a hundredfold." Matthew names no time and no persecutions. Mark has "a hundredfold in the present age ... along with persecutions" (Mark 10:30). Calvin grants that the promise "appears not at all to agree with experience," and replies that God's "immediate grace" is "justly preferred to all the riches of the world."

**First and last (19:30).** The saying returns, reversed, at 20:16, after the parable of the vineyard workers; that link belongs to the next lesson. Calvin applies it to perseverance; the NABRE note, reading it with 20:1-16, to the idea that all who respond "at whatever time (first or last), will be the same in respect to inheriting the benefits of the kingdom."

## Literary Design

Notes on how 19:16-30 is built, not on what the construction is meant to teach.

**A frame of eternal life.** The man asks how to "obtain eternal life" (19:16); the last promise is that those who leave things for Jesus' name "will inherit eternal life" (19:29). Two of the three uses of "eternal life" in Matthew's BSB stand at these two points (the third is 25:46).

**Leaving and following.** The command ends "Then come, follow Me" (19:21); Peter answers, "we have left everything to follow You" (19:27). Chrysostom notes that Jesus "had required of the rich man these two things," and that Peter "also expresses these two things, to forsake, and to follow." The NABRE cross-references point back to the fishermen who "left their nets and followed Him" (4:20, 4:22).

## Positions

Two questions draw named, published disagreement, and one misuse needs to be named plainly.

**1. "Why do you ask Me about what is good?" or "Why do you call Me good?" (19:17).** Two questions overlap: what Matthew wrote, and what the reply says about Jesus.

*The textual question.* Ellicott says "the older MSS." read "Why askest thou Me concerning that which is good?" and that "The alteration was probably made, as before, for the sake of agreement with the other Gospels"; he adds, "In either case the answer has the same force." The Pulpit Commentary reports that "Most modern editors" adopt the shorter reading, but objects: "If this is so, it is difficult to see whence Mark and Luke obtained their wording, unless - which is improbable - our Lord used both interrogations on the same occasion." Gill knows that "Some copies read, 'why dost thou ask me concerning good'," and keeps the Received Text, saying that the Syriac and other versions "read as we do, and this the answer of Christ requires." Meyer gives priority to Mark: "The tradition followed by Matthew seems to have already omitted the circumstance of our Lord's declining the epithet." The NABRE note on 19:17 agrees in substance: "By Matthew's reformulation of the Marcan question and reply (Mk 10:17–18) Jesus' repudiation of the term 'good' for himself has been softened. Yet the Marcan assertion that 'no one is good but God alone' stands."

*What the reply says about Jesus.* Readers of "Why do you call Me good?" (Mark, Luke and the Byzantine Matthew) read it in at least three ways. Some hear a prompt toward Jesus' divinity. Gill says that Jesus "was good, both as God and man," and that "our Lord's view is ... to instruct him in the knowledge of his proper deity." Poole paraphrases, "How callest thou me good, whom thou wilt neither own to be God, nor to derive from God?" Barnes says Jesus "did not intend here to disclaim divinity," but to reprove "a foolish habit of compliment and flattery." Calvin denies that the verse speaks to divinity: "I do not understand this correction in so refined a sense as is given by a good part of interpreters, as if Christ intended to suggest his Divinity"; rather, "Thou falsely callest me a good Master, unless thou acknowledgest that I have come from God," so "The essence of his Godhead, therefore, is not here maintained." Chrysostom says Jesus answered so "Because He came unto Him as a mere man ... for this cause then as a man He discourses with him," that "there is none good" means "none among men," and that Jesus said "God," not "my Father," "that you might learn that He had not revealed Himself to the young man." Riddle's note in the NPNF edition says that the clause Chrysostom quotes "is found in the rec. text, but not in the better supported form of the verse," and that "It is probable, but not certain, that Chrysostom accepted the other form of the text." Reading Matthew's critical wording, the Catechism of the Catholic Church takes the reply as "invoking the necessity to recognize God as the 'One there is who is good,' as the supreme Good and the source of all good" (§2052), and Bengel as "He who [alone] is Good, should be asked concerning that which is good."

These readings answer one another (Calvin rejects the reading Gill holds; the Pulpit Commentary answers the editors Ellicott follows), and this lesson does not decide among them.

**2. "go, sell your possessions and give to the poor" (19:21).** Four readings are reported here in their own words, with Chrysostom's homily alongside.

*A counsel offered to some, inseparable from the commandments.* Thomas Aquinas distinguishes a commandment, which "implies obligation," from a counsel, which "is left to the option of the one to whom it is given." A person "does not need to renounce the things of the world altogether" to reach eternal happiness, "but he will attain more speedily thereto by giving up the goods of this world entirely." He reads 19:21 as "the counsel of perpetual poverty," prefaced by "If thou wilt be perfect" because the counsels, "considered in themselves, are expedient to all" but do not suit every disposition (*Summa Theologiae* I-II, q. 108, a. 4). The Catechism says that Jesus' second reply "does not do away with the first: following Jesus Christ involves keeping the Commandments," and that "The evangelical counsels are inseparable from the Commandments" (§2053); the counsels "are to be practiced in keeping with the vocation of each" (§1974); and "The precept of detachment from riches is obligatory for entrance into the Kingdom of heaven" (§2544). The NABRE note on 19:16-30 says the story "does not set up a 'two-tier' morality," and that "Actual renunciation of riches is not demanded of all."

*A specific diagnosis for this man.* Calvin holds that the command "was not an addition to the law, but the scrutiny of a concealed vice," the "hidden disease of avarice," and that "Christ does not command all without exception to sell all that they have; for the husbandman, who had been accustomed to live by his labor, and to support his children, would do wrong in selling his possession, if he were not constrained to it by any necessity." He stresses the giving: "to part with riches would not be in itself a virtue," so "Christ applauds not simply the selling but liberality in assisting the poor." Benson: "For him literally to sell all, was an absolute duty; for many to do this would be an absolute sin." Benson adds that "sincere piety and virtue require in all an habitual readiness" to sacrifice possessions "at the command of God." Ellicott calls the command "strictly a remedy for the special evil" that hindered this man, "applicable to others so far only as their cases are analogous."

*A demand on every disciple.* Peter Walpot, a sixteenth-century Hutterite bishop, read the passage as a call to all believers to give up private property in a community of goods. In the English excerpt reproduced by Breaking Ground, he writes that "we should not serve money and gold and cling to them (Ps. 62:10), but give them over to God's poor (Matt. 19:21)," and that "Christian community life is a narrow gate, and to the carnal man it is like the small eye of a needle (Matt. 19:24)." He rejects the limiting move: "Therefore you should not say that this applies merely to what is superfluous; do not bring that up!" Community of goods, for Walpot, "is God's earnest will and is as right and fitting now as it was in Jerusalem."

*Detachment of the soul, not disposal of property.* Clement of Alexandria, in *Who Is the Rich Man That Shall Be Saved?*, reads the command inwardly. Jesus "does not, as some conceive off-hand, bid him throw away the substance he possessed, and abandon his property; but bids him banish from his soul his notions about wealth." Riches "which benefit also our neighbours, are not to be thrown away," for wealth is an "instrument," and "The renunciation, then, and selling of all possessions, is to be understood as spoken of the passions of the soul."

*Chrysostom's homily.* Chrysostom fits none of these exactly. He says of the man, "a dissembler I would by no means call him," because, in Chrysostom's words, Mark says that "Jesus beheld him, and loved him." He preaches the command to his hearers: the impossible becomes possible "If you cast away what you have, if you empty yourself of your wealth, if you refrain from the wicked desire." He says Jesus' warning blames "not riches but them that are held in subjection by them."

*Objections each reading faces.* Against the counsel reading: Calvin calls it "excessively ridiculous in the monks, under the pretense of this passage, to claim for themselves state of perfection," and Ellicott calls it "altogether a mistake" to find "a 'counsel of perfection'" here; the Catechism's "inseparable from the Commandments" and the NABRE's rejection of a "two-tier" morality answer from within that tradition. Against the diagnosis reading: Aquinas holds that the counsels "considered in themselves, are expedient to all," and Walpot calls community of goods "an article of faith" for the whole church. Against the universal reading: Calvin's husbandman "would do wrong in selling his possession"; the NABRE names the rich Joseph of Arimathea as a disciple (27:57); Clement says riches "are not to be thrown away." Against Clement's inward reading: Walpot answers that "the self-seeking man need not pretend so artfully that he can possess treasure and property and yet have his heart and mind with God, for this cannot be," and Chrysostom's remedy is to "empty yourself of your wealth." This lesson does not rank these readings. A reader weighing what this command asks of their own money, work or family needs a pastor or their own church's teachers, not a study note.

**3. A misuse to name plainly: the "hundredfold" as a return on donations.** The promise of "a hundredfold" (19:29; Mark 10:30) has been preached as a rule that money given to a ministry comes back multiplied. David J. Downs, in a paper given at the 2014 Lausanne Global Consultation on Prosperity Theology, names who did so: "Expressed by American Robert Tilton as God's 'law of compensation,' scriptural texts such as Mark 10:29-30, Luke 18:29-30, 2 Cor 9:6, and Gal 6:7 are invoked" to teach that "the more one gives—usually to the preacher or the organization requesting donations— the more God will bless the donor with material gifts." He quotes Gloria Copeland's *God's Will Is Prosperity*, which turns Mark 10:30 into a schedule of returns on gifts of money, airplanes and cars and concludes, "In short, Mark 10: 30 is a very good deal." A 2010 study by Kieran James, Les Hardy and Tim Narraway (read here only in its abstract) reports that "The Copelands also believe in a literal 'hundredfold return' on monetary and other gifts made 'for the gospel's sake' (Mark 10:29-30)," and notes that their teaching "is balanced by warnings about the link between money and selfishness."

What answered it, in the sources read here: Downs says the Luke parallel "can only be read to support the claims of the prosperity preachers if it is completely severed from its present literary context," since there the "many times more" signifies "participation in the new family" of Jesus' followers, "far from promising nicer clothes, bigger houses, or fancier cars." He calls the "crass and prescribed 'laws of compensation'" "relatively easy to dismiss," while also arguing that in the New Testament "the hope of reward regularly features as a ground for appeals to care for the poor." The Lausanne Theology Working Group's statement (2010) rejects "the notion that God's miraculous power can be treated as automatic, or at the disposal of human techniques," and deplores "Replacing the traditional call to repentance and faith with a call to give money." Three things in Matthew's text can be checked. The promise is to those who "left" homes, family or fields "for the sake of My name" (19:29); it does not mention gifts to a ministry. The man in 19:21 is told to give "to the poor," not to a teacher. And Mark's form includes "along with persecutions."

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage states in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. This is a model of the shape, not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** "And everyone who has left houses or brothers or sisters or father or mother or wife or children or fields for the sake of My name will receive a hundredfold and will inherit eternal life" (Matthew 19:29, BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the promise answers Peter, who has "left everything" (19:27), and names what was left: homes, family, fields. Inference: for disciples who had left boats and family (4:20, 4:22), the promise said their loss was not the end of the story. This step goes beyond what the text states and is labeled as this lesson's own.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** What is given up to follow Jesus is not lost, though the passage does not say in what form it returns.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the link between leaving and receiving, and the scope "for the sake of My name." Not carried forward: any rate of return on money; any claim about when or in what form the "hundredfold" comes (Matthew names no time, and Calvin, Chrysostom and Downs describe it differently); and any claim that a gift to a particular ministry is what the verse describes.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a promise given in answer to a disciple's question, carried to later readers by analogy. Promise scope: it is made to "everyone who has left" these things "for the sake of My name"; it names "a hundredfold" (or "manifold" in some editions) and "eternal life"; it states no amount of money, no timetable and no exemption from loss. Mark's form adds "along with persecutions."

**Modern domain and situation.** Household finances and religious giving. A man two months behind on rent hears a broadcast appeal: send a "seed" gift today, and Mark 10:30 guarantees a hundredfold harvest. The broadcaster asks for a credit-card number.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: testing a claim against the text's own scope. One faithful response: he reads 19:29 and Mark 10:30 for himself, notices that the promise concerns leaving home and family for Jesus' name, not sending money to obtain more, and does not borrow to give. He talks with a pastor he trusts about his giving and with someone qualified about his debt.

**Cautions.** This bridge does not mean that giving to a church or ministry is suspect, or that generosity has no reward; Positions 3 reports Downs's view that the New Testament links them. It does not mean that anyone who suffers loss lacked faith. If someone is being pressured to hand over money, property or account details to a religious group or leader, a pastor outside that group, a family member or a qualified financial counselor, not a study note, is the right next step. In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission takes reports of fraud at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. This is a worked example, not financial advice.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Ellicott says the two readings of 19:17 have "the same force"; the NABRE says Matthew's has "softened" Mark's. What would you compare, in Matthew's other changes to Mark, to judge between those descriptions?
2. Matthew alone adds "love your neighbor as yourself." Does the added line make the man's "All these I have kept" (19:20) easier or harder to believe, and why?
3. Matthew says the man "went away in sorrow" (19:22) and says nothing more. Calvin writes, "Whether or not this temptation was temporary, so that the young man afterwards repented, we know not." What in the text would you weigh before deciding how his story ends?
4. Matthew's promise in 19:29 names no time; Mark's says "in the present age" and adds "persecutions." What follows, for a reader of Matthew alone, from what Matthew leaves out?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 19:16-30 as three conversations: with the man (16-22), the disciples (23-26) and Peter (27-30). (b) Name the two places where the Byzantine Greek of Matthew 19:16-17 reads like Mark and Luke, and one thing only Matthew has. (c) Name the commandment Matthew adds and its source.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) the reply and the Greek split (Positions 1); (1:00-2:00) the commandments and their sources; (2:00-3:00) "sell your possessions," two readings in their own words with an objection to each (Positions 2); (3:00-4:00) gate, rope and proverb, with who says what; (4:00-5:00) the hundredfold and its misuse, ending on one open question.
3. **Likely objection.** Form your own first. If you are stuck, one candidate, at full strength: reporting Walpot beside Aquinas and Calvin makes a hard saying optional, when Jesus plainly told a man to sell everything. Before answering, notice that every reading in Positions 2 takes the command seriously and differs on whom it binds.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) It does not say that wealth itself excludes a person from the kingdom: Matthew calls Joseph of Arimathea "a rich man" and a disciple (27:57). (b) It does not promise a financial return for gifts to a ministry: 19:29 speaks of those who "left" homes and family "for the sake of My name." (c) It does not say what the Twelve will do in "judging" the tribes; readers say govern, assess or condemn.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Name its type and evidence label, and point to the words in both texts that support that grade.

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mark 10:17-31 | parallel | strong | Same scene and sayings in the same order; only Mark has "Jesus looked at him, loved him" and "along with persecutions" |
| Luke 18:18-30 | parallel | strong | Same exchange; only Luke calls him "a certain ruler" and puts adultery first, as Swete's Septuagint of Deuteronomy 5 does |
| Exodus 20:12-16 | quotation | explicit | "keep the commandments" and "Which ones?" introduce five Decalogue commands in the Septuagint's Greek forms |
| Leviticus 19:18 | quotation | explicit | The same list ends with "love your neighbor as yourself," word for word in the BSB and the Septuagint; only Matthew adds it |
| Genesis 18:14 | motif | plausible | "With God" and the root "impossible" in the Septuagint; listed in the NABRE cross-references, argued by no commentator read here |

Choose one row and defend it, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`. Three candidates were considered and not authored. Daniel 7:9-14 (thrones, "One like the Son of Man," glory) is listed for 19:28 in the NABRE cross-references (Daniel 7:9, 7:22); on the precedent of conn-matt8-sonofman-daniel7 it would be plausible at most, and it was left out to stay within five rows. The return of "the last will be first" at 20:16 is authored by the next lesson. The merchant who "sold all he had" for one pearl (13:45-46) is compared by the Cambridge Bible on 19:21, but the two share an image, not wording or a stated link.

## Sources and Limits

- **Read directly, with raw copies saved:** every source in the source list. Bible Hub's commentary pages are an aggregator, not checked against printed editions; Calvin is the Calvin Translation Society English; Chrysostom and Clement are the nineteenth-century NPNF and ANF translations, and Riddle's footnote on Homily 63 was read on CCEL because the New Advent copy garbles it; the Talmud is the William Davidson translation on Sefaria.
- **Read only in part, or secondhand.** The James, Hardy and Narraway study was read only in its abstract. Gloria Copeland's book and Robert Tilton's teaching are known here only through Downs. Theophylact is known only through Zieminska. Walpot was read in an English excerpt on Breaking Ground that does not name its translator.
- **Polemic in the sources.** Calvin's comments on 19:16-22 attack the "Papists" and describe monasteries in contemptuous terms; Poole's comment on 19:21 is aimed at "the papists." They are quoted only for their reading of the verse, and the Catholic reading is reported from Aquinas, the Catechism and the NABRE, not from its critics.
- **Not settled here.** Meyer and Ellicott disagree on whether any manuscript reads *kamilon* at 19:24; no critical apparatus was consulted. That *palingenesia* occurs in the New Testament only at Matthew 19:28 and Titus 3:5 is reported from the NABRE, Ellicott and Barnes; only those two verses' Greek was checked. The Talmud is later than Matthew and cannot show Jesus' source.
- **Not reported.** No Eastern Orthodox source beyond Chrysostom, and no modern critical commentary on Matthew, was read; that is a sourcing gap, not a claim about those traditions. Riedemann's *Confession* and Basil's *To the Rich* were located but not read on this passage. No source was found that ties a named group's demand for members' property to 19:21, so none is named.
- **Every BSB-attributed quotation in this lesson and its five connection files was printed from `web/public/bible/BSB/*.json` before drafting and re-checked afterward** by a script asserting each is a literal substring of the BSB text. Every count and "only" claim was produced by a script over the same files, case-insensitive and on word boundaries: "eternal life" in Matthew at 19:16, 19:29, 25:46; "rich" in Matthew at 19:23, 19:24, 27:57; "perfect" in Matthew at 5:48 and 19:21; "young man" in Matthew only at 19:20, 19:22; "kingdom of God" in Matthew at 6:33, 12:28, 19:24, 21:31, 21:43; "Good Teacher" only at Mark 10:17 and Luke 18:18; "great wealth" in the New Testament only at Matthew 19:22 and Mark 10:22; "eye of a needle" only at Matthew 19:24, Mark 10:25, Luke 18:25. The scripts and their outputs are kept with this lesson's working files.
