The John the Baptist lesson that precedes this one ended with John's own description of his ministry: "I baptize you with water for repentance" (3:11, BSB), aimed at crowds of whom Matthew writes, "Confessing their sins, they were baptized by him in the Jordan River" (3:6). Five verses later, the one John said was "more powerful" than himself (3:11) walks into that same river to receive that same baptism -- and John's own first response, in the text's own words, is to object. That short exchange (3:14-15), found only in Matthew, is this passage's first piece of real, substantive content, not a detail to pass over on the way to the dove and the voice. This lesson keeps three kinds of claim in three different places. Some things here are directly checkable textual facts, checked against the real Greek of this app's primary texts, not assumed from a secondary summary: the exact wording of John's objection and Jesus's answer; the precise difference between Matthew's "This is My beloved Son" and Mark's and Luke's "You are My beloved Son"; and how closely Matthew's own Greek at 3:17 actually matches the Greek of Psalm 2:7 and Isaiah 42:1, checked word for word rather than taken on a commentator's word for it. Some things are named, live scholarly positions on a question this passage leaves genuinely open: why Jesus, repeatedly treated elsewhere in this same Gospel as without guilt, submitted to a baptism John himself describes as being "for repentance." And one thing is a cited historical fact, reported as a use rather than adopted as this lesson's own conclusion: that this scene -- the Son baptized, the Spirit descending, the Father's voice speaking -- has been read by name, across sixteen centuries of commentary, as evidence for the doctrine of the Trinity.

## Context

**What happens, in order (3:13-17).** "At that time Jesus came from Galilee to the Jordan to be baptized by John" (3:13, BSB). "But John tried to prevent Him, saying, 'I need to be baptized by You, and do You come to me?'" (3:14) -- John's own words, a direct objection, not a narrator's summary of his hesitation. Jesus answers: "'Let it be so now,' Jesus replied. 'It is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness in this way.' Then John permitted Him" (3:15). "As soon as Jesus was baptized, He went up out of the water. Suddenly the heavens were opened, and He saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and resting on Him" (3:16). "And a voice from heaven said, 'This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased!'" (3:17). That is the whole of what this five-verse unit narrates; the temptation in the wilderness that follows (4:1-11) belongs to the next unit, not this one, and this lesson does not draw on it except to note the boundary.

**Where this passage sits.** This is the sixth unit of the pilot curriculum's Matthew sequence, following John the Baptist's preaching ministry (3:1-12, stage 6) and preceding the temptation narrative (4:1-11) -- a structural position in how this app organizes its material, not a claim about the Gospel's own composition history. The hinge between the two units is direct: the "more powerful" figure John described only in the third person at 3:11 ("He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and with fire") now appears in the narrative in person, in the first scene where John and Jesus occupy the same verse.

**John's objection is a textual fact unique to Matthew, not a detail Mark or Luke also report.** Matthew 3:14's exchange -- John's "I need to be baptized by You, and do You come to me?" and Jesus's answer about fulfilling all righteousness -- has no parallel at all in Mark's or Luke's accounts of the same baptism (Mark 1:9-11; Luke 3:21-22, both discussed further in Literary Design below). Neither of the other two Synoptic Gospels records John saying anything to Jesus, or Jesus answering him, at this scene. This is directly checkable by reading all three accounts side by side; it is not, on its own, a claim about why Matthew alone includes it.

**Why John's own objection is a real textual fact worth stating precisely, not a detail to summarize past.** John's own ministry, as this app's prior lesson on 3:1-12 already established, is a baptism John himself describes as being "for repentance" (3:11), for a crowd of whom Matthew writes, "Confessing their sins, they were baptized by him in the Jordan River" (3:6). John's objection in 3:14 -- framed as his own need, "I need to be baptized by You," rather than as a statement about Jesus's own moral standing -- does not, in so many words, state why Jesus submitting to that same baptism is a problem; the text lets the juxtaposition itself (a baptism "for repentance," performed on the one John has just called "more powerful" than himself) carry the weight. Elsewhere in this same Gospel, Matthew's own narration describes Jesus with language that sits uneasily next to a baptism of repentance: Judas calls the blood he betrayed "innocent" (27:4, "I have sinned by betraying innocent blood"); Pilate's wife calls Jesus "that innocent man" (27:19); Pilate himself says, "I am innocent of this man’s blood" (27:24). None of these three statements is a formal doctrinal claim that Jesus never sinned -- that fuller claim is made more directly elsewhere in the New Testament, outside Matthew (2 Corinthians 5:21; Hebrews 4:15), and this lesson does not import those verses to settle what Matthew's own text is doing here. What is checkable within Matthew's own Gospel is that three different characters, in three different scenes, call Jesus "innocent" in the text's own words -- which is at least part of why this passage's own juxtaposition (a baptism for repentance, performed on him) is a real interpretive puzzle rather than a non-issue, and why John's own objection, read plainly, raises exactly that puzzle without this passage's own words resolving it. See Positions, below, for how named sources have proposed to resolve it.

**"Fulfill all righteousness" is the hardest single phrase in the unit, and the Greek does not settle it on its own.** Jesus's answer -- "It is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness in this way" (3:15) -- uses "righteousness" (dikaiosyne), a word central to Matthew's own theological vocabulary elsewhere in this Gospel (5:6, 5:10, 5:20, 6:33, among others), without this verse itself specifying which of several live senses of "all righteousness" is meant: every commanded ordinance, the whole of God's revealed will, or something narrower tied to this specific moment. The Wikipedia article on Matthew 3:15 used here reports this directly: "the phrase 'fulfill all righteousness' presents interpretive challenges," and that scholars disagree over what it names. This lesson reports the named disagreement in Positions, below, rather than picking a reading here.

**The Greek of the divine voice, checked directly against three other texts, not assumed from a secondary report.** Matthew's Greek at 3:17 reads Houtos estin ho huios mou ho agapetos, en ho eudokesa -- "This is My beloved Son, in whom I was well pleased," using a third-person demonstrative (Houtos estin, "this is") and a relative pronoun (en ho, "in whom"). Checked directly against the Greek text of Mark 1:11 and Luke 3:22 (both read identically at this point): Sy ei ho huios mou ho agapetos, en soi eudokesa -- "You are My beloved Son; in You I am well pleased," using a second-person pronoun and verb (Sy ei, "you are") and a personal pronoun (en soi, "in you") instead of Matthew's relative pronoun. The two versions share two core words exactly -- huios mou ho agapetos ("[My] beloved Son") and the verb eudokesa ("I was well pleased") -- and differ at exactly the point where the voice either addresses Jesus directly (Mark, Luke) or speaks about Him in the third person, to whoever else is present (Matthew). This is a directly checked fact about the Greek text, not a report of what a commentary says about it; what it means that Matthew's own version shifts the grammatical person is a question several named sources address differently -- see Literary Design and Positions, below.

**Checked directly against the Septuagint: Isaiah 42:1's Greek does not share Matthew's distinctive vocabulary -- but Matthew's own Greek at 12:18 does.** The Septuagint's Isaiah 42:1 reads (in part) ho eklektos mou, prosedexato auton he psyche mou -- "my chosen one... my soul has received him favorably" -- using eklektos ("chosen") rather than agapetos ("beloved"), and the verb prosedexato ("received favorably") rather than any form of eudokeo ("well pleased"), the verb Matthew's baptism scene actually uses. Checked side by side, the standard Greek Old Testament text of Isaiah 42:1 shares no distinctive vocabulary with Matthew 3:17 at all. But Matthew's own later quotation of this same verse, at 12:18 -- introduced with his own citation language, "Here is My Servant, whom I have chosen, My beloved, in whom My soul delights" (BSB) -- reads in Greek ho agapetos mou hon eudokesen he psyche mou: "My beloved, in whom My soul was well pleased," using both agapetos and a form of eudokeo. Those are precisely the two words Matthew's own baptism account shares with Isaiah 42:1 -- found not in the Septuagint's own wording of that verse, but in Matthew's own, different translation of it, quoted by Matthew himself nine chapters later. This is a comparison this lesson checked directly across four Greek texts (Matthew 3:17, Mark 1:11, the Septuagint's Isaiah 42:1, and Matthew's own 12:18); it is reported here as a textual finding, and what a reader concludes from it about Matthew's intent at 3:17 is a further step addressed in Positions, below.

**Checked directly against the Septuagint: Psalm 2:7 shares a shorter, exact phrase with Matthew's own wording.** The Septuagint's Psalm 2:7 reads huios mou ei sy, ego semeron gegenneka se -- "You are My Son; today I have begotten You" (matching the BSB's own rendering, "You are My Son; today I have become Your Father"). Matthew's huios mou ("My Son") at 3:17 matches two of this verse's words exactly; Mark's and Luke's Sy ei ho huios mou ("You are My Son") matches four of the same words, in reordered sequence (ei sy against Sy ei), including the second-person verb and pronoun Matthew's own third-person wording replaces. Neither Matthew, Mark, nor Luke includes anything corresponding to "today I have begotten You" -- the clause in Psalm 2:7 most often read, in other contexts, as the verse's own distinctive content. This lesson reports the exact words shared and not shared, checked directly; see Positions for how named sources read this difference.

## Literary Design

**A structure built from three figures, stated plainly in sequence, not argued for.** 3:16-17 names, in order, the Son being baptized, the Spirit "descending like a dove and resting on Him," and a voice "from heaven" identifying the Son -- three distinct figures doing three distinct things inside two verses. This is a description of how the text is built: three named actors, three named actions, read off the passage directly. What later tradition has done with that structure -- reading it as evidence for a specific doctrine about God's own nature -- is a separate, cited historical fact, reported above in Context and taken up further in Positions, not a claim this paragraph itself makes.

**The person-shift in the divine voice, and two different named explanations for it.** As Context above already establishes directly from the Greek, Matthew's "This is My beloved Son" (third person) and Mark's/Luke's "You are My beloved Son" (second person, identical to each other) differ at exactly one grammatical point, while sharing the rest of the wording exactly. Two named classical commentators, reported by the BibleHub commentary aggregator used here, propose different accounts of that one difference. The Pulpit Commentary proposes that the three Gospels may simply preserve genuinely different wording of what was said, adding "therefore Matthew is the less accurate" -- a claim that Matthew's version is a less precise record of the actual words spoken. Bengel's Gnomen proposes instead that Mark and Luke preserve the verbatim form (Sy ei, "Thou art") while "Matthew has expressed the meaning" -- a claim that Matthew is paraphrasing the same event's substance rather than mis-recording it. These two proposals disagree about what kind of difference this is -- an error of transmission, on the Pulpit Commentary's reading, or a deliberate editorial choice to convey sense rather than verbatim wording, on Bengel's -- without either claiming the three Gospels disagree about what actually happened at the Jordan.

**Who the voice addresses differs along the same line as the grammatical person.** A voice saying "You are My Son" (Mark, Luke) most naturally reads as addressed to Jesus Himself; a voice saying "This is My Son" (Matthew) most naturally reads as addressed to whoever else is present -- John, by this passage's own narrative, and by extension Matthew's own readers. Matthew's Gospel does not state which reading is correct, and no source consulted for this lesson claims the Greek grammar alone settles whether anyone besides Jesus is understood to hear the voice in Matthew's telling; this is a textual observation about what the third-person form most naturally suggests, not a resolved claim about the original audience of the voice.

**A pattern Eric Barreto, writing for Working Preacher, names across the rest of this Gospel.** Barreto's commentary on this passage, used directly for this lesson, observes that God's approval of Jesus in these terms is not confined to this one scene: the same substance recurs at Matthew 12:17-21 (Matthew's own quotation of Isaiah 42:1, already discussed above) and again at the Transfiguration (17:5, outside this lesson's range). Barreto's point is that 3:17 opens a pattern repeated at two further points in Matthew's own narrative, not that those later scenes are identical to this one; this lesson does not develop the Transfiguration parallel further here, since it belongs to a different unit's range.

**A detail easy to overstate: the Spirit "like a dove," and what a popular connection to Genesis 1:2 or Genesis 8 would need to show.** The Spirit "descending like a dove" (3:16) is often preached alongside Genesis 1:2's Spirit "hovering over the surface of the waters" or Genesis 8's dove sent out from Noah's ark -- a connection this lesson looked for a named, published source to support and did not find one in the sources consulted. The Wikipedia article on dove symbolism used here reports Augustine's own comment on this scene specifically (in De Trinitate, as the article reports it): the dove was a "visible sign" of the Spirit, not a claim that the Spirit is literally a bird -- a point about the form of the sign, not an argument connecting it to either Genesis passage. That same source discusses Genesis 8's dove only in connection with the name "Jonah," not with Jesus's baptism. Because this lesson could not locate a named scholar making the Genesis 1:2 or Genesis 8 connection specifically about Matthew 3:16, no connection file for either is authored here; see Sources and Limits.

## Positions

Two separate, named questions get real scholarly attention in this passage, and this lesson keeps them in two separate groups rather than resolving either.

**Why did Jesus submit to a baptism "for repentance" (3:11) when this passage's own surrounding narrative, and other passages in this same Gospel, describe Him as innocent?**

This is a live, named disagreement, not a settled question this lesson adjudicates.

One family of readings holds that Jesus's baptism establishes a pattern or ordinance for others to follow, rather than addressing any need of His own. Several classic commentaries report this reading in similar terms: Barnes' Notes states that "the phrase 'all righteousness' here is the same as a righteous institution or appointment," with Jesus "owning every Divine institution" by submitting to it; Matthew Henry's commentary, reported by the same aggregator, reads the act as showing Jesus's "readiness to comply with all God's righteous precepts"; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown reads "all righteousness" as "every ordinance" generally, "baptism included." A further, named Reformed reading ties the same event to a specific theological category: R. C. Sproul reads Jesus's baptism as necessary to accomplish what Reformed covenant theology calls the Active Obedience of Christ -- Jesus's own lifelong, positive fulfillment of the law's demands, as distinct from His later, passive bearing of its penalty at the cross. These readings differ in vocabulary and theological framework but agree that the baptism is about obedience to a standing requirement, not about Jesus's own need for repentance.

A second reading holds that the baptism's significance lies specifically in its scope: Oscar Cullmann, reported by the Wikipedia article on Matthew 3:15 used here, emphasizes the word "all" in "all righteousness" and argues that Jesus's baptism is meant to obtain righteousness for all humanity, not merely to model an ordinance for individual believers to copy.

A third reading disputes where the citation's own weight falls. Daniel Harrington, a Jesuit biblical scholar, is reported by the same article as linking "fulfill all righteousness" not to Old Testament prophecy at all, but back to the "fruit" of repentance John has already demanded at 3:8 -- on this reading, Jesus's baptism completes something already introduced earlier in this same chapter, not something reaching outside it.

A fourth reading holds that Jesus's baptism identifies Him with those under judgment, as an anticipation of the cross rather than a demonstration of personal obedience. Pope Benedict XVI (Joseph Ratzinger), quoted directly by Brant Pitre's own discussion of this passage, writes in Jesus of Nazareth (2007, p. 18): "Jesus loaded the burden of all mankind's guilt upon his shoulders; he bore it down into the depths of the Jordan. He inaugurated his public activity by stepping into the place of sinners. His inaugural gesture is an anticipation of the Cross." On this reading, the baptism's meaning is substitutionary and forward-looking, not merely exemplary.

A fifth, older reading holds that the baptism's purpose runs the other direction: not Jesus receiving something from the water, but the water receiving something from Him. Ambrose is reported, by a secondary source used here, as holding that "the Lord was baptized, not to be cleansed himself, but to cleanse the waters, so that those waters... might have the power of baptism" -- though this lesson was not able to trace the quotation to a specific work or chapter in Ambrose's own writing; see Sources and Limits.

A sixth reading, reported by the same Wikipedia article, holds that David Hill -- the same commentator already relied on in the previous lesson's treatment of 3:1-12 -- proposes that the phrase names Jesus "fulfilling divine rules of which only he is aware," a private matter between Jesus and the Father that the text does not fully disclose to the reader.

These six readings are not all answering the same question: the first and sixth concern what kind of obligation Jesus is fulfilling; the second and fourth concern the scope of what the act accomplishes (for all humanity, or substitutionary for sinners specifically); the third concerns which textual antecedent the citation is actually pointing back to; the fifth concerns the water's state rather than Jesus's own. This lesson reports all six without ranking them.

**What is the divine voice's wording actually doing with Psalm 2:7 and Isaiah 42:1 -- conflating them, alluding to one more than the other, or something else?**

This is a genuinely disputed textual question, sharpened rather than settled by the Greek comparison already reported directly in Context, above.

The most commonly reported position, found across more than one source consulted here, holds that the voice's wording is a composite of both texts: "Son," read as supplied by Psalm 2:7, and the structure "[demonstrative], My [title], in whom I [delight/am pleased]," read as supplied by Isaiah 42:1. On this reading, Matthew's Gospel deliberately layers a royal-messianic text (Psalm 2) with a servant text (Isaiah 42) in a single utterance, naming Jesus both king and servant at once. The Jerusalem Bible's editors, reported by the Wikipedia article on Matthew 3:17 used here, read this scene's purpose specifically as showing Jesus to be the "suffering servant foretold by Isaiah" -- a reading that leans the composite toward Isaiah's own emphasis.

A second, more specific proposal, from H. A. W. Meyer's nineteenth-century commentary (reported by the same BibleHub aggregator used throughout this lesson), argues that the word order itself is diagnostic: "the passage in Isaiah 42:1... lies at the basis of the expression of that voice," but "ho huios mou (My Son) is the characteristic point, which is wanting in Isaiah" -- that is, Meyer reads the overall shape of the sentence as drawn from Isaiah, with the specific word "Son" supplied from elsewhere (left unnamed in the quoted sentence, though Psalm 2:7 is the only other candidate this lesson's research located). Meyer's proposal agrees with the general composite reading above but makes a more exact claim about which words come from which source.

A third position is this lesson's own comparison, built directly from the Greek checked in Context, above, and reported here as a textual finding rather than a named scholar's claim: the standard Greek Old Testament (Septuagint) text of Isaiah 42:1 shares no distinctive vocabulary with Matthew 3:17 at all -- "chosen" (eklektos), not "beloved" (agapetos); "received favorably" (prosedexato), not any form of "well pleased" (eudokeo). What does share both of Matthew 3:17's distinctive words, agapetos and a form of eudokeo, is Matthew's own later quotation of the same verse at 12:18 -- his own translation, differing from the Septuagint's. If this comparison is sound, the popular claim that Matthew 3:17 "quotes" or "echoes" Isaiah 42:1 is more precisely a claim about Matthew's own translation vocabulary for that verse, attested nine chapters later in his own Gospel, rather than a claim checkable against the Greek Old Testament text available to his first readers. This lesson flags this as a real, checked difference from how several secondary sources state the connection, without claiming to have resolved why Matthew's own rendering of Isaiah 42:1 differs from the Septuagint's.

A fourth position, reported here only as a textual observation this lesson located but did not pursue into named secondary literature, notes that Matthew's wording shares exactly two words with the Septuagint's Psalm 2:7 (huios mou, "My Son") while differing at the one point -- the second-person "You are" -- that would make the match closer; Mark's and Luke's wording, by contrast, shares four of Psalm 2:7's core words in reordered sequence, including that second-person form. If the Psalm 2:7 echo is judged by exact Greek overlap, it is measurably closer in Mark and Luke than in Matthew's own version of the same scene -- a finding this lesson did not locate addressed directly by any named scholar in the sources consulted, and reports here as an open textual observation rather than a settled conclusion.

These four positions disagree about method as much as about conclusion: the first and second argue primarily from English-level or whole-sentence structural resemblance; the third and fourth argue from exact Greek vocabulary, checked directly for this lesson, and arrive at a more qualified picture than the first two suggest. This lesson reports all four without ranking them.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key: the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step, and a different passage element, situation, or response would be equally legitimate.

**Passage element.** Matthew 3:14-15. John's objection, "I need to be baptized by You, and do You come to me?", states his own sense that the ordinary order of things is reversed. Jesus's answer does not deny that the order looks reversed; it reframes what the act is for: "It is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness in this way."

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text records John's objection in his own words and Jesus's answer in His own words, with no narrator's comment resolving the tension between them beyond "then John permitted Him." Inference: for a first-century reader already told, two units earlier in this app's own sequence, that John's baptism was for repentance and confessed sin, hearing the "more powerful" one submit to that same baptism -- while the text itself records the obvious objection rather than hiding it -- would have registered as Jesus choosing to stand where He did not, by every other textual signal in this Gospel, belong. The Context section above supplies what the text itself records (the objection, the answer, the completed act); this inference goes one step further, into why the text records the objection explicitly rather than skipping past it.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Submitting to a requirement that does not, on its own terms, obviously apply to you can itself be a deliberate act with its own purpose -- not merely compliance, and not necessarily a statement about your own standing relative to the requirement.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the general shape of voluntarily standing inside a process built for a different situation than your own, for a stated reason beyond personal need. Not carried forward: the specific content of John's baptism, any claim about Jesus's own moral status, or a promise that doing this will always be read correctly by onlookers. The bridge holds only where a real, nameable reason for standing inside the process is present and statable -- not wherever someone wants to appear humble.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy from how this scene is built, not a command addressed to the reader by name. Promise scope: none claimed; nothing in 3:14-15 promises a modern reader that a given act of voluntary participation will accomplish what Jesus's baptism is reported in Positions, above, to accomplish.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. A senior team member, already recognized as the most capable person on the team, chooses to go through the same entry-level training and certification process required of new hires, rather than requesting a waiver, when the company restructures and the role technically becomes new.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: a deliberate, reasoned act of solidarity, not false modesty and not an admission of incompetence. One faithful response: the senior team member states plainly, to anyone who asks, why they are completing the process rather than letting the question go unaddressed -- naming the actual reason (standing with newer colleagues inside the same requirement, rather than exempting themselves) instead of letting silence be read as either arrogance or a hidden deficiency.

**Cautions.** This is not a model for situations where going through an unnecessary process would waste real resources, mislead others about actual qualifications, or enable a genuinely unsafe practice to continue unexamined -- "fulfilling the process anyway" is not automatically the wiser choice, and a reasonable business case for a waiver is not itself a failure of character. It does not fit a case where someone is being coerced into an unnecessary process against their own judgment; the passage shows a voluntary, explained choice, not compliance extracted under pressure. The passage is not a workplace-process manual; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing, and a different reader may find it does not hold for their own case.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Six named readings of "fulfill all righteousness" are reported above, disagreeing about what kind of obligation is being fulfilled, its scope, and even which earlier verse it points back to. If you had to choose the single textual detail from 3:13-17 itself (not from outside this passage) that most favors one reading over the others, what would it be, and which reading does it favor?
2. This lesson's own Greek comparison (Positions, above) found that Matthew's wording at 3:17 shares its two most distinctive words with Matthew's own later translation of Isaiah 42:1 at 12:18, rather than with the Septuagint's standard Greek text of that verse. What would you need to find elsewhere in Matthew's Gospel, or in what is known about first-century translation practice, to judge whether this reflects Matthew's own deliberate, consistent rendering choice, or something else -- a different Hebrew or Greek source text he had access to, for instance, that this lesson's research did not locate?
3. Matthew's "This is My beloved Son" and Mark's and Luke's "You are My beloved Son" are identical except at the one point that also changes who the voice most naturally seems to address. If a reader concluded Mark and Luke preserve the more exact wording (the Pulpit Commentary's reading in Literary Design, above), would that change anything about what the scene itself accomplishes in Matthew's own narrative -- or does Matthew's version do its own, separate narrative work regardless of which wording came first?
4. This passage, read on its own, states that Jesus is baptized, that the Spirit descends on Him, and that a voice calls Him "beloved Son" -- three things happening together, in sequence, to one figure. What would a reader need to bring from outside this specific passage (from elsewhere in the New Testament, or from later doctrinal formulation) to move from "three things are reported together here" to a developed claim about the eternal relationship among them?

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first; the point of this prompt is that you do the forming, not that you pick from a list. If you are stuck, here is one worked candidate, stated at full strength: this lesson's own Greek comparison in Positions above argues that Matthew 3:17's match to Isaiah 42:1 is really a match to Matthew's own later translation of that verse at 12:18, not to the Greek Old Testament text his first readers would have known -- but a reader could object that this is backwards: if Matthew's own translation choices are consistent across his Gospel (agapetos and a form of eudokeo for the same underlying Hebrew concept at both 3:17 and 12:18), that consistency is itself stronger evidence of Matthew's own deliberate intent to echo Isaiah 42:1 at 3:17, not weaker evidence against it, regardless of what the Septuagint happens to read. Before you answer it, state plainly what this lesson's own Positions section already says: that the finding is reported as a textual difference from how popular sources describe the connection, not as a claim that no real connection to Isaiah 42:1 exists -- and notice that the objection and this lesson's own hedge ("this lesson flags this... without claiming to have resolved why Matthew's own rendering... differs from the Septuagint's") may not actually be in tension once both are stated precisely.

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 3:13-17 does not state who, besides Jesus, hears or understands the voice from heaven; the text simply reports "a voice from heaven said" (3:17), without naming John, any bystander, or Jesus's own disciples (not yet called at this point in the narrative) as hearers, and the grammatical third person of Matthew's own wording ("This is," not "You are") is, at most, a reason to infer other hearers are in view -- not a statement that any specific person actually heard or understood it. (b) Matthew 3:13-17 does not explain why John changed his mind between 3:14 ("John tried to prevent Him") and the end of 3:15 ("then John permitted Him"); the text records Jesus's answer in between, but it does not state that the answer is what changed John's mind, as opposed to any other unstated factor.
