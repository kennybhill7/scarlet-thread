Matthew 3:13-17 narrates Jesus's own baptism; 4:1 opens this unit by naming the connection in its own words -- "Then Jesus was led by the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil" -- so this passage's own text states the transition, without this lesson needing to depend on how that preceding unit is itself authored elsewhere. What follows (4:1-11) is one of the most densely cross-referenced short passages in this Gospel: Jesus answers all three of the devil's temptations by quoting Deuteronomy (8:3, 6:16, 6:13), and the devil himself quotes back a fourth passage, Psalm 91:11-12, the only place in this unit where he, not Jesus, cites Scripture. Four different kinds of claim appear side by side here, and this lesson keeps them in four different places. Some things are directly checkable textual facts: the exact wording of each of the four quotations, checked word for word against both this passage and its own source text as shipped in this app, including what each quotation adds, drops, or changes. Some things are named scholarly positions on questions this passage leaves genuinely open: whether the episode is also a deliberate echo of Adam's own temptation in addition to Israel's, and what kind of event -- a straightforward encounter, a visionary experience, or a question the narrative itself does not require a reader to settle -- this account claims to narrate. Some things are this lesson's own modeled inferences, labeled as such in the Practice Bridge below. This is the same discipline the first six units of this Matthew sequence already practice.

## Context

**What happens, in order (4:1-11).** "Then Jesus was led by the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil" (4:1, BSB). "After fasting forty days and forty nights, He was hungry" (4:2). The devil's first approach: "If You are the Son of God, tell these stones to become bread" (4:3). Jesus answers: "It is written: 'Man shall not live on bread alone, but on every word that comes from the mouth of God'" (4:4). The devil then takes Him "to the holy city" and sets Him "on the pinnacle of the temple" (4:5), saying, "If You are the Son of God... throw Yourself down. For it is written: 'He will command His angels concerning You, and they will lift You up in their hands, so that You will not strike Your foot against a stone'" (4:6). Jesus answers: "It is also written: 'Do not put the Lord your God to the test'" (4:7). "Again, the devil took Him to a very high mountain and showed Him all the kingdoms of the world and their glory" (4:8), saying, "All this I will give You... if You will fall down and worship me" (4:9). Jesus answers: "Away from Me, Satan! For it is written: 'Worship the Lord your God and serve Him only'" (4:10). "Then the devil left Him, and angels came and ministered to Him" (4:11). That is the whole of what this specific range narrates; Jesus's subsequent move to Galilee and the start of his public ministry (4:12-17) belong to a later unit, not this one.

**Where this passage sits.** This is the seventh unit of the pilot curriculum's Matthew sequence (following John the Baptist's preaching, 3:1-12, and Jesus's own baptism, 3:13-17, authored as a separate unit) -- a structural position in how this app organizes its material, not a claim this lesson makes about the Gospel's own composition history. The text's own transition is 4:1's "then," immediately following the baptism account.

**The three Deuteronomy quotations, checked directly against both texts as shipped in this app.** Deuteronomy 8:3 (BSB) ends: "...so that you might understand that man does not live on bread alone, but on every word that comes from the mouth of the LORD." Matthew 4:4 (BSB) quotes: "Man shall not live on bread alone, but on every word that comes from the mouth of God." Two differences are directly checkable without a secondary source: Deuteronomy's own clause sits inside a purpose statement describing what the Israelites were meant to learn from the manna, while Matthew's quotation stands alone as a freestanding command-like assertion; and "the LORD" becomes "God." On this second point, a secondary source -- David Hill's commentary, reported through the Wikipedia aggregator used throughout this lesson -- states that Matthew's quotation "uses the exact wording of the Septuagint." That claim was checked directly against the Septuagint's own Greek text of Deuteronomy 8:3 for this lesson: the Greek reads *dia stomatos theou* ("through the mouth of **God**"), not the more usual *kyriou* ("of the Lord") that elsewhere renders the Hebrew divine name. Matthew's Greek quotation also reads *theou*. The gap between this app's English Old Testament rendering ("the LORD," translating the Hebrew name directly) and Matthew's "God" therefore traces to a translation choice already present in the Septuagint, not a change Matthew introduces -- a different mechanism than the LORD/Lord capitalization convention noted in the Matthew 3 unit of this sequence, where "Lord" renders the Greek *kyrios* directly.

Deuteronomy 6:16 (BSB): "Do not test the LORD your God as you tested Him at Massah." Matthew 4:7 (BSB) quotes: "It is also written: 'Do not put the Lord your God to the test.'" The command itself matches; what Matthew's quotation does not reproduce is Deuteronomy's own closing clause naming the specific wilderness incident the command refers to -- "as you tested Him at Massah," the events of Exodus 17:1-7, where the people demanded water and accused Moses of bringing them into the wilderness to die of thirst.

Deuteronomy 6:13 (BSB): "Fear the LORD your God, serve Him only, and take your oaths in His name." Matthew 4:10 (BSB) quotes: "Worship the Lord your God and serve Him only." Here the main verb itself changes, not just the surrounding clause. Checked directly against the Septuagint's own Greek text of Deuteronomy 6:13 for this lesson, the verb is *phobethese* ("you shall fear") -- matching the Hebrew behind this app's English "Fear," not Matthew's "Worship." Matthew's Greek quotation reads *proskyneseis* ("you shall worship"), which matches neither the Hebrew nor the Septuagint text of Deuteronomy 6:13 available for this lesson. That same Greek verb, in a closely related form, is what the devil has just used one verse earlier: "if You will fall down and worship [*proskyneses*] me" (4:9, checked directly against the Greek text). Matthew's quotation keeps Deuteronomy's second clause, "serve Him only," word for word in this app's English texts of both passages, but does not reproduce Deuteronomy's third clause about oaths ("and take your oaths in His name").

**The devil's own quotation, and what it leaves out.** Psalm 91:11-12 (BSB): "For He will command His angels concerning you to guard you in all your ways. They will lift you up in their hands, so that you will not strike your foot against a stone." The devil's quotation at Matthew 4:6 (BSB): "He will command His angels concerning You, and they will lift You up in their hands, so that You will not strike Your foot against a stone." Checked directly against both texts as shipped in this app, the devil reproduces the first half of verse 11 and all of verse 12, but does not reproduce the second half of verse 11 -- "to guard you in all your ways." That is a directly checkable fact about which words appear and which do not; what to make of the omission is a separate question. Robert Gundry's commentary, reported through the Wikipedia aggregator, holds that the full verse "makes it clear that God is promising only to protect from accidents, not from deliberate acts such as the one Satan is proposing" -- on this reading, the Psalm's own qualifying clause ("in all your ways," describing protection within the course of ordinary conduct) is precisely what the devil's proposal (jumping from the temple on purpose) would not fall under, which is one reported explanation for why that clause might be absent from the devil's version. This lesson reports Gundry's reading without adopting it as its own conclusion about the devil's intent, which the text itself never states.

**All three of Jesus's replies share a citation formula; the devil's own quotation does too.** "It is written" (4:4), "It is also written" (4:7), and "it is written" (4:10, within "For it is written") all introduce Jesus's quotations. The devil's quotation at 4:6 is introduced the same way: "For it is written." This is the only point in the unit where the devil, rather than Jesus, uses this formula.

## Literary Design

**Two of three temptations open with the same clause; the third does not.** "If You are the Son of God" opens both the first temptation (4:3) and the second (4:6, "If You are the Son of God," he said). The third temptation (4:8-9) opens differently -- "All this I will give You... if You will fall down and worship me" -- with no "If You are the Son of God" clause at all. Whether this patterning is deliberate or simply how three different approaches naturally vary is not something this lesson decides; what is checkable is that the clause appears twice, not three times, and where.

**A directly checkable verbal echo between the devil's own demand and Jesus's quoted answer.** The devil's third demand uses the Greek verb *proskyneses* ("worship," 4:9); Jesus's quoted reply from Deuteronomy 6:13 uses the related form *proskyneseis* ("you shall worship," 4:10) -- a verb that, as the Context section above notes, is not the verb Deuteronomy 6:13 itself uses in either the Hebrew behind this app's English text or the Septuagint's Greek text (both read "fear"). The quoted answer reuses the devil's own word rather than Deuteronomy's, which is checkable directly from the Greek text of both verses and does not depend on a secondary source for the observation itself, though this lesson's account of why Deuteronomy uses a different verb does depend on the Septuagint comparison reported in Context.

**A directly checkable echo of Elijah's forty days and angelic provision.** 1 Kings 19:5-8 (BSB) narrates an angel twice telling Elijah to "get up and eat" before he "walked forty days and forty nights until he reached Horeb, the mountain of God." Matthew 4:2 narrates Jesus "fasting forty days and forty nights," and 4:11 narrates that after the devil left, "angels came and ministered to Him." Both the forty-day span and the detail of angelic provision after a wilderness ordeal are checkable directly against both texts as shipped in this app. The Wikipedia aggregator used in this lesson reports this as a further parallel Matthew's account draws, alongside the Israel/Moses wilderness allusion discussed in Positions below; this lesson does not develop it into its own Positions entry or a separate authored connection, since the textual overlap (the forty-day number, the ministering) is real and checkable but the claim that it is a *deliberate* Elijah echo here, as opposed to a shared wilderness-ordeal convention, was not independently traced to a named scholar's own argument for this lesson -- see Sources and Limits.

**A near-verbatim parallel with Luke's own account, with three checkable differences.** Luke 4:1-13 narrates the same three temptations in a different order: bread, then the mountain and the kingdoms (4:5-8), then the temple (4:9-12) -- where Matthew's order is bread, then the temple (4:5-7), then the mountain (4:8-10). Luke's version of the first quotation stops short of Matthew's: "Man shall not live on bread alone" (Luke 4:4, BSB), with no "but on every word that comes from the mouth of God" clause at all. Luke's version of the devil's Psalm 91 quotation differs from Matthew's in exactly the clause Matthew's omits: "He will command His angels concerning You **to guard You carefully**; and they will lift You up in their hands..." (Luke 4:10-11, BSB) -- a guarding clause is present in Luke's version of the devil's quotation, though its wording ("to guard You carefully") still does not match the Psalm's own "to guard you in all your ways" exactly. All three differences are checkable directly from both Gospels' own text as shipped in this app. Luke also names the city directly -- "the devil led Him to Jerusalem" (Luke 4:9) -- where Matthew's own wording says only "the holy city" (4:5) without naming it.

## Positions

Two separate, named questions get real scholarly attention in this passage, and this lesson keeps them in two separate groups rather than resolving either.

**Is this episode also a deliberate echo of Adam's own temptation, alongside Israel's wilderness generation, or does the typology stop at Israel?**

That Jesus's forty days in the wilderness deliberately echoes Israel's forty years there, and that his three Deuteronomy citations deliberately draw on the book most concerned with that same wilderness period, is reported as a broadly shared scholarly reading. D.A. Carson, in a sermon transcript on this passage fetched directly for this lesson, states that Jesus is "the new Israel, the true locus of the people of God" who "recapitulates Israel's experience in the desert," calling it "a kind of mirror image microcosm" of Israel's own wilderness ordeal. Robert H. Gundry's commentary, reported through the Wikipedia aggregator, likewise "supports the popular view that the reference to the wilderness is an allusion to the Israelites after the Exodus and specifically to Moses." Whether that typology extends further back, to Adam, is where a real difference appears. Gundry, per the same source, "explicitly rejects the Adam/Garden of Eden comparison theory" for this passage -- the Israel/Moses allusion is affirmed, the Adam one is not. The Catholic Church's Catechism takes the wider view: §538 states that Jesus's temptations in the desert "recapitulate the temptations of Adam in Paradise and of Israel in the desert" together, and §539 names Jesus "the new Adam who remained faithful just where the first Adam had given in to temptation," alongside Jesus fulfilling "Israel's vocation" -- both typologies held at once, not one in place of the other. Carson's own statement, quoted above, names the Israel typology explicitly and does not mention Adam either way, so it is reported here only on the point it actually makes, not read as taking either side of the Adam question. This lesson reports both the narrower (Israel only) and the wider (Israel and Adam together) readings without ranking them.

**What kind of event does this narrative claim to be -- a straightforward, real encounter in every particular; an inner or visionary experience; or a question this Gospel's own genre does not require a reader to settle?**

This is a genuinely disputed question among the sources consulted for this lesson, not a settled one this lesson adjudicates.

One position holds the account as a real encounter throughout, while still recognizing that specific scenes within it may be visionary rather than straightforwardly physical. D.A. Carson, in the same sermon transcript fetched for this lesson, states of the second temptation (the temple pinnacle) that "almost certainly, this is a visionary sort of thing" and of the third (the mountain showing "all the kingdoms of the world") that it is "certainly visionary," reasoning that "you can't see all the kingdoms of the world and their splendor from a mountain no matter how high it is." On this reading, the encounter with the devil is real, but at least two of the three scenes are visionary experiences within that real encounter, not claims about ordinary physical sightlines. William Barclay is reported, through the Wikipedia aggregator, taking a related but more thoroughgoing non-literal reading on the same textual ground -- that there is "no mountain high enough in all the world to see the whole world," concluding the narrative "portrays what was going on inside Jesus' mind" more generally, not only for the two scenes Carson isolates.

A second position holds that only a minimal historical kernel underlies the account, with the specific scenes being later elaboration rather than visionary experience within a real encounter. E.P. Sanders, reported through the same aggregator, is described finding it "likely Jesus was alone for meditation at times, felt temptations, and fasted" before his ministry, but reading "the conversation with Satan, the scene at the top of the Temple, and the vision of the kingdoms" as "mythological elaborations" rather than either straightforward events or Jesus's own reported visionary experience.

A third position is genuine, acknowledged uncertainty rather than a settled reading either way. Dale C. Allison is reported, through the same aggregator, as having been "long convinced that Matthew and Luke's temptation stories were fictional stories based on Deuteronomy, but has recently expressed second thoughts" -- a named scholar changing his own mind, reported here as itself evidence that this question remains open among those who have studied it closely, not as this lesson's own reason to prefer one answer.

This lesson did not locate, among the sources consulted, a named scholar defending full, straightforward literalism for every scene in the account (including an ordinary physical sightline to "all the kingdoms of the world" from the mountain) against the visionary reading Carson and Barclay both state on that same textual ground; that absence is reported honestly in Sources and Limits rather than papered over with an invented position.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key: the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step, and a different passage element, situation, or response would be equally legitimate.

**Passage element.** Matthew 4:5-7. The devil quotes Psalm 91:11-12 accurately as far as he quotes it, omitting only "to guard you in all your ways" (4:6), to urge Jesus to jump from the temple pinnacle; Jesus declines, quoting Deuteronomy 6:16: "Do not put the Lord your God to the test."

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the devil's proposal is backed by an accurately quoted, real promise of God's protection, not an invented one, and Jesus's refusal does not dispute that the promise is real -- it names a different category (testing God) than the one the devil is inviting him into (trusting God). Inference: for an audience that already knew Psalm 91 as a genuine statement of God's care, the force of this exchange is that an accurately sourced scriptural promise can still be invoked toward the wrong act, and that recognizing this requires attending to what the promise actually covers (ordinary conduct, per Gundry's reading reported in Context) rather than only to whether a quotation is real. The Context section above supplies what is directly checkable about the quotation itself; this inference goes one step further, into what the exchange is doing rhetorically.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** An accurately quoted, genuinely true promise or principle does not, by itself, validate whatever action someone invokes it to support; the promise's own scope matters, and inviting God to prove himself by removing the ordinary means through which he is already at work is a different thing than trusting the promise as given.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the general shape of a true statement being invoked toward an act that exceeds, or departs from, what that statement actually covers. Not carried forward: the specific content of Psalm 91, the temple, or any claim that a modern reader faces a literal devil quoting Scripture aloud. The bridge holds only where a modern case shares that shape -- a real, true principle, invoked to support an act that goes beyond what the principle itself promises -- not wherever someone simply cites a Bible verse a reader happens to disagree with.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy from how this exchange is built, not a command addressed to the reader by name. Promise scope: none claimed; nothing in 4:5-7 promises a modern reader a particular outcome for any particular case of weighing a cited promise against a proposed action.

**Modern domain and situation.** Personal finances within a faith community. A member of a small group announces he is quitting a stable job with no savings and no next step lined up, telling the group "God will provide" and citing a real, true biblical statement of God's provision, expecting the group's affirmation as a sign of their own faith.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: a clarifying question, not a flat refusal or an automatic affirmation. One faithful response: a friend in the group asks what has actually been prepared or confirmed (savings, a transition plan, counsel already sought) versus what is being presumed, distinguishing trusting God's provision from testing it by removing the ordinary means -- planning, saving, counsel -- through which that provision has usually come.

**Cautions.** This is not a license to doubt every account of God's provision or to demand a fully worked-out plan before any faith-driven decision counts; many well-attested accounts of provision follow exactly this kind of step without one. It does not fit a case where a real discernment process -- counsel, prayer, confirmation from others -- has already taken place; that is a different situation from one where no such process has happened yet. This is not a substitute for a qualified financial advisor or pastor for an actual high-stakes decision; the passage offers a shape for the question, not a verdict on any particular person's situation.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Matthew's quotation at 4:10 uses the Greek verb for "worship," matching neither the Hebrew nor the Septuagint's own Greek text of Deuteronomy 6:13 (both "fear") -- and that same verb is the one the devil has just used one verse earlier. What would you need to see in the wider manuscript tradition of the Septuagint, or in other early citations of Deuteronomy 6:13, to judge whether this substitution originates with Matthew's own Gospel, with an earlier Greek textual tradition not checked for this lesson, or with the oral tradition behind it?
2. The devil's quotation of Psalm 91:11-12 omits "to guard you in all your ways." Gundry's reading (reported in Context) treats the omission as pointing toward the deliberate-acts-versus-accidents distinction. If a reader concluded the omission was simply how the devil happened to remember or compress the verse, rather than a deliberate rhetorical move, what would that do to how much weight the omission can carry as evidence about the devil's own intent, as opposed to evidence only about which words appear in the text?
3. Two readings of the Adam/Israel typology question are reported above: Gundry's Israel-only reading and the Catechism's Israel-and-Adam reading. What would you need to find in Matthew's own Gospel, outside this specific passage, to judge whether Matthew's own text gives any evidence either way, as opposed to the question depending entirely on a reader's prior theological commitments?
4. Three positions on what kind of event this narrative claims to be are reported above (a real encounter with specific visionary scenes; a minimal historical kernel with mythological elaboration; genuine, acknowledged scholarly uncertainty), and this lesson could not locate a named scholar defending full physical literalism for every scene against the visionary reading two of those sources state on the same textual ground (no mountain high enough to see every kingdom). If that absence turned out to reflect a real gap in the sources consulted here, rather than a real absence of such a position in the literature, what would change about how this lesson's report of "three positions" should be read?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate what happens in Matthew 4:1-11 in order -- the Spirit leading Jesus into the wilderness, the forty-day fast, the three temptations and Jesus's three answers, and the devil's departure -- before saying what any of it means. (b) State, in the text's own words, what each of Jesus's three quotations is drawn from, and name the one place in this unit where the devil, not Jesus, quotes Scripture. (c) Name one place elsewhere in this lesson's connections where this passage's language visibly reappears, and say what kind of connection it is.

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the plain sequence of 4:1-11 and where this unit sits in the Gospel so far (see Context); (1:00-2:30) the three Deuteronomy quotations and what each one adds, drops, or changes compared to its own source text as shipped in this app (see Context); (2:30-3:30) the devil's own quotation of Psalm 91:11-12 and what it leaves out (see Context); (3:30-4:15) at least one directly checkable literary feature -- the "worship" verbal echo between 4:9 and 4:10, or the order and wording differences from Luke's parallel account (see Literary Design); (4:15-5:00) the two named-positions questions -- the scope of the Adam/Israel typology, and what kind of event this narrative claims to be -- with at least one named scholar per position (see Positions).

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first; the point of this prompt is that you do the forming, not that you pick from a list. If you are stuck, here is one worked candidate, stated at full strength: this lesson reports Carson stating that the temple-pinnacle and mountain-kingdoms scenes are "visionary," and reports that as one named position among several -- but a reader could object that calling any part of this account visionary undercuts the very point of Jesus's three real, quotable answers, since an answer given inside a vision is not obviously an answer given under the same pressure as a physically real temptation. Before you answer it, state plainly what Positions above already says: that Carson's own reading holds the encounter with the devil as real throughout, with only specific scenes read as visionary within that real encounter, and that this lesson reports his position and two others without ranking them (see Sources and Limits for what this lesson could and could not independently verify about each position).

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 4:1-11 never states that this episode is the only time Jesus faced temptation; Luke's Gospel adds, at the parallel account's own close, "When the devil had finished every temptation, he left Him until an opportune time" (Luke 4:13, BSB) -- a statement about more to come that this specific passage, in Matthew's own wording, does not make. Whatever a reader concludes about Jesus facing temptation elsewhere rests on texts outside this passage, such as Hebrews 4:15's claim that he "was tempted in every way that we are" (see the Hebrews connection below), not on anything 4:1-11 itself states about its own singularity or repetition. (b) Matthew 4:1-11 never states how the devil appeared, whether visibly or audibly, or by what means Jesus was shown "all the kingdoms of the world" (4:8); any claim about the mechanics of the encounter, in either direction, is an inference from outside this specific wording -- see Positions above for how that open question is reported, not resolved, in this lesson.

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection from this passage and defend it: name its type, its evidence label, and point to the words in both texts that support that grade. The five connections attached to this lesson sit at different tiers, which is the point of the exercise:

|---|---|---|---|
| Deuteronomy 8:3 | quotation | explicit | Matthew introduces it with his own citation wording ("It is written," 4:4) and quotes it directly, though the clause's own grammatical setting changes and "God" replaces "the LORD" -- a Septuagint-level difference, checked directly against the Greek, not a Matthean one |

Choose one row and defend it in your own words, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` instead: name a candidate connection you were drawn to (the forty-days/angelic-provision echo of Elijah at 1 Kings 19:5-8, or 1 Corinthians 10:13's statement that "God is faithful" in temptation) and say what evidence is missing before it could be graded. A useful test case is 1 Corinthians 10:13 itself: it is a real, true statement about temptation in general, but it never mentions Jesus's own wilderness temptation, never quotes this passage, and sits inside a chapter about Israel's wilderness generation rather than about Jesus -- so a `no_warrant_yet` on authoring a connection between 1 Corinthians 10:13 and this specific passage, with that reason stated, is a legitimate outcome -- see Sources and Limits for why this lesson did not author one.
