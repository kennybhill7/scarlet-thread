Matthew 5:13-20 picks up the direct, second-person address the Beatitudes lesson in this curriculum already noted beginning at 5:11 ("Blessed are you..."), and keeps it running through two short metaphors (salt, light) before turning, at verse 17, to a sentence that is argued over by entire Christian traditions against each other: "Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets. I have not come to abolish them, but to fulfill them" (BSB). That one sentence, and the three verses that follow it, are read by named, in-print theological traditions in ways that produce genuinely different answers to a question with real practical weight: what, if anything, from the Mosaic Law still binds a Christian today, and in what sense. This lesson practices the same discipline the rest of this curriculum practices on harder material than usual: stating plainly what the eight verses themselves say and how they are built, before turning to the named disputes -- what "losing its savor" refers to, what the "until... accomplished" clause bounds, and above all what "fulfill" means for the Law's ongoing authority -- and reporting each dispute in its own participants' terms rather than picking a side.

## Context

**What the text says, in order (5:13-20).** "You are the salt of the earth. But if the salt loses its savor, how can it be made salty again? It is no longer good for anything, except to be thrown out and trampled by men" (5:13, BSB). "You are the light of the world. A city on a hill cannot be hidden" (5:14, BSB), followed by a lamp set on a stand rather than hidden under a basket, "that it gives light to everyone in the house" (5:15, BSB), and an explicit purpose clause: "let your light shine before men, that they may see your good deeds and glorify your Father in heaven" (5:16, BSB). Then a hinge: "Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets. I have not come to abolish them, but to fulfill them" (5:17, BSB). A temporal claim about the Law's durability: "until heaven and earth pass away, not a single jot, not a stroke of a pen, will disappear from the Law until everything is accomplished" (5:18, BSB). A graded warning about "the least of these commandments" (5:19, BSB), without the text itself specifying which commandments count as "least." And a closing statement that sets up everything from 5:21 onward: "unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven" (5:20, BSB).

**Where this passage sits.** This is the tenth unit of the pilot curriculum's Matthew sequence, following the Beatitudes (5:1-12, also stage 6) and continuing the same scene -- Jesus still seated, still addressing disciples directly. The Beatitudes lesson in this curriculum notes a grammatical shift at 5:11, from third-person description ("Blessed are those who...") to direct second-person address ("Blessed are you..."). That second-person address does not stop at verse 12: "You are the salt of the earth... You are the light of the world" (5:13-14, BSB) continues it without a grammatical break, a checkable continuity across the chapter division this lesson's own passage range imposes but the Greek text does not.

**Two identity statements, not two commands.** Both metaphors open in the indicative, not the imperative: "You are the salt" and "You are the light" state something already true of the audience, before any instruction follows. The commands that follow are different in kind for each: the salt saying's instruction is implicit in a warning (what happens if salt stops being salty), while the light saying's instruction is explicit and separate ("let your light shine," 5:16, an imperative verb distinct from the identity statement "You are the light" at 5:14). This is a grammatical fact checkable directly from the English (and, behind it, the Greek indicative-then-imperative pattern); what it is then taken to mean about whether the disciples' identity precedes or depends on their conduct is a further question this lesson does not resolve here.

**A real chemical anomaly behind the salt saying.** Pure sodium chloride does not chemically degrade into something saltless while remaining visibly salt-like; this is reported as a basic chemical fact by more than one source behind this lesson's research (Wikipedia's article on Matthew 5:13 and Ken Montgomery's Themelios article both note it). That anomaly is not itself the dispute -- it is the reason the dispute in Positions below exists at all: if "losing its savor" cannot describe pure salt's own chemistry changing, something else must be going on, and named scholars disagree about what.

**The purpose clause at 5:16 names an audience and a result, not a private virtue.** "That they may see your good deeds and glorify your Father in heaven" (5:16, BSB) specifies both who the light's visibility is for (an observing "they") and what the visibility is supposed to produce (God being glorified) -- explicitly not the observers simply thinking well of the disciple. This is stated outright in the text's own words, not an inference this lesson is drawing.

**The hinge at 5:17, and what it is answering.** "Do not think that..." (5:17, BSB) is a correction formula -- Matthew's Jesus addressing an inference his hearers might otherwise draw. What inference is being corrected is not stated in the verse itself; the most immediate candidate, checkable by reading forward, is the six "You have heard that it was said... But I say to you" units beginning at 5:21 (murder and anger, adultery and lust, divorce, oaths, retaliation, and love of enemies), which a hearer might mistake for Jesus setting the Law aside rather than intensifying it. That 5:17-20 precedes those six units, rather than following them, is a checkable structural fact about where Matthew places the disclaimer -- before the material that might prompt the misunderstanding, not after.

**"The least of these commandments" (5:19) is not defined by the verse that uses it.** The text grades consequences -- breaking "one of the least of these commandments" and teaching others to do likewise produces being "called least in the kingdom of heaven"; practicing and teaching them produces being "called great" (5:19, BSB) -- without stating which commandments its own phrase "these commandments" refers to, or what makes a commandment "least" rather than "great." That the verse itself leaves this undefined is a plain fact about what is and is not on the page; the named traditions in Positions below each answer the question differently.

**The "I tell you" bracket, stated carefully.** Verse 18 opens "For I tell you truly" and verse 20 opens "For I tell you" (BSB) -- both verses in this short unit open with Jesus's own first-person asseveration, bracketing the "least of these commandments" statement at verse 19 between them. The two openings are not word-for-word identical (verse 18 alone carries "truly," rendering the Greek *amēn*); this lesson states the bracket as a real but not perfectly symmetrical pattern, rather than overstating it as an exact inclusio.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 5:13-20 is built, not on what that construction is meant to teach.

**Paired cosmic-scope nouns opening each metaphor.** "Salt of the earth" and "light of the world" (5:13-14, BSB) each pair a everyday object (salt, light) with a maximal noun (earth, world) -- a checkable two-part parallel structure opening the unit, before either metaphor is developed further.

**The lamp's placement, not its lighting, carries the saying's weight.** 5:15 does not describe a lamp being lit (that is presupposed) but a choice about where a lit lamp is placed -- "under a basket" against "on a stand" (BSB) -- and states a consequence for each ("it gives light to everyone in the house" for the stand). The saying's force sits entirely in that placement choice, a detail worth noting before assuming the point is simply "shine, don't hide," which collapses the placement/lighting distinction the verse itself makes.

**5:17-20 as a four-part unit building toward 5:21.** Read in sequence, the four verses move from a general claim about Jesus's own relationship to "the Law or the Prophets" (5:17) to an absolute temporal claim about the Law's durability (5:18) to a graded warning about individual commandments (5:19) to a comparative statement about righteousness that reaches past the scribes and Pharisees (5:20) -- general to absolute to specific to comparative, each verse narrower in scope than the one before it, ending exactly where 5:21's first "You have heard that it was said" begins. Whether Matthew built this progression deliberately or it reflects the material he had available is not something this lesson decides; what is checkable is that the progression exists in the order the verses are printed.

**Three repeated verbs across 5:17-19: "abolish," "fulfill," "accomplished."** "Abolish" (5:17, used twice) and "fulfill" (5:17) are stated as a contrasting pair in the same verse; "accomplished" (5:18) shares a conceptual range with "fulfill" without being the same English word, translating a different Greek verb (*plēroō* at 5:17 against *ginomai* at 5:18, per the source this lesson's Positions section cites for the Greek). That English readers encounter "fulfill" and "accomplished" as two different words for what may or may not be one underlying idea is a translation-level fact worth flagging before the Positions section below works through what each word is argued to mean.

## Positions

Three separate, named disputes attach to this passage. Each is reported here in terms its own participants use, not resolved.

**What does it mean for salt to "lose its savor" (5:13)?** Three different, named explanations are in print, and this lesson does not rank them.

One line of explanation reads the saying through the physical chemistry of ancient salt specifically, not pure sodium chloride in the abstract. Robert H. Gundry is reported, via the Wikipedia article on Matthew 5:13 used here, as holding that ancient salt was commonly impure, mixed with other compounds; because sodium chloride is highly water-soluble, exposure to moisture could dissolve away the genuine salt content, "leaving a white powder looking just like salt, but not having its flavour or its preservative abilities" -- the same article reports a similar account from Albert Barnes (1834) and a direct observational report from the nineteenth-century traveler William McClure Thomson, who described stored salt that had "entirely spoiled" and was "literally thrown into the street, to be trodden underfoot," while Charles Ellicott's commentary notes a comparable seventeenth-century travel account (Henry Maundrell) that Ellicott himself says later travelers had not confirmed.

A second line treats the saying as a deliberate impossibility, pressed for rhetorical effect rather than describing an observed chemical process. David Hill is reported, via the same Wikipedia article, as holding that Jesus knew salt cannot actually lose its flavor this way, and that the force of the saying is precisely that a disciple remaining genuinely a disciple can no more lose the relevant quality than salt can lose its own chemical nature. Separately, and read directly for this lesson, Robert H. Gundry's own commentary (as quoted in Ken Montgomery's Themelios article) adds a linguistic argument alongside whatever his impurity-based view above holds: the Greek verb Matthew uses, *mōranthē*, carries "what may have been a more obvious double entendre in Hebrew and Aramaic, where the verb *tapel* can mean both to be tasteless and foolish" -- on this reading, the saying trades on a pun available in the language Jesus likely spoke, not only on an observed physical process.

A third line reads the saying's force through salt's agricultural rather than culinary use. Anthony Bradley, writing in *Christianity Today* (as quoted and engaged directly in Montgomery's Themelios article), argues that salt functioned as a fertilizer in the period, and reads the saying as calling disciples to "get messy and go where nothing is growing right now" -- an argument built in part on Luke 14:34-35's detail that spent salt is "fit neither for the soil nor for the manure pile" (BSB). Montgomery himself, in the same article, disputes this specific reading: citing W. F. Albright and C. S. Mann's observation that "oversalination can and does effectively render land infertile," he argues the Lukan detail shows worthless salt has no agricultural use at all, not that good salt was itself used as a fertilizer additive. This lesson reports both sides of that specific disagreement without adjudicating it; see the connections to Mark 9:50 and Luke 14:34-35 below for how the parallel sayings bear on it.

**What does the "until heaven and earth pass away... until everything is accomplished" clause (5:18) bound?** This is a narrower, more technical dispute than the "fulfill" question below, though it bears directly on it. Eduard Schweizer is reported, via the Wikipedia article on Matthew 5:18 used here, as reading "until heaven and earth pass away" as itself an eschatological marker -- the Mosaic Law's binding force lasting "until the end times, and will be superseded in the messianic age" -- and as connecting this clause to the plainly eschatological statement at Mark 13:31 ("heaven and earth will pass away, but My words will never pass away," BSB). R. T. France, reported by the same article, disagrees specifically with Schweizer's reading of this clause: France reads "until heaven and earth pass away" as "simply an idiom for the inconceivable," an emphatic way of saying "never," not a literal temporal marker pointing to a future eschatological event at all. The same article further reports France naming three live candidates for what "until everything is accomplished" refers to -- the end of the world, the completion of all the Law's own requirements, or the arrival of the Messiah -- noting that these three "imply very different understandings of how absolute Mosaic law is for Christians in the current era," without the article reporting France himself choosing among the three.

**What does Jesus mean by "fulfill" the Law (5:17), and what from it still binds?** This is the passage's largest and most consequential dispute, argued across entire Christian traditions, and this lesson reports four of them in terms each tradition states for itself, declining to rank them.

Covenant theology, in the words of the Westminster Confession of Faith (chapter 19, read directly for this lesson), divides the Mosaic Law into three parts with three different fates. The moral law, "delivered by God upon Mount Sinai, in ten commandments," continues to bind: "The moral law doth for ever bind all, as well justified persons as others, to the obedience thereof," and this "not only in regard of the matter contained in it, but also in respect of the authority of God the Creator" (19.2, 19.5). The ceremonial laws, by contrast, "are now abrogated, under the new testament" (19.3). The judicial laws given to Israel as a nation "expired together with the state of that people; not obliging any other now, further than the general equity thereof may require" (19.4). On this reading, "fulfill" does not mean every commandment keeps binding in its original form; it means the moral law keeps binding permanently while the ceremonial and civil law's obligation has ended, with Christ's fulfillment explaining why the ceremonial law in particular is no longer owed.

Dispensationalism, represented here through a Bible-study page (versebyversecommentary.com, read directly for this lesson) that embeds Joel T. Williamson Jr.'s article "The Sabbath and Dispensationalism" and quotes Charles C. Ryrie within it, reads the Mosaic Law as a single, unified code given to Israel under a specific dispensation -- a code the church is simply not under, in any of its parts, because "God set aside the nation Israel with all the laws that pertain to her when He launched the church." On this reading, Jesus commanding His disciples to keep the Law during His own earthly ministry (prior to the church's founding) reflects the dispensation in force at that time, not a standing obligation carried into the church age; the Sabbath commandment specifically, on this view, "has been abolished" for the church along with the rest of the Mosaic code, not singled out and retained the way covenant theology retains the moral law as a category. Ryrie's own book was not independently read for this lesson; this report rests on the embedded quotations the page itself carries, flagged further in Sources and Limits.

New Covenant Theology, in the words of John G. Reisinger's chapter on this exact passage (*Continuity and Discontinuity*, crosstocrown.org, read directly for this lesson), draws the continuity/discontinuity line in a different place than either of the two traditions above: "The Old Covenant... has indeed passed away -- there is complete discontinuity. The Old Testament Scriptures... remain as Scripture -- there is total continuity." On this reading, "fulfill" does not mean Jesus adding to, subtracting from, or partially retaining the Mosaic code category by category; it means the entire Old Testament pointed toward the Messiah (citing Luke 24:44) and Jesus himself was that fulfillment, not merely its teacher or exemplar -- "Jesus 'was' the fulfillment, not merely teaching or exemplifying it." Reisinger's own chapter treats the Mosaic Law, understood as the Old Covenant specifically, as having ended entirely as a direct legal code for the believer, with whatever continues to apply doing so because it is restated as part of "the law of Christ," not because it is still owed as Mosaic law. This differs from covenant theology's moral/ceremonial/judicial division by treating the Mosaic Law's 613 commandments as a single covenantal unit rather than three separately-fated categories, and from dispensationalism by reading "fulfill" as something Jesus himself constitutively was, not primarily a statement about which dispensation is currently in force.

The Catholic Church's Catechism (§§577-582, read directly for this lesson) states that "the perfect fulfillment of the Law could be the work of none but the divine legislator, born subject to the Law in the person of the Son" (§580), and that "Jesus did not abolish the Law but fulfilled it by giving its ultimate interpretation in a divine way" (§581) -- teaching with an authority the Catechism describes as not merely proposing an interpretation alongside others, but as the lawgiver's own. On this reading, Jesus's fulfillment is not primarily about sorting the Mosaic code into categories that continue or lapse (as in covenant theology), nor about a dispensational transition (as in dispensationalism), nor about Christ constituting the fulfillment of the whole Old Testament's forward pointing (as in New Covenant Theology's own framing) -- it centers on Jesus's own person and authority revealing the Law's deepest and divinely intended sense, with the Law's "ultimate interpretation" now given by the one who gave the Law in the first place.

These four traditions are not simply four labels for the same conclusion. They agree that Jesus's own statement is "fulfill," not "abolish," and each takes that word seriously as its own tradition's own stated starting point; they disagree, in ways with real practical consequence, about which commandments (if any) remain directly binding on a Christian today and in what sense "fulfill" explains that. Wikipedia's article on Matthew 5:17 (used here, and not developed further in this lesson beyond this notice) reports two further, more extreme named positions bracketing the four above -- an antinomian reading, under which Christ's fulfillment "made it unnecessary for anyone to do anything further" with the Law at all, and a Torah-observant Christian reading, under which the Mosaic Law remains "entirely applicable to Christians... for simple obedience" rather than for salvation -- which this lesson names without developing, so that the four traditions reported above are not mistaken for the only positions that exist.

**Where these disputes meet.** The salt dispute and the "fulfill" dispute are not the same kind of question -- one is about a metaphor's referent, the other about an entire covenant's ongoing authority -- but both share the same shape this curriculum keeps naming: a text that states something plainly (salt can lose its savor; Jesus has come to fulfill, not abolish, the Law) while leaving open, in its own wording, exactly what that plain statement is describing or requiring. This lesson states the plain wording and reports the named disputes; it does not close either gap.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage states in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. This is a model of the shape, not an answer key -- the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 5:15-16: "Neither do people light a lamp and put it under a basket. Instead, they set it on a stand, and it gives light to everyone in the house. In the same way, let your light shine before men, that they may see your good deeds and glorify your Father in heaven" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the saying's two household details (basket, stand) describe a placement choice for a lamp already lit, not the act of lighting one; the purpose clause that follows (5:16) names a specific intended result -- observers glorifying "your Father in heaven," not the disciple. Inference: for a first-century household audience, a lamp placed under a basket was not a neutral or modest choice but a self-defeating one, since a household lamp's entire function depended on its visibility to everyone in the house; applied to the disciples, the saying treats concealing one's own "good deeds" as similarly self-defeating against the deeds' own stated purpose, which is not the disciple's own credit. The Context section above states the purpose clause's own wording directly; this inference about what a hidden lamp would have meant to a first-century household goes one step further than that cited wording, and is labeled as this lesson's own step.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Visible good conduct is meant to direct an observer's attention toward God, not toward the person acting -- which makes both total concealment and self-promoting display failures of the same stated purpose, for different reasons.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the distinction between visibility aimed at pointing beyond oneself and visibility aimed at drawing credit to oneself, and the refusal to treat concealment itself as the safer or more humble option when the deed's own purpose requires it to be seen. Not carried forward: the specific first-century household objects (basket, lampstand) or any claim about how public a specific modern action must be to count as "shining."

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy from the passage's own stated purpose clause, not a command addressed to the reader by name beyond the one Matthew's text already states ("let your light shine," 5:16). Promise scope: none claimed; nothing in 5:15-16 promises a specific modern outcome for a specific modern act of visibility.

**Modern domain and situation.** Workplace integrity. An employee privately corrects a billing error that overcharged a client, something no one else would have noticed, and then has to decide how to handle a new coworker's later question about why the team's invoicing process includes an unusual extra verification step the employee quietly introduced after that incident.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: visibility calibrated to the deed's own stated purpose (so observers draw a conclusion about the standard being upheld), not performance aimed at personal credit, and not concealment that withholds a true, relevant answer. One faithful response: the employee answers the coworker's question honestly and specifically -- naming what happened and why the step exists -- without either downplaying it into vagueness or steering the story toward their own credit for catching it.

**Cautions.** This does not mean every private good act must be made public, and visibility is not a goal to pursue for its own sake apart from the deed's own purpose -- most private acts of integrity are rightly left private. It does not fit a situation where disclosure would expose confidential client or employee information inappropriately, or where a real whistleblower question (fraud, safety, legal exposure) is at stake; that situation calls for a qualified person -- compliance, legal counsel, or an appropriate outside authority -- not a private calculus about lamps and baskets. The passage is not a disclosure policy; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing, and a different reader may find it does not hold for their own case.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. The impurity reading and the rhetorical-impossibility reading of "loses its savor" (5:13) can both explain the same verse without contradicting each other -- a saying can use a real physical phenomenon rhetorically. What would you need to see, in the text itself or in what is independently known about first-century salt, to judge whether one reading is doing more explanatory work than the other, rather than concluding both simply apply at once?
2. Covenant theology, dispensationalism, New Covenant Theology, and Catholic teaching all read the same Greek word, "fulfill," and all explicitly reject "abolish" as Jesus's own meaning -- yet they reach different practical conclusions about what currently binds. What would you need to see in Matthew's own later use of "fulfill" (the fulfillment-formula quotations in Matthew 1-2, already noted elsewhere in this curriculum) to judge whether Matthew's own usage elsewhere supports any one of these four traditions' reading of 5:17 more than the others?
3. Schweizer reads "until heaven and earth pass away" (5:18) as a real eschatological boundary; France reads the same clause as mere emphatic idiom for "never." What kind of evidence -- about how this exact idiom is used elsewhere in Matthew, or in other first-century Jewish literature -- would actually distinguish those two readings, rather than leaving each reader free to pick whichever fits a Law-and-covenant position they already hold?
4. Verse 19 warns about breaking "the least of these commandments" without the verse itself specifying which commandments are "least." Each of the four traditions in Positions above effectively supplies its own answer (ceremonial law, the whole Mosaic code, the Decalogue minus the Sabbath, or the Law's "ultimate interpretation" read through Christ). If you set all four aside, what would Matthew's own Gospel -- his handling of Sabbath controversies, dietary questions, or Temple practice elsewhere -- need to show you before you could propose a fifth answer of your own?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) state in order what 5:13-20 says happens or is said, verse by verse, before saying anything about what any of it means for Christian practice today. (b) State the one grammatical continuity this passage shares with its immediate predecessor (the direct second-person address beginning at 5:11), and name the two identity statements ("You are...") that continue it. (c) Name one place elsewhere in Scripture where this passage's language visibly reappears, and say what kind of connection it is: a parallel saying, a shared motif, or something else.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the plain sequence of 5:13-20 and the indicative-then-imperative pattern in the salt and light sayings (see Context); (1:00-2:00) the four-part progression from 5:17 to 5:20 and the repeated "I tell you" bracket at vv.18 and 20, stated as textual observations (see Literary Design); (2:00-3:30) the salt dispute, naming at least two of the three reported proposals by the scholar who holds each (see Positions); (3:30-4:30) the "fulfill" dispute, naming at least two of the four traditions in their own stated terms, without picking one (see Positions); (4:30-5:00) one open question from above, stated on purpose as the last thing taught.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first; the point of this prompt is that you do the forming, not that you pick from a list. If you are stuck, here is one worked candidate, stated at full strength: naming four Christian traditions side by side on "fulfill" without picking one is itself a kind of evasion -- the text says something specific, the stakes (what currently binds a Christian) are too practically important to leave unresolved, and a study lesson that reports four answers without preferring any of them is quietly teaching relativism about the Law rather than teaching the passage. Before you answer it, state what this lesson's own Positions section already does: it quotes each tradition's own stated document directly (the Westminster Confession, Reisinger's chapter, the Catechism, and Ryrie as embedded in a dispensational source) rather than paraphrasing a position no one actually holds, and it names the specific practical disagreement (which commandments, in what sense) rather than only the shared agreement ("fulfill," not "abolish"). A second worked candidate, running the other direction: if the four traditions truly agree that Jesus said "fulfill" and meant it as continuity rather than abolition, is the real dispute more about which parts of the Mosaic Law are properly called "the Law" that Jesus is talking about in the first place, rather than about what "fulfill" itself means?
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 5:13-20 does not, in its own wording, specify what causes salt to "lose its savor" or define which commandments count as "the least of these commandments" (5:19) -- both are the text's own open terms, not claims this lesson is reporting as settled and then withholding. (b) Matthew 5:17's "fulfill" is stated as Jesus's own claim about His relationship to "the Law or the Prophets"; the verse itself does not state a worked-out doctrine of which specific Mosaic commandments continue to bind a Christian and in what way -- that further step is each named tradition's own development of the verse, not the verse's own stated content.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection from this passage and defend it: name its type, its evidence label, and point to the words in both texts that support that grade. - **Romans 10:4 and Galatians 3:24 were considered as connections and declined.** Both state something about Christ's relationship to "the law" in Paul's own voice, and both were genuinely tempting candidates given this passage's own "fulfill" language. They are not authored as connections here because the textual link is a generic shared theme ("the law," "Christ") rather than a specific verbal or structural echo of Matthew 5:17-20 itself, and because grading either connection above `plausible` risked quietly importing one Pauline reading of "the law" as if it settled Matthew 5:17's own, differently-worded claim -- exactly the kind of move this lesson's Positions section works to avoid. This is reported as a `no_warrant_yet` candidate in Teach-Back Prompt 5, not silently omitted.

The connection is graded `plausible`, not `strong`, for that reason.
