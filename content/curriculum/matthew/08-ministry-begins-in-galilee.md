The temptation narrative that precedes this unit (4:1-11) ends with the devil's departure and angels ministering to Jesus; this unit opens with no stated interval at all -- "When Jesus heard that John had been imprisoned, He withdrew to Galilee" (4:12, BSB) moves straight from the wilderness to a piece of news the text has not yet narrated the circumstances of. What follows in these fourteen verses covers more narrative ground than any other unit this curriculum has reached so far in Matthew: a geographic relocation tied to an explicit fulfillment-citation of Isaiah (4:12-16); a one-sentence summary of Jesus's own preaching message that turns out, checked directly against this app's own shipped text, to be identical word for word to John the Baptist's message at 3:2 (4:17); the calling of the first four disciples, narrated with no stated reason for their immediate response (4:18-22); and a closing summary of an early ministry already too large for this passage to narrate in detail (4:23-25). This lesson keeps several different kinds of claim in different places. Some things are directly checkable textual facts: the exact wording of the Isaiah quotation against both texts as shipped in this app; the exact, word-for-word recurrence of 3:2 at 4:17; and the exact overlaps and differences among this passage's own calling account and its parallels in Mark and Luke. Some things are named scholarly positions on questions this passage leaves genuinely open: why four fishermen left everything at once, and how Luke's account of a miraculous catch of fish relates to the plainer calling Matthew and Mark both narrate. This is the same discipline the first seven units of this Matthew sequence already practice.

## Context

**What happens, in order (4:12-25).** "When Jesus heard that John had been imprisoned, He withdrew to Galilee" (4:12, BSB) -- a piece of news Matthew's own narrative has not yet told its readers the circumstances of; the arrest and its circumstances are narrated only much later, at 14:1-12, and even there Matthew's own text has already referred to John as "in prison" once before that, at 11:2. "Leaving Nazareth, He went and lived in Capernaum, which is by the sea in the region of Zebulun and Naphtali" (4:13) -- a second move, following the earlier settlement in Nazareth this curriculum's lesson on the flight into Egypt already covered (2:23). Matthew states this "to fulfill what was spoken through the prophet Isaiah" (4:14) and quotes: "Land of Zebulun and land of Naphtali, the Way of the Sea, beyond the Jordan, Galilee of the Gentiles—the people living in darkness have seen a great light; on those living in the land of the shadow of death, a light has dawned" (4:15-16). From that time on Jesus began to preach, "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is near" (4:17). Walking beside the Sea of Galilee, Jesus sees "two brothers, Simon called Peter and his brother Andrew," casting a net, and says, "Come, follow Me... and I will make you fishers of men" (4:18-19); "at once they left their nets and followed Him" (4:20). Going on, he sees "two other brothers, James son of Zebedee and his brother John," in a boat with their father, mending nets, and "immediately they left the boat and their father and followed Him" (4:21-22). Matthew closes the unit with a summary: Jesus "teaching in their synagogues, preaching the gospel of the kingdom, and healing every disease and sickness among the people" (4:23), news spreading "all over Syria," and crowds following him "from Galilee, the Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea, and beyond the Jordan" (4:24-25). That is the whole of what this specific range narrates; the Sermon on the Mount that follows (5:1-12, covered in another unit of this sequence) belongs to a later unit, not this one.

**Where this passage sits.** This is the eighth unit of the pilot curriculum's Matthew sequence, following the temptation narrative (4:1-11) and preceding the Beatitudes (5:1-12) -- a structural position in how this app organizes its material, not a claim this lesson makes about the Gospel's own composition history. The text's own transition at 4:12 ("When Jesus heard...") is the only link Matthew's own wording supplies between the temptation and this unit; no number of days or any other interval is stated.

**The fulfillment-citation formula, checked against this curriculum's own already-established pattern.** The lesson on John the Baptist's preaching (3:1-12, already covered in this sequence) established that Matthew's chapters 1-2 use a recurring "fulfilled... spoken through the prophet(s)" formula (1:22; 2:15; 2:17; 2:23), and that 3:3 instead uses a weaker "spoken of through the prophet Isaiah" wording for John the Baptist -- reported there, via David Hill's commentary, as Matthew reserving the stronger "fulfilled" language for events concerning Jesus himself. Checked directly against this app's own shipped text, 4:14 reads "to fulfill what was spoken through the prophet Isaiah" -- the stronger "fulfill" verb is back, exactly where the subject fulfilling the prophecy (Jesus's own move to Capernaum) is Jesus himself again, not a forerunner. This is a directly checkable continuation of the pattern this curriculum's own prior lesson already named; this lesson does not re-argue Hill's proposed reason for the pattern, only notes that this instance is consistent with it.

**The Isaiah 9:1-2 quotation, checked word for word against both texts as shipped in this app.** Isaiah 9:1-2 (BSB) reads: "Nevertheless, there will be no more gloom for those in distress. In the past He humbled the land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali, but in the future He will honor the Way of the Sea, beyond the Jordan, Galilee of the nations: The people walking in darkness have seen a great light; on those living in the land of the shadow of death, a light has dawned." Matthew 4:15-16 (BSB) quotes: "Land of Zebulun and land of Naphtali, the Way of the Sea, beyond the Jordan, Galilee of the Gentiles—the people living in darkness have seen a great light; on those living in the land of the shadow of death, a light has dawned." Checked directly against both texts as shipped in this app, two differences are immediately checkable without any secondary source. First, Matthew's quotation drops Isaiah's own opening sentence and its past/future contrast entirely -- "there will be no more gloom," "in the past He humbled... but in the future He will honor" -- and presents the four place names (Zebulun, Naphtali, the Way of the Sea, Galilee) as a bare list, with no verb at all until the second half of the quotation. Second, in that second half, exactly one word changes: Isaiah's "the people **walking** in darkness" becomes Matthew's "the people **living** in darkness"; the rest of that sentence -- "have seen a great light; on those living in the land of the shadow of death, a light has dawned" -- is identical, word for word, in both texts as shipped in this app. "Galilee of the Gentiles" (Matthew) against "Galilee of the nations" (Isaiah) is this app's own translation convention for two different source-language words that are, per the Greek New Testament and the Septuagint both report the same underlying term (reported below), the same word -- a rendering choice similar to the LORD/Lord convention this curriculum's earlier lessons already noted, not a textual variant between the two passages.

**The same comparison, checked one level further, against the Greek -- with a limitation stated plainly.** This lesson fetched Bible Hub's Greek interlinear pages for Matthew 4:15-16 and the Septuagint's Isaiah 9:1-2 through an automated fetch-and-summarize tool, not by independently reading the Greek font character by character the way a human researcher with direct page access would; the findings below should be treated as a first-pass check, not a final one, and are flagged accordingly in Sources and Limits. Within that limitation, the fetched pages report that Matthew's Greek for "Galilee of the Gentiles" is *Galilaia ton ethnon*, and the Septuagint's Greek for "Galilee of the nations" at Isaiah 9:1 is also *ton ethnon* -- the same Greek phrase behind both of this app's different English renderings. The fetched pages also report a real asymmetry in verse 2's Greek: Matthew's quotation uses the identical participle (*kathemenos*, "sitting" or "dwelling") for both "the people" and "those in... the land... of death," while the Septuagint's Isaiah 9:2 is reported using two different participles, *poreuomenos* ("walking" or "traveling") for "the people" and *katoikountes* ("dwelling") for the second group -- which, if accurately reported, would mean Matthew's Greek quotation regularizes a wording the Septuagint's own Greek does not regularize, and would line up with the one-word English difference ("walking" against "living") already noted directly above from this app's own shipped text. This lesson reports that finding with the stated limitation on how it was checked; the English-level comparison in the paragraph above rests on no such limitation, since it compares this app's own two shipped texts directly.

**The message itself: an exact, checkable recurrence of 3:2, not developed into a formal connection here.** "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is near" (4:17) is, checked directly against this app's own shipped text, word for word identical to John the Baptist's own message already reported in this curriculum's lesson on 3:1-12: "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is near" (3:2). This is a real, checkable fact about the text, not a claim resting on any secondary source. This lesson does not author a formal `content/connections/` entry for it; see Literary Design, below, for how it is reported instead, and Sources and Limits for why.

**What this unit does not explain about the calling itself.** 4:18-22 narrates two callings and two immediate responses in the text's own words -- "at once they left their nets" (4:20), "immediately they left the boat and their father" (4:22) -- without stating anywhere in this specific range why the response was immediate: whether these four men already knew Jesus, what (if anything) had already passed between them, or how much time the narrative compresses. Named scholarly readings of this gap are reported in Positions, below.

## Literary Design

**The fulfillment-formula pattern, extended by this unit, not newly discovered by it.** As Context above already states directly from the shipped text, 4:14's "to fulfill what was spoken through the prophet Isaiah" continues -- rather than introduces -- the pattern this curriculum's lesson on 3:1-12 already named: "fulfill" language for Jesus himself, a weaker "spoken of" framing reserved for John the Baptist at 3:3. This lesson treats the continuation as checkable and does not re-litigate the earlier lesson's own report of Hill's proposed reason for it.

**A compressed quotation: what Matthew's version of Isaiah 9:1-2 removes, not just what it changes.** R.T. France, reported by the Wikipedia article on Matthew 4:14 used here, states that Matthew "abbreviates" Isaiah's own wording "considerably," omitting grammatical material that makes the Isaiah passage read as a continuous sentence in its own setting. Context above already shows directly what that abbreviation removes: Isaiah's own past/future reversal ("in the past He humbled... but in the future He will honor") disappears entirely, leaving only the place names and the light/darkness couplet. Eduard Schweizer, reported by the same article, is described finding it "odd" that "beyond the Jordan" survives into Matthew's version at all, since that phrase reflects a describer standing east of the Jordan looking toward Galilee -- a perspective foreign to Galilee itself, and one Matthew's own quotation keeps without updating. This lesson reports both observations without adopting either as settled; Robert H. Gundry, reported by the same article, is described reading the whole citation as reworked by Matthew to center on the Sea of Galilee specifically, a claim this lesson does not independently verify.

**A verbatim, intra-Matthew recurrence, reported here rather than as a formal connection.** As Context above already establishes directly from the shipped text, 4:17 repeats 3:2 word for word. This lesson follows the same precedent already set earlier in this curriculum for an in-book recurrence: the three-occurrence Hebrew word study already tracked across the Genesis sequence's lessons on 3:16-17 and 5:29 was reported as a textual observation within those lessons' own prose, not authored as a separate `content/connections/` file, because the recurrence sits inside one book this curriculum is already covering lesson by lesson, and a formal connection file exists to link two different passages a reader might not otherwise read side by side. This lesson treats 3:2/4:17 the same way. What a named source does say about the recurrence: Thomas G. Long (1997), reported by the Wikipedia article on Matthew 4:17 used here, offers a comparison for what stays the same and what changes -- "the message by John the Baptist is like a person pointing to the cloud on the horizon and saying that the rain is near, whereas the message by Jesus is saying that the kingdom begins to happen and through him." Pseudo-Chrysostom, reported by the same article, states the same point more tersely: "the message of the kingdom has not changed, but the messenger has." Both readings agree the words are identical and locate the difference somewhere other than the words themselves; this lesson reports both without adopting either as its own conclusion about what that difference consists of.

**Two pairs of two brothers, built from the same shape twice.** 4:18-20 and 4:21-22 are built from an identical sequence, checkable by reading the two halves side by side: Jesus sees two named brothers at their trade (casting a net; mending nets), speaks or calls, and an adverb of immediacy ("at once," "immediately") introduces their leaving everything to follow. The repetition of this exact shape, twice in three verses, is a feature of how the text is built; what a reader concludes about why the men responded as they did is a separate question, taken up in Positions below.

**A directly checkable grammatical shift between "immediately" in Matthew and in Mark.** Warren Carter's 1997 article, reported by the Wikipedia article on Matthew 4:22 used here, is described noting that Matthew's "immediately" (4:22) grammatically modifies the disciples' own action -- they leave immediately -- while Mark's parallel wording (1:20) instead has the adverb modify Jesus's own action: "Immediately Jesus called them" (BSB), with the disciples' own leaving stated afterward, unmodified by "immediately" in the English as shipped in this app. This lesson checked that specific claim directly against this app's own shipped text of both verses and confirms it holds at the English level: Matthew 4:22's "and immediately they left" places the adverb with the disciples' response; Mark 1:20's "Immediately Jesus called them, and they left..." places it with Jesus's own prior action instead. What, if anything, follows theologically from that placement is Carter's own further argument, reported in Positions below, not re-argued independently here.

**What is shared and what differs between Matthew's and Mark's calling accounts, checked directly.** Matthew 4:19-20 reads, in its own words: "Come, follow Me," Jesus said, "and I will make you fishers of men." And at once they left their nets and followed Him. This is identical, word for word, to Mark 1:17-18's wording of the same exchange, checked directly against both texts as shipped in this app. Three differences are equally checkable. First, Matthew 4:18 already calls Simon "Peter" ("Simon called Peter"); Mark 1:16 names him only "Simon," reserving the renaming for later in Mark's own narrative (3:16, outside this lesson's range). Second, Matthew 4:21 states the second pair's father was present "with their father Zebedee" in the very sentence describing them mending nets; Mark 1:19's parallel sentence names James and John mending nets without stating their father was present in that moment, though Mark's very next verse (1:20) does then name him. Third, Mark 1:20 adds a detail with no counterpart anywhere in Matthew's version: the brothers left their father "in the boat with the hired men" -- a detail about who else was present that Matthew's account of the same scene does not include at all. These three differences, plus the "immediately" placement already noted above, are the basis of the connection to Mark 1:16-20 attached to this lesson.

**What is shared and what differs between Matthew's account and Luke's, checked directly -- and what Luke's account adds that has no counterpart at all.** Luke 5:1-11 narrates a setting Matthew's and Mark's accounts do not: Jesus already teaching a crowd from Simon's boat (5:1-3), a specific instruction to "put out into deep water" (5:4), Simon's own objection that "we have worked hard all night without catching anything" (5:5), a catch so large it threatens to sink two boats (5:6-7), and Simon's own response -- falling at Jesus's knees and saying, "Go away from me, Lord... for I am a sinful man" (5:8) -- before any call to follow is spoken at all. None of this has any counterpart in Matthew 4:18-22 or Mark 1:16-20. Checked directly against this app's own shipped text, Luke 5:1-11 never names Andrew; only "Simon," "Simon Peter," and "his partners James and John, the sons of Zebedee" (5:10) appear in this specific range, where Matthew and Mark both name Andrew explicitly as Simon's brother. The call itself is worded differently too: Luke's "From now on you will catch men" (5:10) shares a general sense with Matthew's and Mark's "I will make you fishers of men," but not the exact phrase; and Luke 5:1-11 records no spoken "follow Me" at all -- "they left everything and followed Him" (5:11) is stated as the men's own narrated action, not as a response to a specific command the text quotes. These checkable overlaps and differences are the basis of the connection to Luke 5:1-11 attached to this lesson; how much weight the overlaps can bear is a separate, disputed question taken up in Positions, below.

## Positions

Two separate, named questions get real scholarly attention in this passage, and this lesson keeps them in two separate groups rather than resolving either.

**Why did Peter, Andrew, James, and John respond so immediately (4:20, 4:22), and how does that relate to John's Gospel's own, different account of when Peter and Andrew first met Jesus (John 1:35-42)?**

This is a live, named question, not one this lesson adjudicates.

One family of readings holds that the four men were not strangers to Jesus at this point, and that John's Gospel's own account of an earlier first meeting explains the readiness this passage itself does not explain. Several classic commentaries, reported by the Bible Hub aggregator used here, state this directly. Ellicott's Commentary is reported stating: "In no part of the Gospel history is it more necessary to remember St. John's record as we read that of the Three, than in this call of the disciples. Here, everything seems sudden and abrupt. There we learn that those who were now called had some months before accepted Him as the Christ (John 1:35-43)." Benson's Commentary is reported stating that Andrew "had been a disciple of the Baptist," and that John's Gospel shows "they had both before been called to the knowledge of Christ, upon the banks of Jordan." Matthew Poole's Commentary, reported by the same aggregator in its comments on 4:20, states: "According to John, they were called to the knowledge of and first acquaintance with Christ while John was in the public exercise of his ministry... Probably they again returned to their old employment, and when John was imprisoned, Christ... then called them to the apostleship" -- a reading that holds both a real prior meeting (John 1) and a real return to ordinary fishing afterward, with this passage narrating a third, later, more decisive call rather than a first encounter. Matthew Henry's Concise Commentary, reported by the same aggregator, states plainly: "They had followed Christ before, as common disciples." Ian Paul, writing for Psephizo and fetched directly for this lesson rather than through a tertiary aggregator, states the same conclusion in his own words: "Although it is unlikely that this was the first encounter between Jesus and those he called, Matthew, like Mark, emphasises the immediacy and urgency of their response," adding that Matthew's own text already assumes Simon has been given the name Peter, pointing back to "an earlier meeting and the giving of this appellation" that John's Gospel narrates. Augustine, reported by the Wikipedia article on John 1:42 used here, proposes a similar account of the sequence to resolve the apparent tension: an initial encounter at the Jordan "before he had gone into Galilee," followed by a return to ordinary work, and then the later, formal call this passage narrates -- stated as one way of understanding the two Gospels together, not as a claim that either Gospel states this sequence outright.

A second emphasis, not strictly opposed to the first but answering a different question, locates the force of the immediate response in the call itself rather than in anything the men already knew. The Expositor's Greek Testament, reported by the Bible Hub aggregator used here in its comments on 4:20, states that a reader "naturally postulate[s] previous knowledge in explanation" of the abruptness, but adds that "all indications point to the uniquely impressive personality of Jesus" as a sufficient account in its own right. Ian Paul's own article, cited above, makes a related point from a different angle: he describes Jesus's call as "more a demand than an invitation," comparing it to "that of a prophet than a rabbi," and draws a parallel to Elijah's own call of Elisha (1 Kings 19:19-21, outside this lesson's range) -- a call narrated, in that Old Testament account, as producing an equally immediate response from a man with his own prior, ordinary occupation. On this reading, the immediacy is explained by the kind of call it is (a prophetic summons, not a rabbi's invitation to discuss), whether or not the hearer already knew the one calling.

What this passage itself states, read alone and without appeal to either reading above: nothing. 4:18-22 narrates the call and the response; it does not say whether Peter, Andrew, James, or John had met Jesus before, and it does not explain why the response was immediate. Both readings above are proposals for filling that silence, drawing on material outside this specific range (John's Gospel, in the first case; the kind of call narrated, in the second) -- not competing claims about what 4:18-22's own words say.

**How do Matthew's, Mark's, and Luke's three calling accounts relate to each other -- and does Luke's miraculous catch of fish belong to the same event Matthew and Mark narrate at all?**

This is a genuinely disputed textual and historical question, sharpened rather than settled by the direct comparison already reported in Literary Design, above.

Matthew's and Mark's own relationship to each other is not seriously disputed among the sources consulted for this lesson: the near-verbatim overlap already reported directly above (the call and the first response worded identically in both) is taken, across the sources used here, as two tellings of one and the same event. The live disagreement concerns Luke's account specifically.

One reading holds that Luke is narrating the same event Matthew and Mark narrate, with Luke supplying additional material -- the teaching from the boat, the miraculous catch, Simon's confession -- that Matthew and Mark either did not know or chose not to include. Several popular harmonization treatments take this view, proposing that the two Gospel traditions preserve a fuller and a more compressed telling of a single occasion. This lesson found this reading stated mainly in non-specialist, apologetics-oriented sources rather than in a named academic commentary's own argument for it, and reports it as a widely circulated popular harmonization rather than a scholarly consensus position; see Sources and Limits.

A second reading treats Luke's account as a genuinely separate occasion from the one Matthew and Mark narrate -- not a fuller telling of the same event, but a distinct call, later than the first, in which the four men moved from occasional or part-time association with Jesus to a full, final departure from their trade. This reading is also reported here as a popular harmonization proposal (sometimes framed as a "second call" or "third call" across the Gospels) rather than as an independently verified academic argument; see Sources and Limits for the same caveat.

A third reading, resting on named academic New Testament scholarship rather than popular harmonization, questions whether Luke 5:1-11 belongs with Matthew's and Mark's calling accounts at all, rather than with a different story altogether. The Wikipedia article on the miraculous catch of fish used here reports Dale Allison's own view that Luke 5:1-11 and John 21:1-10 (the post-resurrection miraculous catch, outside this lesson's range) "represent variants of the same story originally about the first resurrection appearance to Peter," though Allison is reported conceding "the case does not remove all reasonable doubt." On this reading, Luke's miraculous catch may not be an elaborated version of the plain calling Matthew and Mark narrate at all, but a story about a different, later event (Peter's post-resurrection restoration) that Luke has placed earlier in his own Gospel for his own narrative purposes -- which would make the real relationship among the three "calling" accounts more complicated than "two short tellings and one long one." Michael Wolter is reported by the same article taking the opposite view on the Luke/John relationship specifically: "there are actually no overlaps between the two" beyond "the absolutely required elements of a story concerning a catch of fish in Galilee." Mark Goodacre is reported proposing a middle position: no verbatim literary relationship between Luke 5 and John 21, but enough shared pattern that reading the two narratives "in light of" each other is worthwhile.

These three positions are not simply interchangeable. The first and second concern only how Luke's account relates to Matthew's and Mark's; the third concerns whether Luke's account is even the same kind of event, drawing in a text (John 21) outside this specific lesson's range to make that case. This lesson reports all three without ranking them, and without claiming to have independently traced the first two beyond the popular-level sources where this research found them stated.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key: the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step, and a different passage element, situation, or response would be equally legitimate.

**Passage element.** Matthew 4:18-20. Jesus says, "Come, follow Me... and I will make you fishers of men" to two men actively engaged in their trade -- "casting a net into the sea, for they were fishermen" (4:18) -- and "at once they left their nets and followed Him" (4:20).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text states what Simon and Andrew were doing at the moment of the call (working their trade) and what they did in response (left it, at once), without stating what passed through their minds or what, if anything, they already knew about Jesus -- a gap Positions above reports named readings for, without this lesson resolving it. Inference: for a first-century audience for whom a trade like fishing was both livelihood and family inheritance (James and John's own father is present in the very next scene, 4:21), narrating an immediate departure from that trade, with no stated negotiation or delay, would have registered as a response disproportionate to an ordinary invitation -- which is part of why Positions above reports named readers reaching for an explanation beyond the bare words of 4:18-20 itself, whether that explanation is a prior relationship or the distinctive force of the call. The Context and Literary Design sections above supply what is directly checkable about the sequence itself; this inference goes one step further, into why the sequence as narrated would have struck a first reader as remarkable.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** A call that asks for an immediate, visible change in life direction -- leaving a settled trade, not merely adjusting it -- is a different kind of claim than an invitation to consider something alongside one's existing commitments, and responding to it as immediate is itself a meaningful response, whatever the hearer's prior history with the one calling.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the shape of an abrupt, visible, costly change in direction made in response to a specific call, as distinct from a gradual adjustment made on one's own schedule. Not carried forward: the specific content of fishing, first-century Galilee, or any claim that every abrupt change in direction is itself evidence of a true call. The bridge holds only where a real, nameable call and a real, costly change are both present -- not wherever someone simply changes jobs.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy from how this call-and-response is narrated, not a command addressed to the reader by name. Promise scope: none claimed; nothing in 4:18-20 promises a modern reader a particular outcome for responding immediately to any particular call in their own life.

**Modern domain and situation.** Career and family business. An adult child who has worked in a family-run business since adolescence -- the business is the family's main income and the parents' own life's work -- receives an opportunity to leave it for a different vocation they believe they are called to, with no time to "try it out" first; staying or leaving both have to be decided within weeks, not discerned at leisure over years.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: a decisive, time-bound decision, not an open-ended exploration. One faithful response: the adult child names the decision honestly to the family before acting -- not hiding the departure as a drift, and not treating the family's own investment and expectations as irrelevant -- while still treating the decision itself as theirs to make within the time the opportunity allows.

**Cautions.** This is not a license to treat every abrupt departure from a family obligation as itself validated by this passage, or to dismiss a family's own legitimate stake in a shared business as mere resistance to be overcome. It does not fit a situation where leaving would abandon a genuine, otherwise-unmet dependency (small children, an aging parent with no other care arranged, a business that would collapse and harm employees who depend on it) -- those are real further considerations this passage does not address and does not override. The passage is not a career-change manual; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing, and a different reader may find it does not hold for their own case.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Matthew's quotation of Isaiah 9:1-2 drops the "in the past He humbled... but in the future He will honor" contrast entirely, keeping only the place names and the light/darkness couplet. What would you need to see elsewhere in Matthew's own Gospel, or in other first-century Jewish citation practice, to judge whether that omission is a deliberate compression serving Matthew's own point, or simply how a shorter quotation naturally results from citing only the part most directly relevant?
2. This lesson reports 4:17 as a verbatim recurrence of 3:2, treated as a textual observation rather than a formal connection, following this curriculum's own precedent for in-book recurrences (the *itstsabon* word study across the Genesis sequence). If you think a recurrence this exact -- not just a shared word, but an entire identical sentence -- deserves a formal connection regardless of being in-book, what would the evidence bar for that distinction need to be?
3. Three named positions on why Peter, Andrew, James, and John responded immediately are reported in Positions above (prior acquaintance per John's Gospel; the distinctive force of the call itself; and the plain fact that 4:18-22 itself states neither). If a reader concluded the text's own silence should simply be left as silence, rather than filled by either reading, what would that change about how confidently this passage can be taught as showing something about faith's immediacy, as several of the popular sources consulted for this lesson do?
4. The third position on Luke 5:1-11 reported in Positions above (Allison's proposal that Luke 5 and John 21 are variants of one story about a resurrection appearance) draws on a text, John 21, entirely outside this lesson's own range. ## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate what happens in Matthew 4:12-25 in order -- the withdrawal to Galilee and Capernaum, the Isaiah quotation, the preaching summary, the two callings, and the closing ministry summary -- before saying what any of it means. (b) State, in the text's own words, the one sentence this passage shares verbatim with an earlier unit in this sequence, and name which earlier passage it matches. (c) Name one place elsewhere in this lesson's connections where this passage's language or structure visibly reappears, and say what kind of connection it is.

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the plain sequence of 4:12-25 and where this unit sits between the temptation and the Beatitudes (see Context); (1:00-2:00) the Isaiah 9:1-2 quotation and what it adds, drops, or changes compared to its own source text as shipped in this app, including the one limitation on how far this lesson checked the underlying Greek (see Context); (2:00-3:00) the verbatim recurrence of 3:2 at 4:17, and why this lesson reports it as a textual observation rather than a formal connection (see Literary Design); (3:00-4:00) the directly checkable overlaps and differences among Matthew's, Mark's, and Luke's three calling accounts (see Literary Design); (4:00-5:00) the two named-positions questions -- why the response was so immediate, and how Luke's account relates to the other two -- with at least one named source per position (see Positions).

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first; the point of this prompt is that you do the forming, not that you pick from a list. If you are stuck, here is one worked candidate, stated at full strength: this lesson reports that Matthew's quotation of Isaiah 9:1-2 drops the prophecy's own past/future reversal and presents only a bare geographic list plus the light/darkness couplet -- a reader could object that this amounts to Matthew lifting a phrase out of its own context and attaching it to a new setting (Jesus settling in Capernaum) that Isaiah's own sentence was never about, which should at least raise a question about how much weight a fulfillment-citation built this way can carry. Before you answer it, state plainly what Context and Literary Design above already say: that the omission is checkable and real, that France is reported calling it a considerable abbreviation, and that this lesson does not claim to have independently judged whether that compression is a deliberate, defensible reading practice or a looser use of the source text than Isaiah's own sentence would support on its own (see Sources and Limits).

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 4:12 states that Jesus withdrew to Galilee "when" He heard John had been imprisoned, but this specific passage does not narrate who imprisoned John, why, or under what circumstances; that account comes only much later in this Gospel, at 14:1-12, outside this lesson's range, and even the brief reference to John being "in prison" at 11:2 comes before that fuller narration and also outside this lesson's range. (b) Matthew 4:18-22 does not state whether Peter, Andrew, James, or John had met Jesus before this specific calling, and does not explain why their response was immediate; any claim about either question, in either direction, draws on material from outside this specific passage -- see Positions above for how that open question is reported, not resolved, in this lesson.

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection from this passage and defend it: name its type, its evidence label, and point to the words in both texts that support that grade. The three connections attached to this lesson sit at different tiers, which is the point of the exercise:

   | Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Isaiah 9:1-2 | quotation | explicit | Matthew introduces the verse with his own fulfillment-citation wording ("to fulfill what was spoken through the prophet Isaiah," 4:14) and quotes it directly, though checked directly against both texts the quotation drops Isaiah's own past/future framing and changes one word ("walking" to "living") |
   | Mark 1:16-20 | parallel | strong | The call itself and the first response are worded identically in both texts, checked directly; the real, checkable differences (the "Peter" nickname, the father's presence, the hired men, where "immediately" grammatically attaches) are reported, not hidden |

   Choose one row and defend it in your own words, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` instead: name a candidate connection you were drawn to (the verbatim recurrence of 3:2 at 4:17, or John 1:35-42's own, different account of Peter and Andrew's first meeting with Jesus) and say what evidence is missing before it could be graded. A useful test case is John 1:35-42 itself: it is a real, directly comparable account of how Andrew and Peter first encountered Jesus, and several named commentators (reported in Positions above) read it as background that explains this passage's own immediacy -- but 4:18-22 itself never refers back to that earlier meeting, never names it, and does not state that the two accounts narrate the same relationship at two different points, so a `no_warrant_yet` on authoring a formal connection between John 1:35-42 and this specific passage, with that reason stated (a harmonization proposal resting on reading two accounts together, not a textual overlap either account itself states), is a legitimate outcome -- see Sources and Limits for why this lesson did not author one.
