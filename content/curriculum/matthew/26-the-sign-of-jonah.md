Matthew 12:38-50 closes the controversy chapter that began in the grainfields (12:1). Asked for a sign, Jesus refuses every sign but "the sign of the prophet Jonah" (12:39, BSB), and explains it with a comparison the New Testament states outright: "For as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the great fish, so the Son of Man will be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth" (12:40). This lesson checks that type, and the named references to Nineveh and the queen of Sheba, at the level of the words in the BSB, the Hebrew and the Greek Septuagint, and grades each one. It then reports three disputes without settling them: how "three days and three nights" fits the burial account, how far "this wicked generation" reaches, and who Jesus' "brothers" were. One of those questions has been used against Jewish people, and the lesson names that plainly. Every BSB quotation below was checked by script against the app's own Bible files.

## Context

**What happens, in order (12:38-50).** "Some of the scribes and Pharisees" ask for a sign (12:38, BSB). Jesus answers that "A wicked and adulterous generation demands a sign" (12:39), explains the sign of Jonah (12:40), and calls two witnesses, the men of Nineveh and the Queen of the South, who will "condemn" this generation (12:41-42). The story of the unclean spirit follows (12:43-45). Then His mother and brothers stand outside, and Jesus, "Pointing to His disciples," says, "Here are My mother and My brothers. For whoever does the will of My Father in heaven is My brother and sister and mother" (12:49-50).

**Where the passage sits.** The request for a sign comes right after the Beelzebul dispute (12:22-37), in which Jesus has just healed a man "who was blind and mute" (12:22). Ellicott (via Biblehub) reads the refusal from its context: "One sign and only one, such as they demanded, should be given." The same request and answer come back at 16:1-4, where "the Pharisees and Sadducees" ask for "a sign from heaven" and hear the same sentence, ending "except the sign of Jonah" (16:4). In the BSB, "wicked and adulterous" occurs only at 12:39 and 16:4 (a script search). Ellicott and Jamieson-Fausset-Brown read "adulterous" against the prophets' picture of Israel as God's unfaithful wife (Jeremiah 3; Hosea 1-2); Barnes and Gill add a literal sense.

**Jonah 1:17 and the Hebrew numbering.** The verse behind 12:40 is Jonah 1:17 in English Bibles: "Now the LORD had appointed a great fish to swallow Jonah, and Jonah spent three days and three nights in the belly of the fish" (BSB). In Hebrew Bibles the same verse is 2:1; Hebrew chapter 1 has 16 verses (checked in the Masoretic text on Sefaria). Matthew's clause follows the Greek Septuagint of that verse closely. Swete's Septuagint reads *kai en Ionas en te koilia tou ketous treis hemeras kai treis nyktas*, "and Jonah was in the belly of the sea creature three days and three nights" (this lesson's translation). In the Greek editions Biblehub lists, the twelve words after Matthew's opening "For as" match those twelve words in the same order (a script comparison; one edition, the Greek Orthodox Church text of 1904, differs slightly and shares ten). The Pulpit Commentary says plainly: "Verbally from the LXX. of Jonah 1:17 (2:1)." The phrase "three days and three nights" occurs in only three verses of the shipped BSB: 1 Samuel 30:12, Jonah 1:17 and Matthew 12:40.

**"The heart of the earth."** That phrase occurs only at 12:40 in the BSB. Commentators read via Biblehub hear in it Jonah's prayer: "For You cast me into the deep, into the heart of the seas" (Jonah 2:3, BSB; Swete's Septuagint, *kardias thalasses*, "heart of the sea"). They differ on what it names. Barnes: "It means, here, to be in the grave or sepulchre." Ellicott holds that it "means more than the rock-hewn sepulchre, and implies the descent into Hades, the world of the dead"; Meyer agrees, against what he calls "the greater number of expositors."

**Nineveh (12:41).** Jonah 3 tells the event: Jonah proclaims, "Forty more days and Nineveh will be overturned!" (3:4, BSB); "the Ninevites believed God" (3:5); the king calls everyone to "turn from his evil ways" (3:8); and "When God saw their actions—that they had turned from their evil ways—He relented" (3:10). The Greek is worth one look. Matthew's word for "preaching," *kerygma*, is the Septuagint's noun at Jonah 3:2. But Matthew's verb for what the Ninevites did, "repented" (*metenoesan*), is not the Septuagint's verb for them there. Swete's text says they "believed" (*enepisteusan*) and "turned" (*apestrepsan*), and uses *metanoeo* only of God (3:9, 3:10). So "repented" in 12:41 is Matthew's summary of Jonah 3, not a quotation of it.

**The Queen of the South (12:42).** The story is in 1 Kings 10:1-13: "when the queen of Sheba heard about the fame of Solomon ... she came to test him with difficult questions" (10:1, BSB). In the shipped BSB, "queen of Sheba" occurs only in 1 Kings 10 and its parallel, 2 Chronicles 9, and "Queen of the South" only at Matthew 12:42 and Luke 11:31. Matthew's title differs from 1 Kings' (Greek *basilissa notou*, "queen of the south," against the Septuagint's *basilissa Saba*), and "from the ends of the earth" is Matthew's phrase. Matthew Henry and MacLaren (via Biblehub) both name her the queen of Sheba.

**"One greater than Jonah ... One greater than Solomon."** Matthew 12 makes three "greater than" comparisons (a script count): the temple (12:6), Jonah (12:41) and Solomon (12:42). In Greek all three are neuter, literally "something more" (*pleion* at 12:41-42; *meizon* at 12:6, where the Textus Receptus has a masculine form). The BSB renders 12:6 "something greater than the temple" but 12:41-42 "One greater than." The temple saying belongs to this curriculum's lesson on Matthew 12:1-21.

**The unclean spirit (12:43-45).** The spirit "passes through arid places seeking rest and does not find it" (12:43), returns, and "finds the house vacant, swept clean, and put in order" (12:44). It brings "seven other spirits more wicked than itself," and "the final plight of that man is worse than the first" (12:45). Luke has the story too (Luke 11:24-26), but there it comes before the sign of Jonah, and Luke has no closing line about "this wicked generation." The Pulpit Commentary notes that "Our Lord's words are apparently quoted in 2 Peter 2:20," where those "overcome by it again" end "worse than it was at first" (BSB).

**The family scene (12:46-50).** Mark 3:31-35 tells the same scene. Mark alone gives the background earlier: "When His family heard about this, they went out to take custody of Him, saying, 'He is out of His mind'" (Mark 3:21, BSB). Why the family came is disputed. Chrysostom (Homily 44 on Matthew) charges them with "superfluous vanity," though he says Jesus spoke "not as being ashamed of His mother." Meyer replies that "there is nothing to justify Chrysostom and Theophylact in charging the mother and the brothers with ostentation," and the Expositor's Greek Testament reads the visit as "solicitude on His account." One textual note: 12:47 ("Someone told Him...") is printed in brackets in the Westcott and Hort and Tischendorf editions on Biblehub, marking it as doubtful there; the BSB includes it.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 12:38-50 is built, not on what the construction is meant to teach.

**Two witnesses from outside Israel.** The two judgment sayings are built alike. Each names a witness ("The men of Nineveh," "The Queen of the South"), says the witness will stand or rise "at the judgment with this generation and condemn it," gives a reason ("for they repented," "for she came"), and ends "and now One greater than ... is here." Both witnesses are Gentiles who responded to an Israelite, a prophet and a king. Ellicott notes that the Tyre, Sidon and Sodom sayings of 11:21-24 spoke of "what might have been," while these speak of "what actually had been."

**"Generation" at both ends of the speech.** "Generation" frames the speech. It opens with "A wicked and adulterous generation" (12:39), appears in both witness sayings (12:41, 12:42), and closes with "this wicked generation" (12:45). In Greek, "wicked" at 12:39 and 12:45 is the same word, *ponera*, and the spirits of 12:45 are *ponerotera*, "more wicked." The Pulpit Commentary: "The twofold use of 'evil' in this verse is not accidental." In Matthew's BSB, "this generation" occurs in five verses: 11:16, 12:41, 12:42, 23:36 and 24:34 (a script count).

## Positions

Three questions in this passage draw named, published disagreement. None is settled here. Each reading is given in its holders' own words where a source was read directly, and marked as secondhand where it was not.

**1. How does "three days and three nights" fit the burial account?** Matthew narrates Jesus' burial "When it was evening" (27:57), says the next day was "the one after Preparation Day" (27:62), and sets the visit to the tomb "After the Sabbath, at dawn on the first day of the week" (28:1, BSB). Read as hours, that is less than three full days and three full nights. Three readings are on record.

*Inclusive reckoning: a part of a day counts as a day.* Most of the older commentators read for this lesson hold this. Meyer: "Jesus was dead only a day and two nights. But, in accordance with the popular method of computation (1 Samuel 30:12 f.; Matthew 27:63), the parts of the first and third day are counted as whole days." Barnes: "It was a maxim, also, among the Jews, in computing time, that a part of a day was to be received as the whole." Ellicott: "any part of a day, though it were but a single hour, was for legal purposes considered as a whole." Bengel calls the phrase a synecdoche, a part for the whole: "this is the idiom of the sacred style, that in indicating continuous time the intervening nights are added." Augustine reads it the same way (as excerpted in the *Catena Aurea*, via Wikipedia, tertiary). Gill cites the Jerusalem Talmud (tractate Pesachim, not read) for the rule that "a part of a day is as the whole." A form of the rule can be read directly in the Jerusalem Talmud, Shabbat 9:3: "Rebbi Eleazar ben Azariah says, day and night each are a term, and part of a term is like the whole" (Guggenheimer translation, via Sefaria). That text is a ruling about counting days for a purity law, discussed alongside Exodus 19:10. It says nothing about burial or about Jesus, and it shows how one rabbi counted in a legal setting, not by itself how Matthew's first readers counted. Barnes and Jamieson-Fausset-Brown point to Esther, where the fast is for "three days, night or day" (4:16) and Esther goes in "On the third day" (5:1, BSB); Meyer and Jamieson-Fausset-Brown also cite Matthew 27:63. Within Matthew, "on the third day" occurs four times (16:21, 17:23, 20:19, 27:64) and "After three days" once (27:63; a script count).

*A literal seventy-two hours, with a Wednesday crucifixion.* E. W. Bullinger, in *The Companion Bible* (Appendix 144), grants that "three days" alone could be idiomatic: "The fact that 'three days' is used by Hebrew idiom for any part of three days and three nights is not disputed." His point is the added nights: "But, when the number of 'nights' is stated as well as the number of 'days', then the expression ceases to be an idiom, and becomes a literal statement of fact." In Appendix 156 he argues that the "high day" of John 19:31 was the first day of Passover, a festival sabbath, and that "This great sabbath, having been mistaken from the earliest times for the weekly sabbath, has led to all the confusion." He concludes that "Wednesday, Nisan 14th (commencing on the Tuesday at sunset), was 'the preparation day', on which the crucifixion took place," with the burial "before our Wednesday sunset." The BSB of John 19:31 reads "the next day was a High Sabbath," a phrase this reading leans on.

*An approximate phrase taken over from Jonah.* The Pulpit Commentary holds that the Friday-to-Sunday account stands "so far as the balance of evidence goes," and so "The reckoning, therefore, here is, strictly speaking, inaccurate. The words are perhaps a mere adaptation of the phrase in Jonah, and are here used only to roughly mark the time of our Lord's stay in the grave." Its next sentence adds that the word "nights" "tends to emphasize the reality of our Lord's stay there." It also holds that "the phrase was spoken by our Lord himself," and that believers kept repeating it "in the form in which the Lord left it."

**Objections each reading faces.** Bullinger's rule about added nights is aimed at the first reading. Against the second stand Mark's gloss on Preparation Day, "the day before the Sabbath" (Mark 15:42, BSB), and Matthew's "on the third day" sayings; Bullinger answers by distinguishing the festival sabbath from the weekly one. Against the third, Barnes holds that the phrase is "strictly in accordance with the Jewish mode of reckoning," and argues that Jesus' opponents, who remembered the prediction (27:63), would otherwise have charged Him with false prophecy. This lesson ranks none of the three, and none disputes that 12:40 points to Jonah 1:17.

**2. How far does "this wicked generation" reach (12:43-45)?** The story ends: "So will it be with this wicked generation" (12:45). Readers differ on whom it describes, and over what span of time.

*A warning about Jesus' own hearers.* Several commentators read the story as a picture of the generation Jesus was addressing, and they differ on its time span. The Expositor's Greek Testament reports the range of answers: "From the lawgiving till the present time (Hil., Jer.); from the exile till now (Chrys., Grotius, etc.); from the Baptist till now (Weiss. etc.)," and it admits that "Christ gives no hint of what period was in His thoughts." The Cambridge Bible reads it as Israel having put away the idolatry of its earlier history, only for worse sins to enter in. Ellicott reads the "last state" as still future when Jesus spoke, and points to the Jewish historian's account of "the final struggle that ended in the destruction of Jerusalem." The Pulpit Commentary: "This is more than a warning; it is a verdict."

*A general pattern for any person.* Other readers, often the same ones, also apply the story to anyone outwardly reformed but not inwardly changed. Matthew Henry first says that "This parable represents the case of the Jewish church and nation," then adds: "It is also applicable to all those who hear the word of God, and are in part reformed, but not truly converted." The Expositor's Greek Testament states the "general truth implied": "moral and religious reform may be, has been, succeeded by deeper degeneracy." Chrysostom (Homily 43), who also holds the first reading, turns it on his own congregation: "All this might be seasonably said, not of them only, but of us also, when after having been enlightened ... we again cleave unto the same wickedness." These readers often pair the passage with 2 Peter 2:20 (see Context).

**A misuse to name plainly.** "This generation" has been stretched from the people Jesus addressed to Jewish people as a whole, in every age. Chrysostom's Homily 43 is a clear example. After applying the story to Jesus' hearers, he carries it to the Jews of his own city and time. He accuses them of siding with the pagans in Julian's time and of practicing sorcery and magic, and he says that only fear of the emperors keeps them quiet. Later commentators on this verse carry the same picture forward in milder words. Barnes writes that "the nation became worse and worse" until God gave "their temple, and capital, and nation into the hands of the Romans." The Expositor's Greek Testament describes later rabbinic Judaism as a subtler form of idolatry. The Catholic Church rejected this kind of reading in general terms in *Nostra Aetate*, the council declaration proclaimed by Pope Paul VI in 1965, which does not discuss Matthew 12. It says that what happened in Christ's passion "cannot be charged against all the Jews, without distinction, then alive, nor against the Jews of today," and that "the Jews should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God, as if this followed from the Holy Scriptures." Several things in Matthew can be checked directly. The askers are "some of the scribes and Pharisees" (12:38), not a whole people. Matthew opens by calling Jesus "the son of David, the son of Abraham" (1:1), and the disciples He calls "My mother and My brothers" (12:49) belong to the same generation as the askers. The witnesses who "condemn" it (12:41-42) are figures from Israel's own Scriptures. Matthew 12 says nothing about Jews of later centuries. Whatever the warning means, this lesson reports only what its named readers have said.

**3. Who were Jesus' "brothers" (12:46-50)?** Matthew names them in the next chapter: "aren't His brothers James, Joseph, Simon, and Judas? Aren't all His sisters with us as well?" (13:55-56, BSB). Mark 6:3 lists "James, Joses, Judas, and Simon" and adds "His sisters" (BSB; the Greek editions spell the second name *Iosetos* or *Iose*). Elsewhere Paul names "James, the Lord's brother" (Galatians 1:19). How they were related to Jesus is tied to the doctrine of Mary's perpetual virginity. Richard Bauckham (*Themelios*, 1996) sets out the three main answers, named "by the names of their fourth-century proponents: Helvidius, Epiphanius and Jerome." (This curriculum's lesson on Matthew 1:18-25 named the related "until" question in 1:25 without developing it.)

*Children of Mary and Joseph born after Jesus (the Helvidian view).* Helvidius's own work does not survive. The New Advent editors, introducing Jerome's reply, summarize him: he "maintained that the mention in the Gospels of the sisters and brethren of our Lord was proof that the Blessed Virgin had subsequent issue." Heinrich Meyer, a German Lutheran theologian: "even if nothing more were said, these words would naturally be understood to refer to the brothers according to the flesh, sons of Joseph and Mary, born after Jesus; but this reference is placed beyond all doubt by the fact that the mother is mentioned at the same time." He reads "until" in Matthew 1:25 and "firstborn" in Luke 2:7 the same way. Albert Barnes, an American Presbyterian minister: "The natural and obvious meaning is, however, that they were the children of Mary his mother. See also Mark 6:3." Bauckham reports this as the view "which probably most modern exegetes, even including some Roman Catholic scholars, hold."

*Children of Joseph by an earlier marriage (the Epiphanian view).* Epiphanius of Salamis, writing in the fourth century (*Panarion* 78.7, Williams translation), says that Joseph was a widower and "took his first wife from the tribe of Judah and she bore him six children in all, four boys and two girls, as the Gospels according to Mark and John have made clear." James, he writes, "is called the Lord's brother not by nature but by grace, because of being brought up with him." The Orthodox Church in America's life of the Apostle James says: "Tradition says that Joseph had other sons with his first wife. Thus, James was called the Lord's brother." Its commemoration of Joseph and James lists the four sons "from his previous marriage: James, Judah, Joses, and Simon (or Symeon)." On Mary herself, an OCA answer to a reader's question says: "We firmly believe that Mary remained a virgin after she gave birth to Christ, while we reject the notion that she had other children after Him." Bauckham calls this "the traditional view in the Eastern Orthodox churches," and notes that it appears in three second-century works (the *Protevangelium of James*, the *Infancy Gospel of Thomas* and the *Gospel of Peter*), which "probably all derive from Syria."

*Cousins (the Hieronymian view).* Jerome, *Against Helvidius* (c. 383, New Advent), argues from the range of the word: "In Holy Scripture there are four kinds of brethren — by nature, race, kindred, love." His examples of kin called brothers include Lot, Abraham's nephew (Genesis 13:8), and Jacob with his uncle Laban (Genesis 29:15). He identifies "James the Less, who is called in Scripture the son of Mary, not however of Mary the mother of our Lord," and points to the women at the cross in John 19:25. He holds that Joseph too remained a virgin, and he rejects the Epiphanian answer as "an invention which some hold with a rashness which springs from audacity not from piety."

*The Catholic Church's teaching.* The *Catechism* teaches "Mary's real and perpetual virginity even in the act of giving birth to the Son of God made man," and says the liturgy celebrates her as "Aeiparthenos, the 'Ever-virgin'" (§499). On the brothers: "The Church has always understood these passages as not referring to other children of the Virgin Mary. In fact James and Joseph, 'brothers of Jesus', are the sons of another Mary, a disciple of Christ, whom St. Matthew significantly calls 'the other Mary'. They are close relations of Jesus, according to an Old Testament expression" (§500). "Jesus is Mary's only son" (§501). The *Catechism* does not specify whether these "close relations" were cousins or half-brothers. Its argument can be checked in the BSB: Matthew 27:56 names "Mary the mother of James and Joseph" among the women at the cross, and 27:61 and 28:1 speak of "the other Mary."

Commentators do not divide neatly by church. Gill, an English Particular Baptist, preferred "Mary's sister's sons." Ellicott, Bishop of Gloucester, judged that the facts "do not enable us to decide," yet ended: "I incline to rest in the belief that the so-called 'brethren' were cousins who, through some unrecorded circumstances, had been so far adopted into the household at Nazareth as to be known by the term of nearer relationship." Bauckham concludes that "The historical evidence is not sufficient for a firm decision between the Helvidian and Epiphanian views."

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the Helvidian view, Jerome sets his four kinds of "brethren." Ellicott adds that "it is scarcely probable that our Lord should have committed His mother to the care of the disciple whom He loved (John 19:26) had she had children of her own." Against the Epiphanian view, Jerome objects that "it is nowhere written that he had another wife." Bauckham allows that "it is possible that reflection on the idea of the virginity of Mary led to the conclusion that Jesus' brothers and sisters could not be her children." He immediately adds the reverse: "it is also possible that the notion of the perpetual virginity arose only because Mary was already known not to have been the mother of Jesus' brothers and sisters." Against the Hieronymian view, Ellicott writes that "There is no adequate evidence that the term was applied to cousins as such." Bauckham writes that "Although the Hieronymian view still has its advocates, it must be said to be the least probable." He points to Hegesippus, a second-century writer, "who calls James and Jude 'brothers of the Lord', calls Simeon the son of Clopas the 'cousin of the Lord', evidently distinguishing the two relationships." Meyer's objection covers both alternatives: they were "wrung from the words even at a very early period ... in consequence of the dogmatic assumption of Mary's perpetual virginity." This lesson does not decide between the readings. The passage's own point does not depend on the answer. On every reading, Jesus names His disciples as His family.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, a model of the shape and not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 12:49-50: "Pointing to His disciples, He said, 'Here are My mother and My brothers. For whoever does the will of My Father in heaven is My brother and sister and mother'" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text records no word against His family. Jesus answers by pointing to His disciples and widens "family" to "whoever does the will of My Father in heaven," adding "sister," which the message did not mention. Meyer quotes Bengel's Latin on this verse, *Non spernit matrem, sed anteponit Patrem* ("He does not spurn His mother, but puts the Father first"; the translation is this lesson's). Inference (this lesson's): disciples who had left homes and trades would have heard a promise of belonging, not a rejection of their families.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Those who do the Father's will belong to Jesus' family, whatever their standing in their own households.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a new belonging among those who do the Father's will. Not carried forward: any command to cut off, avoid or dishonor one's relatives. The passage gives no such command, and the same Gospel has Jesus quote "Honor your father and mother" (15:4; 19:19, BSB).

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a pronouncement about who belongs to Jesus' family, not a command about relatives. Promise scope: it describes a relationship to Jesus; it does not promise that a church will meet every need a family meets, or that family conflicts will end.

**Modern domain and situation.** Family and church. A young man became a Christian in college. His parents, who do not share his faith, are hurt and distant, and holidays have become tense. Some friends at church tell him that 12:50 means his church is now his real family and he should stop trying with his parents.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: receiving belonging in Jesus' family while continuing to honor one's parents. One faithful response: he takes part in his church community, keeps calling and visiting his parents, and asks his pastor for help with the hard conversations.

**Cautions.** Families are estranged for many reasons, and some readers carry that pain: some have been cut off by their families, and others are being pressed by a group to cut off their own. In the readings above, 12:50 widens Jesus' family; Matthew Henry adds, "Not that, under pretence of religion, we may be disrespectful to parents, or unkind to relations." None of the commentators read here treats the verse as a reason for a church or leader to require anyone to leave a family. This curriculum's lesson on Matthew 10:16-42 has a fuller pastoral note on family conflict over faith, with resources for people facing pressure from a partner or a high-control group. If you are in danger at home, contact local emergency services. A pastor you trust, ideally one outside any group involved, or a licensed counselor can help in ways a study note cannot. This bridge is a worked example for the learner to test, not counsel for any particular person.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Matthew explains "the sign of the prophet Jonah" by the three days and nights (12:40). Luke explains it by Jonah being "a sign to the Ninevites" (Luke 11:30). Matthew 16:4 gives no explanation at all. What would you need to know to decide whether these are two ways of stating one sign, or two different signs?
2. Matthew's "repented" (12:41) summarizes Jonah 3, where the Septuagint says the Ninevites "believed" and "turned" and uses "repent" only of God. What does a summary word that is not the source's word tell you about how Matthew reads the Old Testament?
3. On every reading in Positions 3, the people Jesus calls His family in 12:49 are His disciples. Does it matter for that saying how the "brothers" outside were related to Him? If so, how?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 12:38-50 in order. (b) Recite 12:40 in the BSB's wording and give the source verse in Jonah in English and Hebrew numbering. (c) Name the two witnesses of 12:41-42 and where their stories are told.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) the request, the refusal and 16:4; (1:00-2:00) 12:40 and Jonah 1:17 in Greek and Hebrew; (2:00-3:00) Nineveh, the queen and the "greater than" sayings; (3:00-4:00) Positions 1 and 2, including the misuse; (4:00-5:00) the brothers (Positions 3), all three views in their holders' words, ending on an open question.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, one worked candidate: "Twelve Greek words in a row match the Septuagint, so 12:40 should be graded a quotation, not a type." (No formula introduces the words, and they sit inside an "as ... so" comparison.) A second runs the other way: "Matthew never says 'Sheba,' so grading 12:42 explicit oversells it." (How many Old Testament stories have a queen travel to hear Solomon's wisdom?)
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 12:40 does not state the day of the week of the crucifixion; the readings in Positions 1 bring in other texts and counting rules. (b) Matthew 12:43-45 does not say anything about Jews of later centuries; it speaks of "this wicked generation," and the askers are "some of the scribes and Pharisees" (12:38). (c) Matthew 12:46-50 does not say how the "brothers" were related to Jesus, and it does not tell anyone to abandon their family.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words in both texts:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jonah 1:17 | type_antitype | explicit | Matthew states the comparison himself ("as ... so"), and twelve consecutive Greek words match the Septuagint; no citation formula |
| Jonah 3:4-10 | explicit_reference | explicit | Jesus names Nineveh, Jonah and his preaching; "repented" is Matthew's summary, not the Septuagint's verb for the Ninevites |
| 1 Kings 10:1-13 | explicit_reference | explicit | Solomon is named and the Old Testament narrates only one such royal visit; the title "Queen of the South" differs from "queen of Sheba" |
| Luke 11:29-32 | parallel | strong | Near-verbatim sayings in a different order, with Luke's sign "to the Ninevites" instead of the three days |
| Mark 3:31-35 | parallel | strong | The same scene in the same order, with "the will of God" for "the will of My Father in heaven" and Mark's framing at 3:21 |

The Jonah 1:17 row is this lesson's thread connection: the New Testament itself sets Jonah's three days beside the Son of Man's. Choose one row and defend it in your own words, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` instead. One candidate this lesson considered and did not author is Jonah 2:2-6, Jonah's prayer from "the belly of Sheol" (2:2, BSB). Ellicott suggests that the psalm's closeness to Psalm 16:10-11 "may well be thought to have prompted" Jesus' reference. It was declined because Matthew quotes no words of the prayer, and "the heart of the earth" shares only the word "heart" with "the heart of the seas" (2:3). If authored, it would be an `allusion` at `plausible` at most.

## Sources and Limits

- **Read directly, with raw copies saved:** the Biblehub commentary pages on Matthew 12:39-42, 12:45 and 12:46 (public-domain commentaries, not checked against printed editions); Biblehub's parallel Greek editions of Matthew 12:6, 12:39-42, 12:45, 12:47, 12:50 and Mark 6:3; Swete's Septuagint of Jonah 1-3 and 1 Kings 10 on Biblehub (not a critical edition); the Hebrew of Jonah 2:1 on Sefaria; the Jerusalem Talmud, Shabbat 9:3 (Guggenheimer, via Sefaria); Bullinger's *Companion Bible* Appendices 144 and 156; Chrysostom's Homilies 43 and 44 on Matthew and Jerome's *Against Helvidius* (New Advent); *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §§499-501; Epiphanius, *Panarion* 78.7 (Williams translation, archive.org); three Orthodox Church in America pages; Bauckham's 1996 *Themelios* article; *Nostra Aetate*.
- **Secondhand only:** Helvidius (through the New Advent editors and Jerome); Hegesippus (through Bauckham); Bengel's Latin on 12:49 (through Meyer). Gill's Talmud references were not read. No commentator read for this lesson cites Jerusalem Talmud Shabbat 9:3 for Matthew 12:40; it was found and read directly.
- **Tertiary:** Wikipedia's "Matthew 12:40," used only for Augustine and Jerome as excerpted in the *Catena Aurea*; Wikipedia biographies of Barnes, Meyer, Gill and Ellicott, used only for their church affiliations.
- **Orthodox sourcing.** The Orthodox Church in America's lives of the saints and its question-and-answer page are teaching materials published by that church on its website, not synodal or conciliar statements. No Orthodox commentary on Matthew 12:46-50 was read.
- **Chronology sources.** Only Bullinger was read for the Wednesday reading; R. A. Torrey, reported in a search result to hold it, was not read and is not cited. Harold Hoehner's Passion chronology was not located in a readable copy.
- **Anti-Jewish material.** Chrysostom's Homily 43, Barnes and the Expositor's Greek Testament are cited in Positions 2 only as examples of the misuse named there; their derogatory claims are paraphrased, except one short line from Barnes. Ellicott's and the Cambridge Bible's notes on 12:45 also characterize the Judaism of Jesus' day negatively; they are cited only for their reading of the story's time span.
- **Not done:** major critical commentaries (for example Davies and Allison, Luz, or France) and modern studies of "this generation" in Matthew were not consulted. No Jewish commentary on Jonah 1:17 or 3:5-10 was read for this lesson.
- **Every BSB quotation in this lesson and its five connection files was printed from `web/public/bible/BSB/*.json` before drafting and re-checked afterward** by a script asserting each one is a literal substring of the BSB text. Every count or "only" claim was produced by a script over the same files, and the twelve-word Greek overlap by a script over the saved Greek texts. The scripts and their outputs are kept with this lesson's working files.
