Matthew 11:20-30 holds two of the sharpest tones in the Gospel side by side. It opens with Jesus denouncing three Galilean towns that saw most of His miracles and did not repent. It closes with one of the most loved invitations in Scripture: "Come to Me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest" (11:28, BSB). Between them stands a short prayer and a claim about the Father and the Son that Christian theology has leaned on heavily for centuries. The passage is also unusually dense with the Old Testament. Capernaum's fall is told in the shape of Isaiah's taunt against the king of Babylon, and "you will find rest for your souls" is a clause from Jeremiah. The yoke image belongs to a Jewish way of speaking that can still be read today in the Mishnah. This lesson checks each of those echoes at the level of the words, in this app's BSB and, where it matters, in the Hebrew and the Greek Septuagint. It grades each one honestly. It then reports two real disputes without settling them: what the passage says about who receives revelation, and what Jesus' "easy" yoke is being set against. Because 11:28 is so often carried into hard seasons of life, the lesson ends by holding that promise to what it actually says. Every BSB quotation below was printed from the app's own Bible files before drafting and checked again by script afterward.

## Context

**What happens, in order (11:20-30).** "Then Jesus began to denounce the cities in which most of His miracles had been performed, because they did not repent" (11:20, BSB). Two woes follow, on Chorazin and Bethsaida (11:21-22) and then on Capernaum (11:23-24). Each woe compares a Galilean town with a place remembered for judgment: Tyre and Sidon for the first two towns, Sodom for Capernaum. "At that time" (11:25) Jesus turns from the towns to the Father in a short prayer of praise (11:25-26). He then makes a claim about the Father and the Son (11:27) and ends with an invitation to anyone weary (11:28-30). The scene follows directly on Jesus' words about John the Baptist and "this generation" (11:7-19). The section before closes with "But wisdom is vindicated by her actions" (11:19, BSB), a line worth holding onto when the yoke appears below.

**The towns.** In the shipped BSB, Chorazin is named in exactly two verses, Matthew 11:21 and Luke 10:13 (a script over the whole BSB found no other occurrence). In Matthew, Bethsaida also appears only at 11:21. Matthew narrates no miracle located in either town, so the "miracles that were performed in you" (11:21) are known from this verse alone. Capernaum is different. Matthew says Jesus "went and lived in Capernaum" (4:13), calls it "His own town" (9:1), and sets the centurion's request there (8:5). Ellicott's commentary lists the healings Matthew, Mark and John place in or near Capernaum, and reads its being "exalted unto heaven" (KJV wording) as a matter of privilege, "not in any outward prosperity." On location, a tertiary source (Wikipedia, "Chorazin") puts the ancient village on a hill above the north shore of the Sea of Galilee, about 4 km from Capernaum.

**Tyre, Sidon and Sodom as measures.** Tyre and Sidon were Phoenician coastal cities, outside Israel, and the prophets spoke against them: "This is the burden against Tyre" (Isaiah 23:1, BSB); "Behold, O Tyre, I am against you" (Ezekiel 26:3, BSB). Later in Matthew, Jesus withdraws "to the district of Tyre and Sidon" (15:21), where a Canaanite woman begs Him for her daughter. Sodom is named three times in Matthew (10:15, 11:23, 11:24). What Sodom's sin was is a separate question, taken up in this curriculum's Genesis 19 lesson. Here the lesson reports only what Jesus' comparison itself says. It makes a conditional claim about response: "if the miracles that were performed in you had been performed in Sodom, it would have remained to this day" (11:23). And it ranks outcomes at the judgment: "it will be more bearable for Sodom on the day of judgment than for you" (11:24). The comparison measures Capernaum by what it saw and how it responded. It says nothing further about Sodom.

**Capernaum "brought down to Hades," and a reading difference.** "And you, Capernaum, will you be lifted up to heaven? No, you will be brought down to Hades!" (11:23, BSB). The commentators read via Bible Hub differ on how far "Hades" reaches. Ellicott takes it as "Hades, the grave, not Gehenna" and points to the final judgment, as the next verse does. Barnes reads it mainly as the city's coming "desolation." The Expositor's Greek Testament calls the two phrases "proverbial expressions for the greatest exaltation and deepest degradation." The BSB's question ("will you be lifted up...?") follows the Greek text printed in Nestle 1904 and Westcott-Hort. The Byzantine Majority text, which stands behind the KJV's "which art exalted unto heaven," reads a statement instead. The Cambridge Bible notes the question reading of the Sinaitic and Vatican manuscripts, and the Expositor's Greek Testament judges that the difference "does not affect the sense." The Isaiah 14 echo in this verse is graded in the connections below.

**The prayer and the claim (11:25-27).** "I praise You, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because You have hidden these things from the wise and learned, and revealed them to little children" (11:25, BSB). "Yes, Father, for this was well-pleasing in Your sight" (11:26). Then: "All things have been entrusted to Me by My Father. No one knows the Son except the Father, and no one knows the Father except the Son and those to whom the Son chooses to reveal Him" (11:27). Two textual notes come from Meyer's commentary (via Bible Hub). First, "All things have been entrusted" is set beside Matthew's closing scene, "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Me" (28:18, BSB); Ellicott makes the same link. Second, some early witnesses (Meyer lists the Clementine writings, Justin and Marcion) knew the saying with its two halves in the reverse order, Father first. After weighing the witnesses, Meyer concludes that this order "must be excluded from the text." The verse is central to discussion of who Jesus is, and its two-way knowledge of Father and Son reads very close to the Gospel of John. That thread is graded in the connections below. The same clause is also where the dispute reported in Positions begins.

**The invitation (11:28-30).** "Come to Me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest" (11:28, BSB). "Take My yoke upon you and learn from Me; for I am gentle and humble in heart, and you will find rest for your souls" (11:29). "For My yoke is easy and My burden is light" (11:30). These three verses have no parallel in Luke. Luke 10:21-22 matches 11:25-27 closely, then moves on to something else (10:23). Matt Skinner (Working Preacher, 2026) says the same: "We find these appeals and promises only in Matthew's Gospel." A BSB search confirms that "weary and burdened" and "My yoke is easy" occur nowhere else. In Matthew, "yoke" occurs only in 11:29 and 11:30.

**Jeremiah 6:16 and "you will find rest for your souls."** The BSB's "you will find rest for your souls" is word for word the clause in Jeremiah 6:16: "Ask for the ancient paths: 'Where is the good way?' Then walk in it, and you will find rest for your souls. But they said, 'We will not walk in it!'" (BSB). A script over the whole BSB finds that clause in these two verses only. The Hebrew noun in Jeremiah, *margoa*, means "rest." BDB glosses it "rest (poetry)" and lists only this verse. The Septuagint, however, translated it differently, with *hagnismon*, "purification" (Greek text via eBible). Brenton's 1851 English translation of the Septuagint reads "ye shall find purification for your souls." Matthew's Greek has *anapausin*, "rest." So at this point Matthew matches the Hebrew, not the Greek translation most of his readers would have known. The Pulpit Commentary says so directly: the words "are taken from Jeremiah 6:16 (not the LXX.; cf. also Ecclus. 6:28)." Bengel's Gnomen prints the Septuagint's wording beside Matthew's.

**The yoke in Jewish speech.** Several commentators read via Bible Hub point out that "yoke" was everyday Jewish religious language for accepting God's rule and teaching. Gill calls the phrase "Rabbinical" and cites the Mishnah. Vincent's Word Studies quotes Edersheim: "One of the most common figurative expressions of the time was that of the yoke for submission to an occupation or obligation." The Mishnah passage can be read directly. In Mishnah Berakhot 2:2, Rabbi Yehoshua ben Korha explains why the first paragraph of the Shema is recited before the second: "so that one will first accept upon himself the yoke of the kingdom of Heaven... and only then accept upon himself the yoke of the mitzvot" (William Davidson translation via Sefaria). In Pirkei Avot 3:5, Rabbi Nehunia ben Hakkanah teaches: "whoever takes upon himself the yoke of the Torah, they remove from him the yoke of government and the yoke of worldly concerns" (Kulp translation via Sefaria). In both texts, taking the yoke of the kingdom or of the Torah is something chosen and valued, not a weight resented. The Mishnah was compiled around 200 CE, after Matthew was written, so these texts show the idiom's later Jewish form. They do not date it to Jesus' own day. How the Positions below handle this matters.

**Sirach 51 (deuterocanonical).** Commentators have long set 11:28-30 beside the closing poem of Sirach (also called Ecclesiasticus or Ben Sira). Catholic and Orthodox Christians receive this book as Scripture; most Protestants do not. It is not in this app's BSB corpus, so it is quoted here from the public-domain World English Bible: "Draw near to me, all you who are uneducated, and live in the house of instruction" (Sirach 51:23); "Put your neck under the yoke, and let your soul receive instruction. She is near to find" (51:26); "See with your eyes how I labored just a little and found for myself much rest" (51:27). The KJV-with-Apocrypha and Brenton's translation, read alongside, also have "the yoke" at 51:26, not "her yoke." The "she" in 51:26 is wisdom. Ellicott and Meyer both cite Sirach 51:26. The Pulpit Commentary says that 11:29-30 "have so much in common with both the language and the thought of Ecclus. 51:26, 27, that probably this passage was in our Lord's mind." Michael Mulder (*In die Skriflig*, 2026) notes that the shared invitation, the words for "labour" (*ekopiasa*) and "rest" (*anapausis*), and the "salvific yoke" connect the two texts closely. Mulder also quotes Sirach 51:23-27 in an unnamed translation that reads "her yoke"; that wording is not what the three translations read here have. Whether Matthew's readers knew Sirach directly is not something this lesson can establish. The verbal overlap is real and checkable.

**Jesus "gentle and humble in heart" (11:29).** In Matthew, "gentle" describes Jesus here and again at 21:5, where Matthew quotes Zechariah 9:9 ("your King comes to you, gentle and riding on a donkey"). The BSB renders the same Zechariah verse "humble and riding on a donkey" in the Old Testament. Gill links 11:29 to Zechariah 9:9. Matthew gives no citation formula at 11:29, so this lesson notes the shared word and does not grade it as a connection.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 11:20-30 is built, not on what the construction is meant to teach.

**Two woes built on one frame.** Both woes run the same way. Each names the town and then states a contrary-to-fact condition ("if the miracles that were performed in you had been performed in..."), which appears at 11:21 and again at 11:23. Each closes with a near-identical verdict: "But I tell you, it will be more bearable for Tyre and Sidon on the day of judgment than for you" (11:22) and "But I tell you that it will be more bearable for Sodom on the day of judgment than for you" (11:24). The repetition can be checked word for word. Only the town and the measuring city change. In the BSB, "more bearable" occurs five times, all in this saying and its close relatives (Matthew 10:15, 11:22, 11:24; Luke 10:12, 10:14).

**Opposites in pairs.** The passage moves on paired opposites: "lifted up to heaven" and "brought down to Hades" (11:23); "hidden" and "revealed" (11:25); "the wise and learned" and "little children" (11:25); "weary and burdened" and "rest" (11:28); the "easy" yoke and the "light" burden (11:30). These pairings are visible in the English. Whether they were built to answer each other across the passage (for example, the towns' being "lifted up" against Jesus' being "humble in heart") is a reader's inference. No source read for this lesson argues it.

**Three commands and two promises of rest.** The invitation has three imperatives, "Come to Me," "Take My yoke," "learn from Me" (11:28-29), and two promises of rest. In the first, rest is given: "I will give you rest" (11:28). In the second, it is found: "you will find rest for your souls" (11:29). Vincent's Word Studies draws the distinction directly: "The rest of Christ is twofold - given and found." The Pulpit Commentary does the same, noting "the full force of the two expressions." The shift from "give" to "find" is in the text. What each kind of rest consists of is part of what readers dispute.

**Placement.** The prayer opens "At that time" (11:25), tying it to the woes just spoken. Matthew 12 opens with the same phrase, "At that time" (12:1), and goes straight into two disputes about the Sabbath (12:1-14). So the invitation to "rest" is followed at once by controversy over the day of rest. That is a fact about where Matthew places the material. Matthew does not mention the Pharisees anywhere in chapter 11; they appear at 9:34 and next at 12:2. Who exactly hears 11:28-30 is not stated, beyond the crowds Jesus addressed at 11:7.

## Positions

Two real, named disputes attach to this passage. Each is reported here in its participants' own words and is not resolved.

**What does 11:25-27 say about who receives revelation?** The phrases "You have hidden these things... and revealed them" (11:25), "this was well-pleasing in Your sight" (11:26) and "those to whom the Son chooses to reveal Him" (11:27) are part of the long debate over election. Three traditions are reported here, in line with how this curriculum's Matthew 7:13-29 lesson reported them on 7:21-23.

The Reformed tradition reads 11:25-26 as describing God's free choice. John Calvin's commentary on this passage states: "that some arrive at faith, while others remain hardened and obstinate, is accomplished by his free election; for, drawing some, and passing by others, he alone makes a distinction among men, whose condition by nature is alike." The Canons of Dort (1618-1619) cite Matthew 11:25-26 by name as a proof text. In the Rejection of Errors under the first main point, the Synod rejects the view that "it was not on the basis of his just will alone that God decided to leave anyone in the fall of Adam... or to pass anyone by in the imparting of grace necessary for faith and conversion." Calvin then reads 11:27 as an answer to the anxiety this doctrine can raise. People who "begin to inquire anxiously how they may be assured of God's secret purpose" are, he writes, directed elsewhere: "Christ enjoins them to come direct to himself, in order to obtain certainty of salvation."

The Arminian and Wesleyan tradition reads the same verses as describing grace that can be resisted, offered to those who respond. The 1610 Remonstrance says that God determined to save "those who, through the grace of the Holy Ghost, shall believe on this his Son Jesus." Of that grace it says, "it is not irresistible, in as much as it is written concerning many that they have resisted the Holy Ghost" (Articles 1 and 4, quoted via a secondary source reproducing Schaff's text; see Sources and Limits). John Wesley's *Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament* (1755) reads the hiding in 11:25 as permissive: "thou hast suffered these things to be hid from men, who are in other respects wise and prudent." On 11:27 he writes that "none can know it by natural reason: none but those to whom he revealeth it." He takes the next verse as the answer to whom the Son chooses: "Here he shows to whom he is pleased to reveal these things to the weary and heavy laden."

The Catholic Church's Catechism draws on these verses in the three paragraphs read for this lesson, none of them framed as an argument about predestination. It quotes 11:27 to say that "Jesus revealed that God is Father in an unheard-of sense" (§240). It reads 11:25-26 as a prayer of thanksgiving in which Jesus blesses the Father "because he has hidden the mysteries of the Kingdom from those who think themselves learned and has revealed them to infants, the poor of the Beatitudes," with "Yes, Father!" expressing "his adherence to the Father's 'good pleasure'" (§2603). It also says that "the kingdom belongs to the poor and lowly, which means those who have accepted it with humble hearts," and that to these "little ones" the Father "is pleased to reveal what remains hidden from the wise and the learned" (§544).

The three traditions are not three labels for one view. They disagree about why some receive what is revealed. On the Reformed reading, the cause is God's choice alone. On the Arminian and Wesleyan reading, grace is offered to all and can be resisted. The Catechism, in these paragraphs, speaks of the Father's good pleasure and the humble hearts that accept the kingdom, without addressing the question in the terms the other two use. On one point, though, Calvin and Wesley meet in this passage: both send the reader to the invitation in 11:28. Calvin writes that "Christ included all, without exception, who labor and are burdened, that no man may shut the gate against himself by wicked doubts," and adds at once that "all such persons are few in number; for, among the innumerable multitude of those that perish, few are aware that they are perishing." Wesley, as quoted above, reads "the weary and heavy laden" as those to whom the Son reveals the Father. This lesson does not decide between the traditions. A reader troubled about their own standing before God, or about someone they love, needs a pastor who knows them, not a study note.

**What is the "easy" yoke being contrasted with (11:29-30)?** The passage itself names no rival yoke. The words "Pharisee," "scribe," "law" and "command" do not occur in the BSB of 11:20-30. Four readings are reported here, followed by one reading that has been widely criticized as a misuse of the passage.

One reading sets Jesus' yoke against the way some religious leaders of His day taught and applied the law. Ellicott writes: "As the teaching of the Pharisees was a yoke too grievous to be borne, so the yoke of Christ is His teaching, His rule of life." The Cambridge Bible pairs "my burden is light" with Matthew 23:4 ("Contrast with this the burden of the Pharisees"). There Jesus says the scribes and Pharisees "tie up heavy, burdensome loads and lay them on men's shoulders" (23:4, BSB). The Pulpit Commentary reports Ryle and James "suggesting that our Lord was contrasting his yoke with the yoke of minute legal observance laid upon the people by the scribes and Pharisees." Michael Mulder quotes two modern commentaries in the same line: Walter Grundmann ("Jesus places his yoke in contrast to the yoke of rabbinic interpretation of the law") and W. F. Albright and C. S. Mann ("An easy yoke and a light burden are offered in exchange for the arbitrary demands of Pharisaic legalism and the uncertainties of ever-proliferating case law"). Mulder reports this as the most common reading, and its textual anchor is in Matthew itself, at 23:4. In the same chapter, Matthew also has Jesus say, "practice and observe everything they tell you. But do not do what they do" (23:3).

A second reading takes the burden to be sin and a troubled conscience. Calvin says the "labor" of 11:28 "does not mean generally those who are oppressed with grief and vexations, but those who are overwhelmed by their sins." He rejects a narrower reading outright: "They who limit the burden and the labor to ceremonies of the Law, take a very narrow view of Christ's meaning." Wesley reads "heavy laden" as "With the guilt and power of sin" and the promised rest as "rest from the guilt of sin by justification, and from the power of sin by sanctification."

A third reading takes the burdens to be social as well as spiritual. Matt Skinner (Working Preacher, 2026) notes that "Elsewhere in Matthew, Jesus rails against leadership that makes religious devotion oppressive (for example, 23:4)." He reads 11:28-30 alongside the Beatitudes: "read them instead as restatements of the Beatitudes. Jesus is in the business of removing the heavy burdens that our societies and systems inflict on certain people." He adds a limit to his own reading: "He isn't telling people to run from religious devotion, and he certainly is not urging people to abandon Judaism and its high regard for Torah."

A fourth reading denies that the yoke saying sets Jesus against the Torah at all. Mulder argues from Jeremiah 6:16 and Sirach 6 and 51 that the image "echoes a positive use of this image," and that "there is no strong contrast here between Jesus's use of the yoke image and the way his contemporaries thought about the yoke of the Torah." On his reading, the difference "does not lie in contrasting the heavy yoke of the Torah with the light burden of freedom from the law. It rather concerns the question of how one can live such a life." The decisive difference, he writes, "is found in the One who speaks here: Jesus himself, the Messiah of Israel." Mulder frames 11:28-30 as spoken in dialogue with the Pharisees. As Literary Design notes, Matthew 11 does not name them; that framing is Mulder's.

**A misuse to name plainly.** A long line of Christian preaching and commentary has read "My yoke is easy" as Jesus replacing the Torah itself, pictured as a crushing burden Jews labored under. A clear example is Albert Barnes's 19th-century *Notes on the Bible*. On 11:29 he tells readers to compare Jesus' yoke with "the burdensome and expensive ceremonies of the Jews." Calvin, while rejecting the ceremonies-only reading quoted above, still grants in passing that the Law was burdensome. This picture of Judaism has been criticized in scholarship on rabbinic Judaism and on the New Testament. Mulder reports that Solomon Schechter, in *Aspects of Rabbinic Theology*, challenged "the notion that obedience to the Law is burdensome." He quotes *The Jewish Annotated New Testament* (ed. Amy-Jill Levine and Marc Z. Brettler) that this "stereotypically negative charge of Jewish legalism" has "not fully evaporated from Christian critiques of Judaism, whether popular or scholarly" (both secondhand through Mulder). Mulder himself calls the reading "a reductive caricature." Three things can be checked directly against the text and the Jewish sources. First, Matthew 11:20-30 does not mention the Torah, the law, the Pharisees or the scribes. Second, in this same Gospel Jesus says, "Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets" (5:17, BSB). Third, the Jewish texts that speak of a "yoke" (Mishnah Berakhot 2:2, Pirkei Avot 3:5, both above) present it as something taken on gladly and as freeing a person from other yokes. Whichever of the four readings above a learner finds most persuasive, none of them requires this picture. Its critics hold that it misdescribes the Judaism of Jesus' day and of today.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage states in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. This is a model of the shape, not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 11:28-29: "Come to Me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest" (11:28, BSB); "Take My yoke upon you and learn from Me; for I am gentle and humble in heart, and you will find rest for your souls" (11:29, BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the invitation is addressed to "all you who are weary and burdened," and the rest comes in two forms, given ("I will give you rest") and found under a yoke ("you will find rest for your souls"). The rest is not offered as the removal of every yoke. It comes with a yoke and a burden, called "easy" and "light" (11:30). Inference: for people who had just heard Jesus denounce whole towns, the invitation would have sounded like a personal call to come to Him and learn from Him, to take on His teaching as a disciple takes on a teacher's. It would not have sounded like a promise that life's labor was over. This step goes beyond what the text states about its hearers and is labeled as this lesson's own.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Coming to Jesus is pictured as finding rest for the soul while carrying a yoke He describes as easy. It is not pictured as escape from all labor, grief or difficulty.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the invitation is open to "all" who are weary and burdened; the rest is tied to coming to Jesus and learning from Him; and it is called rest "for your souls." Not carried forward: any claim that the text promises the end of fatigue, illness, grief or hard circumstances in this life, or that someone who still feels weary has failed to "come." The text states neither.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a direct invitation with a promise attached ("Come... and I will give you rest"), joined to commands ("Take... learn"). It is not an unconditional statement about every person's present experience. Promise scope: the text promises rest to those who come, and rest "for your souls" under Jesus' yoke. It does not say when that rest is felt, or how fully, and it does not promise that outward burdens will be lifted. Named readers from different traditions draw the same boundary. Calvin writes that the rest "is not at all intended to exempt the disciples of Christ from the warfare of the flesh." The Cambridge Bible glosses "rest unto your souls" as "Not relief from external bodily toil." Colin Yuckman (Working Preacher, 2017) writes that "The promise of rest should not be taken as guaranteed vacation time." What "weary and burdened" covers is itself disputed (sin and conscience, oppressive teaching, social burdens; see Positions). That is another reason not to stretch the promise past its wording.

**Modern domain and situation.** Caregiving and exhaustion. A woman works full time and spends most evenings caring for her father, who has advancing dementia. She has not slept well in months. At a church small group, a friend tells her kindly that if she would "just come to Jesus" with her burden, she would not feel so tired, and that her exhaustion may show she is not trusting Him.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: receiving the invitation as a real call to come to Christ, in prayer, in worship and in learning from Him, without treating continuing tiredness as proof of failure, and without letting the verse replace practical help. One faithful response: she brings her weariness to Christ honestly, in prayer and in her church community. She also tells her pastor and small group what she needs, looks into respite care and support for caregivers, and sees her own doctor about her sleep. Her ongoing tiredness is a real burden to be carried with others, not a verdict on her faith.

**Cautions.** The friend's reading claims more than the text states. 11:28 promises rest to those who come; it does not say that anyone who still feels weary has not come. That reading has hurt many people in hard seasons. The passage also does not ask a person to set aside ordinary care. Rest "for your souls" is not a substitute for sleep, medical treatment, counseling, or help from others. Exhaustion, depression and anxiety are not evidence of weak faith, and a verse is not a treatment plan. If weariness has become hopelessness, or if you are thinking about ending your life, please reach out now: in the United States you can call or text 988 (the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline), and elsewhere you can contact local emergency services. A pastor, a doctor or a licensed counselor can walk with you in ways a study note cannot. This bridge is a worked example for the learner to test, not counsel for any particular person.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. The section just before this one ends, "But wisdom is vindicated by her actions" (11:19). In Sirach 51 it is wisdom who calls the uneducated to "put your neck under the yoke" and promises "much rest." What would you need to check, in Matthew and in Jewish wisdom writing, to judge whether Matthew presents Jesus as speaking in wisdom's voice in 11:28-30, or whether the overlap is shared vocabulary that both draw from a common stock?
2. The BSB's "I will give you rest" occurs at Exodus 33:14, 2 Samuel 7:11 and Matthew 11:28. Colin Yuckman reads 11:28 as recalling Exodus 33, where God's "rest" follows a conversation about knowing ("I know you by name," 33:12; "that I may know You," 33:13). What would distinguish a deliberate echo of Exodus 33 from a common phrase that happens to match in English? Would the Greek (Matthew's *anapauso*, the Septuagint's *katapauso* at Exodus 33:14) strengthen or weaken the case?
3. Ellicott, Cambridge and Grundmann set Jesus' yoke against the Pharisees' teaching, pointing to Matthew 23:4. Mulder argues there is no strong contrast with the yoke of the Torah. Matthew 23:3 tells hearers to "practice and observe everything they tell you." How much weight should 23:3-4, written twelve chapters later, carry for reading 11:28-30, a passage that names no rival yoke?
4. Calvin and Wesley read the "burdened" of 11:28 mainly as burdened by sin; Skinner reads it as including burdens societies and systems impose. Which words in 11:28-30, if any, could decide between them? Or does the text leave "weary and burdened" deliberately open?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 11:20-30 in order: the denunciation, the two woes and their measuring cities, the prayer, the claim of 11:27, and the invitation. Say what the text records before saying what any tradition concludes from it. (b) State the repeated frame of the two woes and the one place where they differ. (c) Recite 11:29's last clause in the BSB's wording, "you will find rest for your souls," and name the one other verse in the BSB where it occurs.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the towns, their measuring cities, and the Isaiah 14 shape of 11:23 (see Context and the connections); (1:00-2:00) the prayer and the claim of 11:27, with its link to 28:18 and to John 10 (see Context and the connections); (2:00-3:00) the election dispute, naming all three traditions in their own words (see Positions); (3:00-4:15) the yoke: Jeremiah 6:16 (Hebrew, not Septuagint), Sirach 51, the Mishnah's "yoke of the kingdom," the four readings of the contrast, and the misuse named in Positions; (4:15-5:00) the scope of 11:28's promise, and one open question stated as the last thing taught.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one worked candidate, stated at full strength: limiting the promise to rest "for your souls," as the Practice Bridge does, spiritualizes away Jesus' real compassion for exhausted bodies and crushed lives. Skinner's reading takes the "heavy burdens that our societies and systems inflict" seriously, and a study note that keeps saying "not outward relief" is quietly teaching that Jesus does not care about them. Before you answer, notice what the Practice Bridge actually limits. It does not say outward burdens fall outside Jesus' concern. It says 11:28-30 does not promise their removal, which is a claim about the wording. A second worked candidate, running the other direction: if 11:27 says the Son reveals the Father to "those to whom the Son chooses to reveal Him," how can 11:28's "all you who are weary" be a real offer to everyone? Before answering, compare how Calvin and Wesley each handle the move from 11:27 to 11:28 (Positions).
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 11:28-30 does not mention the Torah, the law, the Pharisees or the scribes. Any reading that sets Jesus' yoke against the Torah brings that contrast in from elsewhere, and in this Gospel Jesus says He did not come "to abolish the Law or the Prophets" (5:17). (b) Matthew 11:23-24 does not say what Sodom's sin was. It says only that Sodom would have "remained to this day" had it seen these miracles, and that the judgment will be "more bearable" for Sodom than for Capernaum. (c) Matthew 11:27 does not state on what basis the Son "chooses to reveal Him." The traditions in Positions supply that from their wider reading of Scripture, not from this verse alone.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection from this passage and defend it: name its type and its evidence label, and point to the words in both texts that support that grade. The five connections attached to this lesson sit at different kinds of overlap, which is the point of the exercise:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Luke 10:12-15 | parallel | strong | The woes are nearly verbatim, but Luke sets them in the sending of the seventy-two and attaches the Sodom comparison to a rejecting town, not to Capernaum |
| Luke 10:21-22 | parallel | explicit | The prayer and the claim run continuously and almost word for word; Luke adds that Jesus "rejoiced in the Holy Spirit," and has no 11:28-30 |
| Isaiah 14:13-15 | allusion | strong | Same rise-and-fall shape, and in Greek the same "Hades" with the same verb form; named by the Cambridge Bible and the Pulpit Commentary, but no citation formula |
| Jeremiah 6:16 | allusion | strong | An exact BSB clause found nowhere else, matching the Hebrew "rest" against the Septuagint's "purification"; no formula introduces it |
| John 10:14-15 | doctrinal_synthesis | strong | Two independent statements of the same two-way knowledge of Father and Son, linked by Barnes; neither text quotes the other |

Choose one row and defend it in your own words, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` instead. One candidate this lesson considered and did not author is Exodus 33:12-14 ("I will give you rest," 33:14). Yuckman writes that the language of 11:28 "clearly recalls Moses's own vocation (Exodus 33:12-17)." It was declined for three reasons. Only one named source read for this lesson makes the link, in a short sermon-preparation piece. The exact BSB phrase also occurs at 2 Samuel 7:11, so the English match is not unique. And the Greek verbs differ (*anapauso* in Matthew, *katapauso* in the Septuagint of Exodus). Sirach 51:23-27 is a stronger verbal parallel but could not be authored as a connection, because this app's connection graph covers only the 66-book canon. It is reported in Context instead.
