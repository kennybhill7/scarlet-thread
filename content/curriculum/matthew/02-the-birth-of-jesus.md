Matthew 1:1-17 spent seventeen verses establishing that Jesus descends from Abraham and David in an unbroken line ending "Jacob, and Jacob the father of Joseph, the husband of Mary, of whom was born Jesus, who is called Christ" (1:16, BSB). Verse 18 picks up exactly where that sentence left a gap: "of whom was born Jesus" skipped past how, and 1:18-25 is Matthew's answer -- a tightly built scene that is also the first real interpretive crux of the book. This lesson keeps three kinds of claim in three different places. Some things here are cited facts: what first-century Jewish betrothal legally was, and what the Hebrew and Greek words in play actually say. Some are textual observations anyone can verify by reading the passage closely: Matthew's own citation formula at 1:22, the specific Greek verb his quotation uses, the repeated naming language that frames the unit. And some are named positions -- real, live disputes among named scholars over why Joseph's "righteous" response was a "quiet" divorce, and over what Isaiah 7:14's almah/parthenos question does and does not settle. Keeping these apart is the same discipline the genealogy lesson practiced on 1:1-17, applied now to the narrative that genealogy was building toward.

## Context

**What happens, in order (1:18-25).** Mary is "pledged in marriage to Joseph" -- betrothed, not yet living with him -- when she is "found to be with child through the Holy Spirit" before they have come together (1:18, BSB). Joseph, described as "a righteous man" unwilling "to disgrace her publicly," resolves "to divorce her quietly" (1:19). Before he acts on that plan, "an angel of the Lord appeared to him in a dream," addressed him as "Joseph, son of David," told him not to be afraid to take Mary as his wife because the child is "from the Holy Spirit," and instructed him: "you are to give Him the name Jesus, because He will save His people from their sins" (1:20-21). Matthew then states that all of this fulfills what the Lord said through the prophet, and quotes Isaiah 7:14: "Behold, the virgin will be with child and will give birth to a son, and they will call Him Immanuel" (which means, "God with us") (1:22-23). Joseph wakes, does as instructed, and takes Mary as his wife; the chapter closes: "he had no union with her until she gave birth to a Son. And he gave Him the name Jesus" (1:24-25).

**Betrothal as a cited legal fact, not a live dispute.** First-century Jewish betrothal (erusin, later formalized as kiddushin) was a distinct, legally binding stage prior to the home-taking that completed a marriage: once betrothed, a couple was considered married under the law, even though they did not yet live together, and the betrothal could be ended only by the man's death or by a formal divorce. This is why Joseph's situation in 1:18-19 makes sense as the text presents it -- discovering Mary pregnant during betrothal (rather than after the marriage was completed) still gave him grounds to "divorce her," using exactly that word, rather than simply breaking an informal engagement. This lesson treats betrothal's binding legal force as a cited fact about the period, not a named dispute, because the sources behind it do not disagree on this point; the real, named disagreement in this chapter is over what Joseph's specific choice (divorce, done quietly) reveals about him, which the Positions section below takes up.

**"Righteous" (dikaios) and "quietly" -- the words themselves, before any tradition's conclusion about them.** Joseph is called δίκαιος, "righteous" or "just" (1:19) -- the same word family Matthew uses elsewhere for right standing before God and for right conduct toward other people, without this verse itself specifying which sense is primary here. "Quietly" translates λάθρᾳ, "secretly" or "without public notice." Deuteronomy 24:1 required a certificate of divorce, and rabbinic practice (reported, not independently verified against the Mishnah itself in this research pass -- see Sources and Limits) distinguished a divorce brought before a council, with a public accusation, from one certified privately before two witnesses. Which of these "quietly" refers to, and what that choice says about Joseph, is a named dispute -- see Positions, below.

**The angel's address and the naming instruction tie back to the genealogy.** The angel calls Joseph "son of David" (1:20) -- the same title the genealogy spent seventeen verses establishing (1:1, 1:17) -- before instructing him to name the child: "you are to give Him the name Jesus, because He will save His people from their sins" (1:21). In Hebrew, "Jesus" (Yeshua, a shortened form of Yehoshua) is built from the verb "to save" plus a short form of the divine name -- "the LORD saves" is the name's own built-in sense, which is why the angel's statement reads as an explanation of the name rather than an unrelated promise attached to it. That the name carries this sense, and that 1:21 states the connection explicitly ("you are to give Him the name Jesus, because He will save His people from their sins"), is a fact about what this verse itself says; what a reader concludes from it about the scope or mechanism of that saving is a further step this lesson does not take here.

**The fulfillment-citation formula (1:22-23) is Matthew's own words, not this lesson's label.** "All this took place to fulfill what the Lord had said through the prophet" (1:22) is Matthew's own introduction to the Isaiah 7:14 quotation that follows -- a formula he uses roughly ten times across the Gospel to mark a direct citation. That Matthew introduces the quotation this way, and that the quotation is Isaiah 7:14, is a cited fact about the text; what the formula claims about how Isaiah 7:14 relates to this birth, and what Isaiah 7:14 meant in its own eighth-century-BC setting, are exactly the ground the Positions section below reports as disputed.

**A detail usually skipped: "until" (1:25).** "He had no union with her until she gave birth to a Son" uses a Greek construction (ἕως οὗ, "until") whose force in a sentence like this -- whether it implies a change after the point named, or simply states what was true up to that point without commenting on afterward -- is itself debated, and that debate connects to a larger, separate theological question (the perpetual virginity of Mary) that this lesson does not research or take up. It is named here, briefly, as a real question the verse raises, not resolved.

## Literary Design

**A four-times-repeated pattern: the angel-in-a-dream to Joseph.** "An angel of the Lord appeared to him in a dream" (1:20) is not a one-time device. The identical pairing -- an angel of the Lord, appearing to Joseph specifically, in a dream -- recurs at 2:13 ("an angel of the Lord appeared to Joseph in a dream"), 2:19 ("an angel of the Lord appeared in a dream to Joseph in Egypt"), and 2:22 ("having been warned in a dream"). Four times, always to Joseph, always directing what he does next. This is a checkable pattern across Matthew's infancy narrative, not a claim about its significance; what any given reader makes of a Joseph who receives guidance only through dreams, while never speaking a single recorded word in the entire Gospel, is a further inference this lesson leaves to the reader.

**An inclusio: the naming instruction and its fulfillment frame the unit.** The angel's instruction, "you are to give Him the name Jesus" (1:21), and the narrator's closing report, "he gave Him the name Jesus" (1:25), use the same naming language to open and close the passage -- command, then compliance, bracketing everything between them (the righteous-man dilemma, the dream, the Isaiah quotation). This is a textual observation: the same act is named at both ends of the unit, framing the whole as the story of that instruction being carried out.

**A directly checkable Greek detail inside the Isaiah quotation.** Matthew's quotation at 1:23 ends "they will call Him Immanuel" -- the Greek verb is καλέσουσιν, future active indicative, third person plural ("they will call"). The Septuagint's own text of Isaiah 7:14 (the Greek version Matthew's wording elsewhere tracks closely, including its use of παρθένος, "virgin," for the Hebrew almah) reads καλέσεις at this point -- future active indicative, second person singular ("you will call"). This was checked directly against both Greek texts for this lesson (Matthew 1:23 and the Septuagint's Isaiah 7:14) rather than taken from a secondary report. What this difference means -- a deliberate change, a different Greek exemplar Matthew was working from, or something else -- is not resolved here; the fact of the difference is what is directly checkable, and it sits alongside a further, separately reported dispute over the Hebrew verb underlying both (see Sources and Limits).

**A near-verbatim parallel with Luke's own annunciation account.** The angel's instruction to Joseph, "you are to give Him the name Jesus" (Matthew 1:21, BSB), and the angel Gabriel's instruction to Mary in Luke's account, "you are to give Him the name Jesus" (Luke 1:31, BSB), are worded identically in English translation -- directly checkable by reading the two verses side by side. The two scenes differ in almost everything else (who receives the message, Nazareth versus an unnamed location, waking versus a dream, an extended dialogue versus a brief command), which is part of what the connection to Luke 1:26-38 attached to this lesson weighs.

## Positions

**Why did a "righteous" Joseph plan a "quiet" divorce? Three named readings, reported by Raymond E. Brown.** Raymond E. Brown's *The Birth of the Messiah* (1977), as reported by the Wikipedia article on Matthew 1:19 used here, catalogues three basic answers. The first, associated with most ancient commentators, reads Joseph's righteousness as law-abiding: discovering Mary's pregnancy, he was bound by the law to divorce her, and his mercy shows up only in doing it privately rather than publicly. The second, traced to Clement of Alexandria and reported as the view held by most modern Christians, locates Joseph's righteousness in the mercy itself -- choosing not to expose her to public shame is not an exception to his righteousness but its proof. A third, minority reading holds that Joseph already suspected or knew the child's origin was not ordinary, and his planned divorce reflects reverence or caution in the face of something he did not yet understand, rather than suspicion of wrongdoing.

Robert H. Gundry, also reported by that Wikipedia article, reads "quietly" specifically: Gundry argues it means Joseph intended to forgo even the two witnesses a certified private divorce would normally require, making it "an entirely private affair" with no documentation at all -- a stronger reading of "quietly" than the more commonly reported view, also noted there, that Joseph chose the standard private-certification method (two witnesses, no public council) rather than the fully undocumented route Gundry proposes.

**Which kind of divorce, and why that choice itself is read as both just and merciful.** David Instone-Brewer's research into first-century divorce practice (summarized by Dwight Gingrich, drawing on Instone-Brewer's *Divorce and Remarriage in the Bible*) distinguishes an "any-cause" divorce, available without a public trial, from a formal adultery-based divorce, which would have required public accusation but also would have publicly vindicated Joseph's own standing as the wronged party. On Instone-Brewer's reading, reported by Gingrich, Joseph had grounds for the second kind -- the kind that would have cleared his own name -- and chose the first instead, absorbing the appearance of being an inadequate or overly lenient husband so that Mary would not be publicly shamed; Instone-Brewer is further reported as noting it "was considered very suspect when a man refused to divorce his unfaithful wife" at all, which sharpens what Joseph's choice cost him socially. R. T. France, in *The Gospel of Matthew* (NICNT), is reported by the same piece as describing betrothal itself as "a binding contract... which could be terminated only by death... or by divorce as if for a full marriage," with sexual unfaithfulness during betrothal providing grounds for exactly this kind of divorce. Separately, Philip F. Esler's 2022 article in *New Testament Studies*, reading Matthew 1:18-25 against Judean legal papyri from the Dead Sea region and from the Jewish community at Herakleopolis, argues that Joseph's plan to divorce "secretly" reflects a documented first-century concern for protecting a woman's honor in an honor-based society, and that this concern is what Matthew's own word "righteous" is pointing to -- this lesson has read only the article's abstract, not its full argument (see Sources and Limits).

**Was Isaiah 7:14's almah a "virgin," and did Isaiah's own audience hear a messianic promise?** This is a live, current, and genuinely contested question, not a settled one this lesson adjudicates.

One position, reported by the Wikipedia article on Isaiah 7:14 used here (citing Marvin A. Sweeney and Craig A. Evans) and by the Wikipedia article on almah, reads the Hebrew word in its own eighth-century-BC setting as simply "a young woman of childbearing age," without a specific claim to virginity, and reads the sign itself as addressed to King Ahaz about the Syro-Ephraimite crisis he faced -- Evans is quoted there as stating plainly that "Isaiah 7:14 in its historical context is not about the birth of an awaited messiah." On this reading, the almah is someone known to Isaiah's own audience, and the sign's force was that the political threat would pass before the child was old enough "to reject evil and choose good" (7:15-16, BSB) -- a near-term timetable, not a distant one.

A second position defends "virgin" as a legitimate, not merely a later Christian, reading of almah. Alec Motyer, quoted in an article by Daniel Hoffman (Knowing Scripture) used here, states: "There is no ground for the common assertion that had Isaiah intended virgo intacta he would have used betula. Alma lies closer to this meaning than the other word." The same article reports R. T. France as acknowledging that the context of Isaiah 7 "suggests something other than a normal childbirth within marriage" -- part of why the Septuagint's own translators, centuries before Matthew and with no stake in a later Christian reading, rendered almah with parthenos, the ordinary Greek word for a virgin.

A third position argues the question of audience and timing should not be collapsed into "either Ahaz's own time or a distant Messiah." Seth D. Postell's 2025 article in the *Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society*, titled "Is Isaiah 7:14 Messianic?," argues against treating a messianic reading of the verse as a later imposition external to Isaiah's own text -- this lesson was not able to read the article's full argument in a reliably extractable form during this research pass (see Sources and Limits) and reports only its thesis and bibliographic identity, not its supporting argument in the author's own words.

These three positions disagree on more than translation: they disagree on what Isaiah's own first audience could have understood the sign to mean, which is a different question from what Matthew does with the verse in 1:22-23. This lesson reports all three without ranking them.

## Practice Bridge Example

This is one worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage states in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key: the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step, and a different passage element, situation, or response would be equally legitimate.

**Passage element.** Matthew 1:19-20. Before the dream, Joseph's settled plan is a quiet divorce -- a response built on the information he has. After the dream gives him new, decisive information, he does not adjust that plan; he abandons it entirely and takes Mary as his wife.

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text gives no indication Joseph consulted anyone else or sought a public resolution before the dream -- his plan was private, and it was also provisional, in the sense that nothing in the text suggests he treated the matter as closed before he acted on it. Inference: for a first-century reader, a "righteous" man's quiet divorce plan, held but not yet executed, models a way of responding to something that looks damning without yet being certain -- protecting the other person from public harm while the situation is still unresolved, rather than either ignoring the problem or escalating it immediately. The Context section above supplies what the text itself says about Joseph's plan and his character description; this inference goes one step further, into why a quiet-but-real plan might be the reasonable middle position for a reader who does not yet know what Joseph does not yet know.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When facing a situation that looks damning but is not yet fully resolved, there is a difference between a response that protects the other party from unnecessary public harm while remaining genuinely open to new, decisive information, and a response that either covers the matter up entirely or escalates it publicly before the facts are settled.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: a measured, provisional response to an unresolved, damaging-looking situation, held open to being completely revised by new information rather than merely adjusted at the edges. Not carried forward: the specific content of Joseph's situation, the angel, the dream as a mechanism of revelation, or any claim that new information will always arrive to resolve a hard case. The bridge holds only where a real, unresolved situation and a real possibility of new information are both present -- not wherever someone simply wants to avoid confrontation.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: principle carried by analogy from how Joseph is shown responding, not a command addressed to the reader. Promise scope: none claimed; nothing in 1:19-20 promises a modern reader that decisive new information will arrive to resolve their own hard case.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. A small-business owner discovers a bookkeeping discrepancy that implicates a long-trusted employee, but the records are ambiguous enough that several explanations remain possible, and a full audit will take time.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: a provisional, dignity-protecting response, not avoidance and not public accusation. One faithful response: the owner quietly adjusts oversight and access in the interim, says nothing to other staff, and waits for the audit's findings before deciding how to act -- neither pretending nothing is wrong nor naming the employee to others before the facts are settled.

**Cautions.** This is not a model for situations involving real danger, ongoing harm, or a legal duty to report -- a quiet, provisional response is not the faithful one when someone is being actively hurt, or when law or policy requires immediate disclosure; a qualified authority (HR, legal counsel, law enforcement) is the right next step in those cases, not a wait-and-see posture modeled on this passage. It is also not license to permanently avoid a hard conversation once the facts are known; the point of the bridge is the provisional, information-gathering posture before resolution, not indefinite quiet. The passage is not a workplace-investigations manual; the bridge is an analogy the learner is testing, and a different reader may find it does not hold for their case.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Brown's three readings of Joseph's "righteousness" (law-abiding-but-merciful; mercy itself as the righteousness; prior knowledge of the child's origin) each rest on how a reader fills in what the text does not say about what Joseph believed before the dream. What, if anything, in 1:18-19's own wording bears on which of these is more likely, apart from each tradition's larger theological commitments?
2. Matthew's quotation at 1:23 reads "they will call Him Immanuel" (καλέσουσιν, third person plural), while the Septuagint's own text of Isaiah 7:14 reads "you will call" (καλέσεις, second person singular) -- a difference this lesson checked directly against both Greek texts. What would you need to see in the underlying manuscript tradition to judge between "Matthew deliberately changed the verb" and "Matthew's source already read this way"?
3. The almah/parthenos dispute is partly a translation question and partly a question about what Isaiah's own first audience could have understood. If you could somehow settle definitively that almah meant only "young woman" with no virginity implication in ordinary eighth-century-BC usage, would that settle the dispute over what Matthew does with the verse in 1:22-23, or are these two separate questions that only look like one?
4. The angel tells Joseph the child will "save His people from their sins" (1:21) -- a statement the text makes about what the name itself means and does. What would count as evidence, from within Matthew's own Gospel read forward from this point, that this statement is being developed rather than simply repeated?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate what happens in 1:18-25 in order -- Mary's pregnancy discovered during betrothal, Joseph's plan, the dream, the Isaiah quotation, Joseph's action -- before saying what any of it means. (b) State, in the text's own words, what the angel says the child's name should be and why (1:21). (c) Name one place elsewhere in Scripture where this passage's language or structure visibly reappears, and say what kind of connection it is.

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) betrothal as a cited legal fact and the plain sequence of 1:18-25 (see Context); (1:00-2:00) the repeated angel-in-a-dream pattern and the naming inclusio that frames the unit (see Literary Design); (2:00-3:00) the directly checkable Greek verb difference between Matthew's quotation and the Septuagint's own text of Isaiah 7:14 (see Literary Design); (3:00-4:00) the three named readings of Joseph's "righteous" quiet divorce, each attributed to its own source (see Positions); (4:00-5:00) the almah/parthenos dispute, with its three named positions kept separate from the question of what Matthew does with the verse (see Positions).

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one worked candidate, stated at full strength: Matthew's citation of Isaiah 7:14 changes the verb from the Septuagint's own second-person singular ("you will call") to a third-person plural ("they will call") not reported by this lesson's sources as deliberate manuscript variation -- which, together with the Septuagint's own translation choice of parthenos for an almah that most modern lexicographers read as having no inherent virginity component, suggests Matthew is adapting the text to fit a theological conclusion he had already reached, rather than reading Isaiah 7:14 on its own terms. Before you answer it, state what the Wikipedia-reported Sweeney/Evans position (Positions, above) and the Motyer/France-defended "virgin" reading (Positions, above) each actually claim about almah's own semantic range, and notice that this objection assumes the first is correct without engaging the second. A second worked candidate, running the other direction: if Isaiah 7:14 is purely about an eighth-century sign to Ahaz with a near-term timetable (7:15-16), what explains the Septuagint translators -- working centuries before Matthew, with no stake in a Christian reading -- choosing parthenos rather than a more neutral Greek word for "young woman," a choice this lesson's sources report as feeding directly into Matthew's own wording?

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 1:18-25 does not state what, if anything, Joseph believed about the child's origin before the dream in 1:20 -- the text records his plan (1:19) and then the dream that follows it (1:20), with nothing in between describing his state of mind; every claim in the Positions section above about what Joseph suspected, knew, or believed before the dream is a scholar's or tradition's own inference from the gap, not this passage's own statement. (b) This passage does not, by itself, settle what Isaiah 7:14 meant to its own eighth-century-BC audience; it states that Jesus's birth fulfills what Isaiah said (1:22-23), which is a claim about Matthew's own reading of Isaiah, not a claim this passage makes about what Isaiah's first hearers understood at the time he spoke.

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection from this passage and defend it: name its type, its evidence label, and point to the words in both texts that support that grade. The three connections attached to this lesson sit at different tiers, which is the point of the exercise:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |

| Isaiah 7:14 | quotation | explicit | Matthew introduces the verse with his own fulfillment-citation formula (1:22) and quotes it directly; the label rests on Matthew's own formula, not on how the two texts otherwise compare |
| Luke 1:26-38 | parallel | strong | Same event (the angelic announcement of a virginal conception) told from the other participant's side, with one instruction worded identically in English translation (Matt 1:21/Luke 1:31); real differences in setting and content keep this below "explicit" |
| Luke 2:21 | motif | plausible | Both Gospels report the name "Jesus" given in obedience to an angelic command, each within its own independent narrative; this lesson's own textual observation, not a named scholar's specific argument for this verse pairing |

Choose one row and defend it in your own words, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet` instead: name a candidate connection you were drawn to (the "son of David" address at 1:20 tying back to the genealogy, or the Holy Spirit's role at 1:18 and 1:20 compared to its role elsewhere in Matthew) and say what evidence is missing before it could be graded.
