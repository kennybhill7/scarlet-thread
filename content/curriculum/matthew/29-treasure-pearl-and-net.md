Matthew 13:44-58 closes the parable chapter and then leaves it. Indoors, with the crowds dismissed (13:36), Jesus tells three short parables: a treasure hidden in a field, a merchant's pearl, and a net full of fish. He asks His disciples, "Have you understood all these things?" (13:51, BSB), and compares "every scribe who has been discipled in the kingdom of heaven" to a homeowner bringing out "new treasures as well as old" (13:52). Then He goes to His hometown, where the people who know His family "took offense at Him" (13:57). This lesson asks first who, in each picture, stands for whom. Named readers divide on whether the man who sells everything is the disciple or Christ, and on what the scribe's "new" and "old" are. The Nazareth scene differs from Mark's in two small details that readers explain in different ways. Those disputes are reported, not settled, and the connections are graded, including one suggested Old Testament root for the prophet saying. Every BSB quotation was checked by script against this app's own Bible files.

## Context

**What happens, in order (13:44-58).** A man finds treasure in a field and, "in his joy," sells all he has to buy the field (13:44, BSB). A merchant finds "one very precious pearl" and does the same (13:45-46). A net catches "all kinds of fish," which are sorted, and Jesus explains it of "the end of the age" (13:47-50). The disciples say they have understood, and Jesus speaks of the scribe (13:51-52). In His hometown synagogue the hearers are astonished, name His family, and take offense; Jesus answers with the prophet saying, and does few miracles there (13:53-58).

**Spoken to the disciples.** Wesley: these three parables are "proposed, not to the multitude, but peculiarly to the apostles." None of them, nor the scribe saying, has a parallel in the other New Testament Gospels; the Expositor's Greek Testament calls 13:52 a saying "preserved by Matthew only."

**The treasure and the pearl (13:44-46).** Ellicott explains the hidden hoard from the times: "Every village had its story of men who had become suddenly rich by finding some hidden hoard that had been hastily concealed in time of war or tumult." Barnes calls the finder's conduct "dishonest" but adds that "Christ does not intend to vindicate his conduct." The Pulpit Commentary notes a difference in tense: in 13:44 the verbs for going, selling and buying are "All in the present tense," while 13:46 uses past tenses. In the BSB, "sold all he had" occurs only at 13:44 and 13:46 (a script search). Readers also set the treasure beside Jesus' words to the rich young man, "go, sell your possessions and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven" (19:21). The Pulpit Commentary bids readers "contrast the young man's grief with the joy spoken of here." How that comparison is used is part of Position 1.

**The net (13:47-50).** The explanation of the net repeats the explanation of the weeds almost word for word. Both begin "so will it be at the end of the age" (13:40, 13:49). Both end "throw them into the fiery furnace, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth" (13:42, 13:50). In Greek, 13:42 and 13:50 are identical in all eight editions Biblehub lists (a script comparison). In the BSB, "weeping and gnashing of teeth" occurs six times in Matthew (8:12, 13:42, 13:50, 22:13, 24:51, 25:30) and once in Luke (13:28). Among the Gospels, "end of the age" occurs only in Matthew (13:39, 13:40, 13:49, 24:3, 28:20), and "fiery furnace" occurs in the New Testament only at 13:42 and 13:50 (all script searches). The weeds and their explanation belong to this curriculum's lesson on Matthew 13:24-43. What the "fiery furnace" says about final judgment, including the dispute over whether it ends in destruction or in punishment without end, is reported in the Positions of the lesson on Matthew 10:16-42 (lesson 21) and is not argued again here.

**"Have you understood all these things?" (13:51-52).** The disciples answer "Yes." Then: "For this reason, every scribe who has been discipled in the kingdom of heaven is like a homeowner who brings out of his storeroom new treasures as well as old" (13:52). Three details can be checked. First, "discipled" translates a verb, *matheteuo*, that occurs four times in the New Testament: here, of Joseph of Arimathea, "who himself was a disciple of Jesus" (27:57), in "go and make disciples of all nations" (28:19), and once in Acts (14:21) (Biblehub's concordance, which follows one edition). Second, "storeroom" renders *thesaurou*, the same noun as "treasure" in 13:44, in all eight editions. The Greek ends simply *kaina kai palaia*, "new things and old" (this lesson's translation); the word "treasures" in the BSB's "new treasures" is supplied. Third, the editions split on one phrase: Nestle, Westcott-Hort and Tischendorf read *te basileia*, "to the kingdom" (dative), while the Byzantine, Greek Orthodox and Textus Receptus texts read *eis ten basileian*, "into the kingdom" (this lesson's renderings). In the BSB of Matthew, the singular "scribe" occurs only at 13:52 (a script search).

**The end of a discourse (13:53).** "When Jesus had finished these parables" closes the third of Matthew's long speeches. The same formula, "When Jesus had finished," closes the others in the BSB (7:28, 11:1, 19:1, 26:1). The notes to the New American Bible, Revised Edition (NABRE) observe a contrast. After the Sermon on the Mount, "the crowds are in admiring astonishment at Jesus' teaching (Mt 7:28); here the astonishment is of those who take offense at him." Matthew does not name the town. He calls it "His hometown" (Greek *patris*), and Chrysostom (Homily 48) writes, "As it seems to me, Nazareth."

**The family names (13:55-56).** "Isn't His mother's name Mary, and aren't His brothers James, Joseph, Simon, and Judas? Aren't all His sisters with us as well?" (BSB). Mark 6:3 has "James, Joses, Judas, and Simon." In Matthew the Greek editions split on the second name: Nestle, Westcott-Hort, Tischendorf and the Greek Orthodox text read *Ioseph*, while the Byzantine and Textus Receptus texts read *Ioses*. How these brothers and sisters were related to Jesus is reported in full in Position 3 of this curriculum's lesson on Matthew 12:38-50 (lesson 26). There the Helvidian, Epiphanian and Hieronymian readings, and the Catholic and Orthodox teaching, are each given in their own words, and the question is left open. The commentators on this verse divide the same way. Barnes: "The fair interpretation of this passage is, that these were the sons and daughters of Joseph and Mary." The Pulpit Commentary: "Probably sons of Joseph by a former wife." Wesley: "They were the sons of Mary, sister to the virgin, and wife of Cleophas or Alpheus." This lesson does not decide between them.

**"The carpenter's son" against "the carpenter" (13:55; Mark 6:3).** Matthew has "Isn't this the carpenter's son?" Mark has "Isn't this the carpenter, the son of Mary" (6:3, BSB). All eight Greek editions Biblehub lists read *ho tekton*, "the carpenter," at Mark 6:3. The noun *tekton* occurs only in these two verses of the New Testament (Biblehub's concordance). The NABRE notes that "no other gospel calls Jesus a carpenter," and that at Mark 6:3 "Some witnesses have 'the carpenter's son,' as in Mt 13:55." Origen, answering the pagan critic Celsus, wrote that "in none of the Gospels current in the Churches is Jesus Himself ever described as being a carpenter" (*Against Celsus* 6.36). Named readers explain the difference in two ways, and the copies of Mark add a third consideration.

- *Both were said.* Augustine (*Harmony of the Gospels* 2.42) says Jesus "might quite fairly" have been called by both names: "For in taking Him to be the son of a carpenter, they naturally also took Him to be a carpenter." Barnes: "Both these expressions would probably be used in the course of the conversation, and Matthew has recorded one and Mark the other."
- *Matthew avoided calling Jesus a carpenter.* The Expositor's Greek Testament: "Mk. has ὁ τέκτων, which our evangelist avoids." The Pulpit Commentary allows that Mark's form "may possibly be a doctrinal correction, made to avoid representing our Lord as the son of Joseph." It then judges Mark's form "more probably the earlier form of the narrative," which Matthew, "or one of those who transmitted the source he used, avoided out of a feeling of reverence."
- *The copies of Mark.* The NABRE's note on "some witnesses" and Origen's remark suggest that some early copies of Mark did not call Jesus "the carpenter." Neither source says which reading of Mark is original, and every edition consulted here prints "the carpenter."

This lesson does not choose among these explanations.

**Mark 6:1-6 and Luke 4:16-30.** Mark tells the same visit in the same order. Luke 4:16-30 has Jesus in "Nazareth, where He had been brought up" (4:16), hearers who ask, "Isn't this the son of Joseph?" (4:22), and the saying "no prophet is accepted in his hometown" (4:24). Luke also has much that Matthew lacks: the reading from Isaiah, the examples of Elijah and Elisha, and the attempt "to throw Him over the cliff" (4:29). Readers disagree whether it is the same visit. Augustine holds it is: Luke tells it early, "introducing by anticipation what really happened only after the occurrence of a number of intervening circumstances." Calvin: "A narrative which Luke gives (4:22) is nearly similar, but is not the same." Wesley reads Matthew's visit as a return to Nazareth, "with no better success than he had had there before." Because the identification is disputed, the Luke connection is graded plausible, not strong.

**"A prophet without honor" (13:57).** The BSB reads, "Only in his hometown and in his own household is a prophet without honor." Mark adds "among his relatives" (6:4). Luke has "no prophet is accepted in his hometown" (4:24). John reports the saying in a different setting: "Now He Himself had testified that a prophet has no honor in his own hometown" (John 4:44). In the BSB's Gospels, the saying occurs in these four verses (a script search). The Expositor's Greek Testament calls it a proverb "not Jewish merely, but common property of mankind," with parallels in Greek and Roman authors. The Pulpit Commentary suggests a root in Israel's own Scriptures: "Possibly Jeremiah's experience (Jeremiah 11:21; Jeremiah 12:6) gave rise to this proverb." That suggestion is graded in Teach-Back 5.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 13:44-58 is built, not on what the construction is meant to teach.

**Six "kingdom of heaven is like" parables, then a seventh likeness.** In the BSB of Matthew 13, six parables open with "The kingdom of heaven is like" (13:24, 13:31, 13:33, 13:44, 13:45, 13:47). The chapter's teaching ends with a different likeness: the scribe "is like a homeowner" (13:52). Gaebelein counts the seven parables as one series, and so does Scofield.

**Two pairs.** Chrysostom (Homily 47) pairs the treasure and the pearl as he paired the mustard seed and the leaven: "these declare its value, and great price." Gibbs (see Position 1) lists how unlike the two are: the treasure has hiddenness, joy and present tenses, while the pearl has seeking and past tenses. He argues that what they share, "sells all that he has and buys," is therefore the center of both.

**"Treasure" at both ends.** The noun *thesauros* occurs at 13:44 (the treasure) and 13:52 (the storeroom). This lesson notes the repetition but draws no structural conclusion from it, and no source read here does either.

## Positions

Three questions in this passage draw named, published disagreement. None is settled here.

**1. Who buys the treasure and the pearl (13:44-46)?** Most readers, as Gaebelein and Gibbs both acknowledge, take the man and the merchant to be anyone who finds the kingdom and gives up everything for it. A minority take the buyer to be Christ.

*The disciple who gives everything for the kingdom.* Chrysostom (Homily 47): both parables signify "that we ought to value the gospel above all things." We are to give up everything else "with joy," he adds, knowing "that the transaction is gain, and not loss." Calvin: "The first two of these parables are intended to instruct believers to prefer the Kingdom of heaven to the whole world." He qualifies the word "buy" at once: "By the word buy Christ does not mean, that men bring any price, with which they may purchase for themselves the heavenly life." Rather, "we are said to buy it, when we cheerfully relinquish the desires of the flesh." Trench reads two kinds of finder: those "seeking everywhere and inquiring for this good," like the merchant, and those like "the finder of the hid treasure, who stumbled upon it unawares." The *Catechism of the Catholic Church*, speaking of the parables in general, says Jesus "also asks for a radical choice: to gain the kingdom, one must give everything" (§546). In Gaebelein's report, Luther read the treasure as "the Gospel, which gives us grace and righteousness without our merit" (secondhand).

*Christ, who gives everything to buy His people.* C. I. Scofield's *Reference Bible* (1917): "Our Lord is the buyer at the awful cost of His blood," and the treasure is Israel, "His hidden Israel, yet to be restored and blessed." He cites Exodus 19:5 ("you will be My treasured possession," BSB) and Psalm 135:4. In his reading the pearl is "The true Church": "Christ, having given Himself for the pearl, is now preparing it for presentation to Himself." Arno Gaebelein argues the same: "the man who bought the field and the treasure in it, and the merchantman, who sold all to obtain one pearl of great value, is the Lord Himself." Jeffrey Gibbs, a Lutheran writer, argues for Christ as buyer from a different starting point (*Concordia Theological Quarterly*, 1987). He holds that the treasure is the disciples, not Israel as a nation. He draws on the Old Testament picture of God's people as His "treasured possession," which, he writes, the New Testament applies to the church: "It is the disciples of Jesus, and not Israel, who are now God's 'special, treasured possession.'" On 13:44 he writes: "Jesus portrays himself as a man who rejoices at finding a treasure hidden in a field." He concludes: "They are his treasure. He has purchased the entire field in order to possess them." Trench reports two earlier holders: Salmeron ("The man who found the treasure, that is, the precious Church of the elect, is Christ") and Drexelius ("Who is a truer merchant than Christ the Lord, Who gave for that precious merchandize the infinite price of his blood?"). Both are quoted only as Trench translates them.

Holders do not divide neatly by tradition: Luther, as Gaebelein reports him, reads the treasure as the gospel, while the Lutheran Gibbs reads the buyer as Christ. The Christ readings also differ from one another: for Scofield and Gaebelein the treasure is Israel and the pearl the church, and for Gibbs both are Jesus' disciples.

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the first reading, Scofield holds that it "has no warrant in the parable itself": "The field is defined (v. 38) to be the world," and "the sinner has nothing to sell, nor is Christ for sale." Gaebelein argues that the gospel "does not ask of the sinner to sell all he has to receive the grace of God." Gibbs points to the rich young man (19:21-26), whom Jesus tells to sell everything and who cannot; that passage, he argues, "is about the inability of men to do what Jesus requires in general." He also points to 18:25, where selling "all that he had" is the master's sentence on the debtor. Against the second reading, the Pulpit Commentary, without naming it, holds that "field" in 13:44 does not signify what it does in the parable of the weeds: "though the figure is the same as in ver. 24, the thing signified is very different. Here field represents merely that which contains the treasure." Calvin and MacLaren read "buy" as giving up, not paying a price. MacLaren: "We buy it when we acknowledge that we have nothing of our own to buy it with." Trench calls the Christ reading "an interpretation which strangely reverses the whole matter." Gibbs himself grants the weight on the other side: "It is no small thing to contradict such a monolith of exegetical tradition." This lesson does not choose between the readings.

**2. Who is the scribe, and what are the "new" and the "old" (13:52)?**

*The Old and New Testaments, or the law and the gospel.* Chrysostom (Homily 47): "Do you see how so far from excluding the Old Testament, He even commends it, and speaks publicly in favor of it, calling it a treasure?" Meyer reports the same view in "Irenaeus, Origen, Chrysostom, and Jerome" (secondhand except for Chrysostom). Bengel: "by παλαιά (old), therefore, are meant things known from Moses and the prophets," and the new are "things then first revealed." The NABRE note: the scribe "knows both the teaching of Jesus (the new) and the law and prophets (the old) and provides in his own teaching both the new and the old as interpreted and fulfilled by the new."

*A full store, not one pair.* Calvin: "Many of the ancient expositors understand by things new and old the Law and the Gospel; but this appears to me to be forced. I understand them simply to mean a varied and manifold distribution, wisely and properly adapted to the capacity of every individual." Meyer: the words are "on no account to be restricted to any one thing in particular," and he calls the Testament reading "a dogmatic limitation." The Pulpit Commentary calls Irenaeus' interpretation "only partially right," because the new truths the disciples learned "would, after a few weeks or months, in their turn become old to them."

*Who is the scribe?* Calvin and the Pulpit Commentary take him to be the disciples as future teachers. The NABRE limits the saying to "the Twelve and these their later counterparts," the church's "scribes" of 23:34. A further reading hears in the verse a portrait of the Gospel writer himself. Wikipedia reports (tertiary) that a Jerusalem Bible footnote "suggests that this reference may portray the evangelist himself." In a recent study, as Mark Strauss's review reports it, Patrick Schreiner argues that Matthew is a "discipled scribe," who "expounds the wisdom of Jesus by becoming a scribe and teacher to future generations, mediating the instruction of his sage." No source read for this lesson argues against that reading, and none of the older commentators raises it. Neither Schreiner's book nor the Jerusalem Bible was read directly.

**3. "Did not" or "could not" (13:58; Mark 6:5)?** Matthew: "And He did not do many miracles there, because of their unbelief." Mark: "So He could not perform any miracles there, except to lay His hands on a few of the sick and heal them" (6:5, BSB). Mark's Greek is *ouk edynato*, "was not able." Named readers explain the difference in three ways, which overlap.

*He would not: the hindrance was moral, not a lack of power.* Chrysostom (Homily 48) says Jesus held back "so as not to inflame them unto more envy, nor to condemn them more grievously, by the aggravation of their unbelief," because "He looked not to the display of Himself, but to their profit." Gill, on Mark 6:5, calls "cannot" for "will not" "an usual way of speaking with the Hebrews." He cites the angel at Sodom, "I cannot do anything until you reach it" (Genesis 19:22, BSB). The Geneva Bible's note on Mark 6:5: "That is, he would not: for we need to have faith if we are going to receive the works of God." Calvin: unbelievers "bind up the hands of God by their obstinacy; not that God is overcome, as if he were an inferior, but because they do not permit him to display his power."

*Faith was a real condition of the healings.* Ellicott: "The wonder-working power was not absolute and unconditioned, but depended on the faith of those who came to Him. Without that, the will and the power were alike thwarted." The Cambridge Bible, on Mark: "His miraculous power was not magical. It was an influence which required and presupposed faith." MacLaren: "Startling as the expression is, it is not to be weakened down because it is startling." The NABRE note on Mark 6:5: "according to Mark, Jesus' power could not take effect because of a person's lack of faith."

*Matthew's wording as an editor's choice.* The Expositor's Greek Testament says that "editorial discretion is at work": "Matthew changes this into a statement that He did few miracles there because of their unbelief, and passes over the marvelling in silence." The NABRE note on Matthew lists this among the ways "Matthew modifies his Marcan source." The Pulpit Commentary: "Our account is abbreviated from Mark's." This reading assumes that Mark wrote first. Augustine held the opposite order: Mark "follows him closely, and looks like his attendant and epitomizer" (*Harmony of the Gospels* 1.2).

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the first stands MacLaren's warning, quoted above, against weakening Mark's verb. Against the second, Barnes writes, "We are not to suppose that his power was limited by the belief or unbelief of people," and Gill says it was "not for want of power, or as if their unbelief was too mighty for him to overcome." Against the third, Augustine's order of the Gospels runs the other way. Every reading agrees on what both Gospels say: some miracles were done (Mark says He healed "a few of the sick"), and the reason given is "their unbelief." This lesson does not decide between the readings.

**A reading to handle with care.** Some readers have widened the Nazareth scene from one town to the Jewish people as a whole. Calvin writes, "I readily agree, however, with Chrysostom, who thinks that this proverb was applied in a peculiar manner to the Jews." His next sentence narrows it again: "But what was usually spoken against the whole nation, Christ now asserts with special reference to his Galilean countrymen." In his comment on 13:54-58 Gaebelein writes, "And thus He is rejected still by His earthly people." The same sentence continues: "and alas! many of those who call themselves by His name during this age treat Him no better." Several things can be checked in Matthew. The hearers are the people of one synagogue in "His hometown" (13:54). The saying names "his hometown and ... his own household" (13:57), not a nation. The disciples who have just said they understood (13:51) belong to the same people, and so does Jesus' family. The Catholic Church's Second Vatican Council rejected this kind of reading in general terms, without citing Matthew 13. It said that "the Jews should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God, as if this followed from the Holy Scriptures" (*Nostra Aetate* 4, 1965, addressed to Catholic teaching and preaching).

**A pastoral note.** Some readers know this scene from the inside. They have been dismissed or cut off by their own families or hometowns because of their faith, or they are being pressed by others to cut off family who do not share it. Matthew 13:57 records what happened to Jesus; it is not a verdict on anyone's family. This curriculum's lesson on Matthew 10:16-42 (lesson 21) has a fuller pastoral note on family conflict over faith, with resources for people facing pressure from a partner, a household or a high-control group. A pastor you trust, ideally one outside any group involved, or a licensed counselor can help in ways a study note cannot. If anyone is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, a model of the shape and not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 13:54-55: "'Where did this man get such wisdom and miraculous powers?' they asked. 'Isn't this the carpenter's son?'" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the townspeople do not deny the wisdom or the "miraculous powers"; they ask where they came from, and they answer from what they already know about His family. Chrysostom notes that they "find no fault with the teaching, but take refuge in the meanness of His race," meaning His humble family. Inference (this lesson's): for Matthew's first readers, the scene showed that knowing someone's background well can become a reason not to hear him.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Familiarity with a messenger's background can keep a hearer from weighing the message itself.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the habit of asking whether one is judging a message by the messenger's origins. Not carried forward: any claim that a speaker who meets resistance at home must be right, or that critics who know a speaker well are always wrong. The verse records one town's offense; it does not make every rejection a sign of truth.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a narrative report of a response to Jesus, with His proverb about it; it is not a command. Promise scope: the passage promises nothing about how any modern speaker will be received.

**Modern domain and situation.** Church life. A man who grew up in a small congregation returns after years away and is asked to lead a study. A longtime member says, "I remember him as a kid. What could he teach me?"

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: humility in listening. One faithful response: the longtime member attends, weighs what is taught against Scripture as with any teacher, and keeps her memories of the young man from settling the question beforehand.

**Cautions.** Weighing a message on its merits does not mean setting aside real concerns about a teacher's conduct or accountability; those belong with a church's leaders. This bridge is a worked example for the learner to test, not counsel for any particular person.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Scofield argues from 13:38 that "the field is the world" in 13:44 too, and the Pulpit Commentary holds that the same figure can signify something different in a different parable. What would count as evidence that a word keeps its meaning from one parable to the next?
2. Gibbs reads "sold all he had" against the rich young man (19:21-26) and the debtor (18:25); the Pulpit Commentary contrasts the young man's grief with the finder's joy. Can the same cross-reference support two readings? What does each reader take to be the point of comparison?
3. Matthew has "the carpenter's son" and "did not," Mark "the carpenter" and "could not." If you held that Mark wrote first, what would you conclude about Matthew's choices? If you held that Matthew wrote first, what would you conclude about Mark's?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 13:44-58 in order, from the treasure to "their unbelief." (b) Recite 13:52 in the BSB's wording. (c) Name the two differences between Matthew 13:55 and 13:58 and Mark 6:3 and 6:5.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) the three parables and the net's repetition of 13:40-42; (1:00-2:00) Position 1, both readings with one holder each; (2:00-2:45) the scribe and Position 2; (2:45-4:00) Nazareth, the carpenter difference and the Luke question; (4:00-5:00) Position 3, ending on an open question.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: "Reading the buyer as Christ ignores Jesus' own pattern in Matthew 13, where the parables describe how people respond to the word." (Check: who is the actor in 13:3, 13:24 and 13:31, and who sows in 13:37?) A second runs the other way: "Reading the buyer as the disciple makes the kingdom something a person can buy." (Check: what do Calvin and MacLaren say "buy" means, and does their answer meet Scofield's objection?)
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 13:44-46 does not say who the man or the merchant stands for; Jesus explains the net (13:49-50) but not these two. (b) Matthew 13:55-56 names Jesus' brothers and mentions sisters but does not say how they were related to Him (lesson 26, Position 3). (c) Matthew 13:57-58 concerns one hometown; it does not pass judgment on a nation, and it is not a rule that every rejected speaker is a prophet.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words in both texts:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mark 6:1-6 | parallel | strong | The same visit in the same order; "carpenter" for "carpenter's son," "could not" for "did not" |
| Luke 4:16-30 | parallel | plausible | Same town, synagogue, family question and saying, but much more material, an earlier setting, and a disputed identification |
| John 4:44 | parallel | strong | The same saying, attributed by John to Jesus, in a different setting and wording; what "his own hometown" means there is disputed |
| Matthew 13:40-42 | motif | strong | The net's explanation repeats the weeds' explanation word for word in English and in Greek |
| Jeremiah 11:21-12:6 | motif | plausible | A prophet opposed by his own town and his father's household; suggested tentatively by one commentator, with no shared wording or citation |

The Jeremiah row is this lesson's Old Testament thread, and it is only plausible: Matthew does not name Jeremiah, the Pulpit Commentary says "Possibly," and the Expositor's Greek Testament treats the saying as a proverb found far beyond Israel.

Three candidates were considered and not authored. Exodus 19:5 ("you will be My treasured possession," BSB) is central to the Christ-as-buyer reading in Scofield, Gaebelein and Gibbs. Authoring it would settle Position 1 by connection, so it is left as part of that dispute. Proverbs 2:4 ("search it out like hidden treasure") is cited by Ellicott, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown and the Pulpit Commentary as background, not as a text Matthew points to. Song of Songs 7:13 ("new as well as old, that I have treasured up for you," BSB) is cited by Bengel and the Pulpit Commentary for the idiom "new and old"; no source read here argues that Jesus or Matthew meant to evoke it. Each would be a shared word or image, not a connection.
