Matthew 9:18-38 closes the run of healing stories that began at 8:1, and it moves fast: a father whose daughter has died, a woman bleeding for twelve years who touches Jesus's cloak on the way to his house, two blind men, a man who cannot speak, and then a summary, a sentence about compassion, and a saying about a harvest. This lesson's file name uses the traditional English word "ruler" for the father; this app's own BSB text calls him "a synagogue leader" (9:18), and that is the wording quoted throughout. Matthew never gives his name. The name "Jairus" comes from Mark 5:22 and Luke 8:41, and keeping track of what Matthew says, as against what readers usually import from the other two Gospels, is the main study skill this lesson practices. The second skill is checking Old Testament links at the level of the actual words. Several are often attached to this passage: the tassels of Numbers 15, the opened eyes of Isaiah 35, the shepherdless sheep of Numbers 27 and Ezekiel 34, and Malachi 4:2's "healing in its wings." This lesson grades each of them separately, and one of them does not survive the check.

## Context

**What the text says, in order (9:18-38).** "While Jesus was saying these things, a synagogue leader came and knelt before Him. 'My daughter has just died,' he said. 'But come and place Your hand on her, and she will live.'" (9:18, BSB). On the way, "a woman who had suffered from bleeding for twelve years came up behind Him and touched the fringe of His cloak," saying to herself, "If only I touch His cloak, I will be healed" (9:20-21). Jesus "turned and saw her. 'Take courage, daughter,' He said, 'your faith has healed you.' And the woman was cured from that very hour" (9:22). At the house he sees "the flute players and the noisy crowd" and says, "Go away... The girl is not dead, but asleep" -- "And they laughed at Him" (9:23-24). With the crowd put outside, he "took the girl by the hand, and she got up," and the news "spread throughout that region" (9:25-26). Two blind men follow him "crying out, 'Have mercy on us, Son of David!'" (9:27); he asks, "Do you believe that I am able to do this?", touches their eyes, and says, "According to your faith will it be done to you" (9:28-29). Then "their eyes were opened" (9:30a); he warns them, "See that no one finds out about this!", and they spread the news anyway (9:30-31). A mute, demon-possessed man is brought; when the demon is driven out "the man began to speak," the crowds say "Nothing like this has ever been seen in Israel!", and the Pharisees answer, "It is by the prince of demons that He drives out demons" (9:32-34). Matthew then summarizes Jesus's travels (9:35), says that when he saw the crowds "He was moved with compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd" (9:36), and closes with: "The harvest is plentiful, but the workers are few. Ask the Lord of the harvest, therefore, to send out workers into His harvest" (9:37-38).

**What Matthew does not say about the father.** Read on its own, Matthew 9:18-26 gives the father no name, gives the daughter no age, and has the father arrive saying she "has just died." Mark names him ("A synagogue leader named Jairus," 5:22) and has him say, "My little daughter is near death" (5:23), with news of her death arriving only later (5:35). Luke also names him ("a synagogue leader named Jairus," 8:41) and says "his only daughter, who was about twelve, was dying" (8:42); her death is reported later too (8:49). The Wikipedia article on the daughter of Jairus, reporting Arie Zwiep (2015), sums up the difference: "In Matthew, the synagogue ruler is unnamed, the girl's age is not mentioned, she has already 'just died'," so that "in Matthew he requests Jesus to reverse her death rather than prevent it, in contrast to the accounts of Mark and Luke." The same article reports that "Scholars have long recognised the Lukan and Matthean accounts of the story derive from the Markan account." That is a report of a widely held view, given through a tertiary source, not something this lesson verified independently. How readers have handled the dying/dead difference is set out in Positions below.

**The "fringe" of the cloak.** The BSB says the woman "touched the fringe of His cloak" (9:20). The Greek noun is *kraspedon*. Biblehub's lexicon entry, reporting Thayer, glosses it "fringe, tassel, hem, border" and connects it to the tassels "according to Numbers 15:37f." It lists five New Testament occurrences: Matthew 9:20, 14:36, 23:5, Mark 6:56, and Luke 8:44. The Septuagint uses the same noun at Numbers 15:38, where Israel is told "to make for themselves tassels for the corners of their garments, with a blue cord on each tassel" (BSB), "so that you may remember all the commandments of the LORD" (15:39). Matthew himself uses the word again for the Pharisees, who "broaden their phylacteries and lengthen their tassels" (23:5, BSB). Inside Matthew's own Gospel, then, the word names the commanded tassel, not just any edge of a garment. Public-domain commentators make the same identification. Meyer (via the Biblehub aggregator): "Such was the name given to the tassel which, in accordance with Numbers 15:38." Vincent: "The fringe worn on the border of the outer garment, according to the command in Numbers 15:38." The fringe returns as an in-book recurrence at 14:36, where the sick at Gennesaret "begged Him just to let them touch the fringe of His cloak" (BSB). That recurrence is noted here rather than authored as a separate connection.

**The woman's condition.** Leviticus 15:25 (BSB) describes a woman with "a discharge of her blood for many days at a time other than her menstrual period," who "will be unclean all the days of her unclean discharge." Nineteenth-century commentators routinely read Matthew 9:20 against that law. Ellicott: "The 'issue of blood' was probably of the kind that brought with it ceremonial uncleanness (Leviticus 15:26)." The Pulpit Commentary: "Physically and (Leviticus 15:25) ceremonially unclean." It is also a plain, checkable observation about the text that Matthew 9:18-26 never uses the words "unclean," "impure," or "law." What, if anything, the story says about purity is a real dispute, reported in Positions below.

**The flute players.** Jesus finds "the flute players and the noisy crowd" (9:23). The Pulpit Commentary (via the Biblehub aggregator) quotes the Mishnah, tractate Ketubot 4:4, as background for hired mourning music: "Even the poorest among the Israelites [his wife being dead] will afford her not less than two pipes, and one woman to make lamentation." Two cautions apply. The Mishnah was compiled later than the Gospels. And the rule concerns a husband burying his wife, so applying it to a daughter's funeral is an analogy. This lesson did not read the Mishnah text itself.

**"Son of David" from two blind men.** "Have mercy on us, Son of David!" (9:27) is the first time anyone in Matthew's narrative addresses Jesus with the title Matthew 1:1 opened with ("Jesus Christ, the son of David"). This curriculum's genealogy lesson already carries a connection from 1:1 to the promise to David in 2 Samuel 7:12-16. Rather than duplicate it, this lesson notes that the title recurs in Matthew mostly in healing scenes: "Could this be the Son of David?" (12:23), "Lord, Son of David, have mercy on me!" (15:22), and "Lord, Son of David, have mercy on us!" (20:30-31). The publisher's description of Lidija Novakovic's monograph *Messiah, the Healer of the Sick* (2003) states the puzzle plainly: "Even though it is a well-known axiom that the Davidic Messiah was expected neither to do miracles nor to be a healer, Jesus in the Gospel of Matthew is addressed with the messianic title »Son of David« almost exclusively within the context of his healing activity." Dennis Duling's 1975 article in the *Harvard Theological Review*, "Solomon, Exorcism, and the Son of David," opens on the same problem. The title "is associated primarily with a figure who is so addressed by people in need of exorcism or healing," and "The usual solution to this problem in works on Christology" is that miracle working was not linked to the royal Messiah in Jewish texts of the period. Only that opening of Duling's article was read for this lesson, not his full argument. One proposed background is Solomon, David's son, whom Josephus credits with power over demons: "God also enabled him to learn that skill which expels demons" (*Antiquities* 8.2.5, Whiston translation). Novakovic's publisher description rejects that route: "The underlying principle of Matthew's portrayal of Jesus, however, cannot be found in the traditions about Solomon as an exorcist or healer, or about the eschatological prophet like Moses. Rather, the intelligibility of the healing Messiah is secured through Matthew's own skillful interpretation of selected biblical passages, especially from the Book of Isaiah." This lesson reports both lines without deciding between them.

**Opened eyes and Isaiah.** Matthew cites no prophet at 9:27-33. The verses still pair blind men whose "eyes were opened" (9:30) with a man who cannot speak (9:32-33), next to Isaiah 35:5-6: "Then the eyes of the blind will be opened... and the mute tongue will shout for joy" (BSB). Checked in Greek, the match is narrower than the English suggests. The "eyes opened" wording does correspond. Matthew 9:30's *ēneōchthēsan autōn hoi ophthalmoi* uses the same verb and noun as the Septuagint's *anoichthēsontai ophthalmoi typhlōn* (Isaiah 35:5). But the English "mute" match is a coincidence of translation. Matthew's word for the man at 9:32 is *kōphon*, which the Septuagint of Isaiah 35:5 uses for the "deaf" (*ōta kōphōn*), while Isaiah's "mute tongue" in 35:6 is *glōssa mogilalōn*, a different word. The link is drawn more openly two chapters later. When John asks whether Jesus is "the One who was to come" (11:3), Jesus answers, "The blind receive sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the good news is preached to the poor" (11:5, BSB). Meyer (via the Biblehub aggregator) calls those "words that seem an echo of Isaiah 35:5 f., 8, Isaiah 61:1 ff." Matthew 11:5 is a forward pointer here, and the connection attached to this lesson is graded with that distance in mind.

**A charge that returns.** The Pharisees' reply, "It is by the prince of demons that He drives out demons" (9:34), comes back at 12:24 in a fuller form, after a similar healing of a man "who was blind and mute": "Only by Beelzebul, the prince of demons, does this man drive out demons" (BSB). This is an in-book recurrence, noted here and taken up when this curriculum reaches chapter 12.

**The summary, the sheep, and the harvest.** Matthew 9:35 reads: "Jesus went through all the towns and villages, teaching in their synagogues, preaching the gospel of the kingdom, and healing every disease and sickness" (BSB). That is very close to 4:23 (see Literary Design). The phrase in 9:36, "like sheep without a shepherd," appears word for word in this app's BSB at Numbers 27:17, where Moses asks for a successor "so that the congregation of the LORD will not be like sheep without a shepherd." It also appears at 1 Kings 22:17 (and its parallel 2 Chronicles 18:16), where Micaiah sees "all Israel scattered on the hills like sheep without a shepherd." Ezekiel 34:5 has "They were scattered for lack of a shepherd," and Zechariah 10:2 has "the people wander like sheep, oppressed for lack of a shepherd." Mark uses the same phrase in a different setting, before the feeding of the five thousand (Mark 6:34). Older commentators point in different directions. The Pulpit Commentary: "For the thought, cf. Ezekiel 34:5; also Numbers 27:17; 2 Chronicles 18:16; and its parallel passage, 1 Kings 22:17." Bengel: "Cf. Numbers 27:17." MacLaren: Matthew "casts our Lord's impression of what He then saw into language borrowed from the prophecy of Ezekiel." The harvest saying (9:37-38) is word for word identical in this app's BSB to Luke 10:2, where Luke places it at the sending of the seventy-two. On what "harvest" carries, Cleophus LaRue (Working Preacher, 2023) acknowledges that "Some say the harvest is a frequent symbol for eschatological judgment," but states his own view: "In this particular passage I do not believe that the harvest imagery refers to eschatological judgment. The passage is clearly missional in its intent." He adds: "This is not to dismiss judgment completely from this text, for there is a sense in which the merciful shepherd and the Lord of judgment stand in juxtaposition throughout the whole of Matthew's gospel."

## Literary Design

Notes on how 9:18-38 is built, not claims about what that construction is meant to teach.

**Two stories, one inside the other -- with Matthew's link removed.** The Wikipedia article on the daughter of Jairus reports that the paired stories "have been used as an example of intercalation ('sandwich story'), where one incident is inserted within another, linked in this case by the connection between the 12-year ailment and the 12-year-old girl." That twelve/twelve link is real in Mark (5:25, 5:42) and Luke (8:42-43). Matthew keeps the sandwich but not the link: the woman's "twelve years" is there (9:20), the girl's age is not. Checked against this app's text, Matthew's version runs nine verses, against twenty-three in Mark (5:21-43) and seventeen in Luke (8:40-56).

**"Daughter," twice.** The father says "My daughter" (9:18), and Jesus calls the woman "daughter" (9:22). Mark and Luke have the same address (Mark 5:34; Luke 8:48). In Matthew's shorter telling, though, the two uses sit only four verses apart. The opening "Take courage" (9:22) also repeats Jesus's words to the paralytic at the start of the chapter: "Take courage, son; your sins are forgiven" (9:2, BSB). Son at the start of the chapter, daughter here: this is an observable echo inside chapter 9, not a claim about Matthew's intention.

**Who touches whom.** The woman touches Jesus's fringe (9:20-21); Jesus takes the girl "by the hand" (9:25); Jesus "touched their eyes" for the blind men (9:29). In the first scene the touch runs from the person in need to Jesus; in the other two it runs from Jesus. That distinction matters to one of the positions reported below.

**Faith, named three times.** "When Jesus saw their faith" (9:2), "your faith has healed you" (9:22), "According to your faith will it be done to you" (9:29). The blind men are also asked, "Do you believe that I am able to do this?" and answer "Yes, Lord" (9:28). The repetition across the chapter can be checked; how faith and healing relate is a theological question this lesson does not settle.

**Duplicate stories inside Matthew.** Matthew has two blind men again at 20:29-34, crying "Lord, Son of David, have mercy on us!" (20:30). He has a speechless demon-possessed man again at 12:22-24, followed by the same charge from the Pharisees. Ulrich Luz (Hermeneia), as quoted by Christian Think Tank, writes: "It is especially noteworthy that Matthew does not hesitate to relate the same miracle story twice and to do so as different stories." The same page reports R. T. France reading the blind-men stories as distinct events. Both are reported secondhand and neither commentary was read for this lesson.

**A frame: 4:23 and 9:35.** Matthew 4:23 (BSB): "Jesus went throughout Galilee, teaching in their synagogues, preaching the gospel of the kingdom, and healing every disease and sickness among the people." Matthew 9:35 (BSB): "Jesus went through all the towns and villages, teaching in their synagogues, preaching the gospel of the kingdom, and healing every disease and sickness." The middle clause is identical in this app's text. The setting differs ("throughout Galilee" against "through all the towns and villages"), and 9:35 drops "among the people." Newman and Stine's UBS *Handbook* (1988), working from a different English base, calls the repetition "word for word." They report that "several scholars conclude that these two verses summarize the contents of chapters 5–9," and add: "One commentator characterizes the entire section 4.23–9.34 as 'the work of Jesus Christ in word and deed.'" On that reading, chapters 5-7 (teaching) and 8-9 (healing) sit inside a frame that opens and closes with the same summary. The Handbook frames that as a conclusion several scholars draw, and this lesson reports it the same way.

## Positions

Three questions in this passage draw named, published disagreement. None is settled here.

**1. Does the bleeding woman's story show Jesus setting aside the purity laws?** Some readers say yes. Marcus Borg's *Meeting Jesus Again for the First Time* (1994), quoted here from a blog's excerpt rather than the printed book, reads Jesus's healings this way: "The stories of his healings shatter the purity boundaries of his social world. He touched lepers and haemorrhaging women." He concludes: "The politics of purity was replaced by a politics of compassion."

Amy-Jill Levine ("Matthew and Anti-Judaism," *Currents in Theology and Mission*, 2007) rejects that reading as one preachers commonly make: that "by touching the hemorrhaging woman and then a corpse (9:18-26) Jesus does away with the purity laws." Her first objection is to what the reading implies about Judaism: "This interpretation reinforces the view that Judaism is about law and Jesus is about grace; worse, it suggests that the law makes women into 'outcasts and marginals.'" Her corrections are then textual: "Jesus does not touch the woman; she touches him. Indeed, she touches his 'fringe,' his tzitzit, which symbolizes the law (9:20). Second, there is no law forbidding such touch... Fourth, no version of this story (see also Mark 5:22-43 and Luke 8:41-56) says anything about purity laws." Her fifth point is about emphasis: "to focus on purity takes the emphasis off the story's good news--a woman who takes initiative and the healing of a body." On the girl she writes, "To touch a corpse is not a sin."

Matthew Thiessen's *Jesus and the Forces of Death* (Baker Academic, 2020), reported here through Christine Hayes's review rather than read directly, takes a third route. Hayes reports that Thiessen treats the account as one in which "the story is clearly a story about impurity and not merely healing," and that he answers the reading Borg represents directly: "Against this widespread view, Thiessen maintains that no such violations occur in the gospel narratives." Thiessen, as Hayes reports him, calls that view "a retrojection of later Christian supersessionist views" onto Jesus. Hayes adds: "As Thiessen correctly emphasizes, to destroy the sources of ritual impurity is not to destroy the system of ritual impurity itself (6, 93, 121); on the contrary, to do battle with ritual impurity and to vanquish it assumes the latter's reality and power." On this reading Jesus works within the purity system and removes the source of the impurity, rather than abolishing the system.

Both Levine and Thiessen, in the sources above, present the reading in which Jesus abolishes purity law as resting on a caricature of first-century Judaism. More broadly, readings that portray Jewish purity law of the period as oppressive have been criticized as feeding Christian anti-Judaism. All three readers agree the woman is healed and that her condition was serious. They disagree about whether purity is a subject of the story at all (Levine: no version of the story raises it; Thiessen: it is "clearly a story about impurity"), and, if it is, whether Jesus overturns the system (Borg) or works within it (Thiessen). Levine's first and fourth points can be checked against the text. In Matthew 9:20-22 she touches him, and he "turned and saw her"; and, as Context notes, the passage never uses the words "unclean," "impure," or "law." Everything beyond that is the readers' own argument.

**2. "The girl is not dead, but asleep" (9:24): was she dead?** Heinrich Meyer's commentary (via the Biblehub aggregator) records that some nineteenth-century scholars read Jesus's words as describing apparent death only, a coma or trance. He names them: "Paulus, Schleiermacher, Olshausen, Ewald, Schenkel; Weizsäcker, without being quite decided." Meyer rejects that reading: "It is wrong to found upon these words the supposition of a mere apparent death." The Pulpit Commentary agrees: to take the words "as a literal statement of a present fact, meaning that she was only in a trance, is to contradict the words of the messenger." The Cambridge Bible, by contrast, leaves the question open: "it is open to question whether they ought not to be taken literally," noting that the Greek word for "sleepeth" (*katheudei*) "does not bear the metaphorical force" of *koimasthai*, the verb more often used for death as sleep. The Expositor's Greek Testament calls the saying "A welcome word to naturalistic commentators, giving a plausible basis for the hypothesis of an apparent death or swoon," but holds that it is "not to be taken prosaically as meant to deny death." It then answers Carr's Cambridge Greek Testament view directly: "The derisive laughter of the crowd (κατεγέλων) is good evidence to the contrary." The texts add data. Matthew's father says she "has just died" (9:18). Luke says the mourners laughed, "knowing that she was dead" (8:53), and that "Her spirit returned" (8:55). John's Gospel has Jesus use sleep language of a man who is plainly dead ("Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep," John 11:11, BSB), but with *kekoimētai*, the *koimasthai* verb, not Matthew's *katheudei*. A possible counter-case runs the other way: 1 Thessalonians 5:10 uses *katheudōmen*, Matthew's verb, in a sentence about Christ: "He died for us so that, whether we are awake or asleep, we may live together with Him" (BSB). Whether "asleep" there means death is itself a reading to test; this lesson did not source one. This lesson reports the readings and does not rule on them.

**3. Matthew's "has just died" against Mark's "near death": a discrepancy, or a compression?** Augustine (*Harmony of the Gospels* 2.28) states the problem directly: Matthew "represents the ruler of the synagogue to have spoken to the Lord of his daughter, not merely as one likely to die, or as dying, or as on the very point of expiring, but as even then dead." His answer is that "by reason of brevity in the narrative, Matthew has preferred to express it as if the Lord had been really asked to do what it is clear He did actually do," and that "Matthew has exhibited rather what the man secretly wished and thought." John Calvin's *Harmony* says Matthew "merely glances at those particulars which the other two give in minute detail," and "puts down at the very beginning of his narrative what took place at various times": "Jairus first requested that his daughter might be cured of her disease, and afterwards that she might be restored from death to life." Barnes says similarly: "Matthew combined the two facts." Meyer takes a different line: "The want of harmony here with Mark 5:23... is to be recognised," and Matthew "follows a condensed form of the tradition." All four readers agree that Matthew's wording is not what the father literally said when he first arrived. Augustine has Matthew giving "what the man secretly wished and thought," and Calvin and Barnes have him putting at the start what happened over time. The real split is over what to call that. Augustine, Calvin, and Barnes treat it as a faithful compression of the father's intent and of the event as a whole. Meyer treats it as a "want of harmony" with Mark that should be acknowledged rather than explained away. This lesson reports both views and does not rank them.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage states in its own setting to a modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key; the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 9:36-38: "When He saw the crowds, He was moved with compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd. Then He said to His disciples, 'The harvest is plentiful, but the workers are few. Ask the Lord of the harvest, therefore, to send out workers into His harvest.'" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the text gives Jesus's inner response first ("moved with compassion"), then a diagnosis ("harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd"), then an instruction to the disciples. The instruction is not "go" but "Ask," addressed to "the Lord of the harvest." In the very next verse "His twelve disciples" are given authority (10:1), and a few verses later they are sent (10:5). Inference: a first hearer would have heard need that outstripped the people available to meet it, and a first response that was prayer about who would be sent rather than immediate activity. The disciples who are told to ask then turn out to be among the ones sent. This step goes beyond the text's wording and is labeled as this lesson's own.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When need is larger than the people available, the passage's sequence -- see it, feel it, ask for workers, be willing to be one -- is a different response from either despair or frantic activity.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the order (compassion, honest diagnosis, asking, readiness to be sent). Not carried forward: the agricultural image, any claim about what the "harvest" refers to (see LaRue in Context), or any promise about how many workers will come or when.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a command addressed to the disciples ("Ask," 9:38), carried to a later reader by analogy, not addressed to the reader by name. Promise scope: none. The passage does not promise that asking produces workers, how many, or on what timeline.

**Modern domain and situation.** Volunteer service. The coordinator of a church food pantry sees demand double over a winter while the volunteer list shrinks to four people, the same four each week, who are exhausted.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: prayer joined to a concrete, limited action, not prayer instead of action or action instead of prayer. One faithful response: the coordinator names the shortfall honestly to the congregation, prays specifically for more workers alongside the team, invites people by name, and reduces hours rather than letting the four burn out while waiting.

**Cautions.** This passage does not make anyone personally responsible for meeting every need they see, and it is not a reason to shame people who cannot volunteer. If a caregiver or volunteer is exhausted to the point of affecting their health, sleep, or mood, the right next step is a doctor, a counselor, or a pastor, not more effort. The bridge above is an analogy the learner is testing, not a staffing plan.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Matthew tells the synagogue leader's story in nine verses where Mark uses twenty-three, and in doing so has the father report a death Mark and Luke place later. What would you need to know about how ancient writers abbreviated their sources to judge whether Augustine's explanation (Matthew reports "what the man secretly wished and thought") or Meyer's (a "condensed form of the tradition") better describes what Matthew did?
2. Levine notes that "no version of this story... says anything about purity laws," yet many commentators (Ellicott, the Pulpit Commentary) read Leviticus 15 straight into it. When a text is silent on something its first readers would likely have known, how much of that background is it fair to bring in, and who decides?
3. "Son of David" appears in Matthew mostly on the lips of the sick and the outsider (blind men, a Canaanite woman). Novakovic's publisher description locates the explanation in Isaiah, while the Solomon-exorcist background Josephus reports is another proposal. What evidence in Matthew's own text would you look for to tell these two explanations apart?
4. The exact phrase "like sheep without a shepherd" belongs to Numbers 27:17 and 1 Kings 22:17, while Ezekiel 34 shares the theme and the later "lost sheep" language (10:6). When wording points one way and theme points another, which should weigh more in grading an allusion, and why?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) narrate 9:18-38 in order: the father, the woman, the girl, the two blind men, the mute man, the summary, the sheep, the harvest. (b) State what Matthew calls the father in this app's BSB, and say where the name "Jairus" comes from. (c) State the father's first words in Matthew and how they differ from his words in Mark 5:23.

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the plain sequence (Context); (1:00-2:00) Matthew against Mark and Luke: unnamed father, "has just died," no age given, with the two readings of that difference (Positions 3); (2:00-3:00) the fringe as the Numbers 15 tassel, and the purity question with at least two named readers (Context; Positions 1); (3:00-4:00) "Son of David" and opened eyes, with Matthew 11:5 as the later, clearer pointer to Isaiah (Context); (4:00-5:00) the 4:23/9:35 frame, the sheep, and the harvest, ending on one open question.

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: "This lesson treats Matthew as if he 'changed' Mark, but Augustine and Calvin show the accounts fit together, so talking about differences only unsettles readers." Before answering, note that the differences reported here are checkable in the text (the name, the age, "has just died" against "near death"), and that Positions 3 reports Augustine's and Calvin's harmonizations in their own words alongside Meyer's. A second candidate running the other way: "Harmonizing is special pleading; Matthew simply contradicts Mark." Ask what Augustine's appeal to the father's state of mind would need in order to count as evidence rather than a rescue.

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 9:18-26 does not name the synagogue leader, and does not say how old the girl was; both details come from Mark and Luke (Mark 5:22, 5:42; Luke 8:41-42). (b) Matthew 9:20-22 does not mention purity, uncleanness, or the law. Levine's point that the story says nothing about purity laws is a claim about that silence, and it can be checked against the text. Borg's reading (Jesus shatters purity boundaries) and Thiessen's (Jesus works within the purity system and removes the impurity's source) are each inferences from first-century background, not something the passage states.

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words in both texts:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mark 5:21-43 | parallel | strong | Same paired stories and near-identical sayings; Mark names Jairus, has the girl "near death," and adds material Matthew lacks |
| Luke 8:40-56 | parallel | strong | Same paired stories; Luke names Jairus and has the girl "dying," but shares Matthew's "fringe" against Mark |
| Numbers 15:37-41 | motif | strong | Matthew's word for "fringe" is the Septuagint's word for the commanded tassels, and Matthew 23:5 uses it that way; Matthew does not echo Numbers' wording, so motif, not allusion |
| Isaiah 35:5-6 | allusion | plausible | The opened eyes match the Septuagint of Isaiah 35:5 in verb and noun, but the English "mute" match is a translation coincidence and there is no citation here; Matthew 11:5 is the clearer pointer |
| Ezekiel 34 | allusion | plausible | The verbatim phrase matches Numbers 27:17 and 1 Kings 22:17; Ezekiel 34 rests on a thematic cluster (healing the sick, seeking the lost, "My servant David") and on scholarship read only by description |

Or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`. The strongest candidate this lesson declined is the popular claim that the woman reached for Jesus's fringe because of Malachi 4:2: "the sun of righteousness will rise with healing in its wings" (BSB). The claim rests on one real fact. The Hebrew word *kanaph* means both "wing" and "corner," and Biblehub's concordance lists it at both Numbers 15:38 ("corners of their garments") and Malachi 4:2 ("wings"). Two further checks are reported here without weighing for or against the link. First, the grammar: in Malachi the "wings" are the sun's. Biblehub's interlinear parses the Hebrew *bikhnapheha* with a feminine singular suffix, matching "its" in the BSB, and Brown-Driver-Briggs (as reported by Biblehub) files the verse under wings "of sun of righteousness"; the Septuagint's *autou* likewise agrees with *hēlios*, "sun." That is not an argument against the link, because readers who take the link take "the sun of righteousness" itself as a messianic figure. Second, the Greek: it does not cleanly separate "tassel" from "corner." Matthew's word *kraspedon* is the Septuagint's word for the tassels at Numbers 15:38, but the Septuagint also uses it for the Hebrew *kanaph*, "corner," at Deuteronomy 22:12 ("the four corners of the cloak," BSB; Septuagint *epi tōn tessarōn kraspedōn*) and at Zechariah 8:23 ("the robe of a Jew," BSB; Septuagint *tou kraspedou andros Ioudaiou*). Thayer gives the word's classical sense as "edge, skirt, margin." The Greek therefore neither strengthens nor weakens the Malachi link.

What the decline rests on is the absence of an argument. No named scholar was found who argues that Matthew intends Malachi 4:2. Dale Allison (*New Cambridge History of the Bible*, 2013, as quoted by intertextual.bible) raises it only as a question: "given that Jews spoke of the fringes of their garments as 'wings' (Numbers 15:38; Deuteronomy 22:12)," does the fringe-healing notice "derive from a christological reading of Malachi 4:2"? Wikipedia attributes the link only to unnamed "Christian interpreters." None of the public-domain commentaries on 9:20 consulted for this lesson mention Malachi. For this link to be authored, a named scholar would need to argue for it from evidence that Matthew or his first readers connected the tassel with Malachi's "wings." Popular repetition of the claim is not that evidence.

Two smaller declines are worth naming. A 9:27 to 2 Samuel 7 connection was not authored because the genealogy lesson's connection from 1:1 already carries the "Son of David" thread. Numbers 27:17 was not authored as a separate row, despite matching 9:36 word for word, because the same phrase also stands verbatim at 1 Kings 22:17 and 2 Chronicles 18:16, so the wording alone cannot pick out one source.
