Matthew 20:1-16 is a story about a day's pay. A landowner hires workers at dawn and agrees "to pay them a denarius for the day" (20:2, BSB). He hires more at the third, sixth, ninth and eleventh hours, promising the first of these later groups "whatever is right" (20:4). At evening he has his foreman pay "starting with the last ones hired" (20:8), and everyone receives a denarius. The first hired grumble: "you have made them equal to us who have borne the burden and the scorching heat of the day" (20:12). He answers, "Friend, I am not being unfair to you. Did you not agree with me on one denarius?" (20:13), and asks, "Do I not have the right to do as I please with what is mine? Or are you envious because I am generous?" (20:15). The story is bracketed by one saying, given in both orders (19:30; 20:16). This lesson checks that frame, the Greek behind "envious," the BSB's notes on the coin and the hours, and a line some manuscripts add. It reports two disputes without settling them, sets a rabbinic parable beside this one, and names one misuse plainly. Every BSB quotation below was checked by script against this app's own Bible files.

## Context

**Where it sits.** A rich young man has gone "away in sorrow, because he had great wealth" (19:22). Peter says, "we have left everything to follow You. What then will there be for us?" (19:27). Jesus promises the Twelve "twelve thrones" (19:28) and everyone who has left home or family "a hundredfold" and "eternal life" (19:29), then says, "But many who are first will be last, and the last will be first" (19:30). The parable follows at once, opening with "For" (Greek *gar*). Meyer reads that word as "explaining and confirming what has been said in Matthew 19:30"; Haydock notes that "The participle for, is found in the Greek, and connects the present parable with the last verse of the preceding chapter." After the parable come the third prediction of the Passion (20:17-19) and the request for the seats at Jesus' right and left (20:20-23), which ends with "whoever wants to be first among you must be your slave" (20:27). The commentaries read here note that the parable is found only in Matthew (Meyer, the Cambridge Bible, the Pulpit Commentary).

**The frame (19:30; 20:16).** A case-insensitive, word-boundary script over this app's BSB finds "first will be last" and "last will be first" in the same four verses: Matthew 19:30 and 20:16, Mark 10:31 and Luke 13:30. In Matthew the saying occurs only at these two places, one on each side of the parable. The order is reversed: 19:30 begins with "first," 20:16 with "last," and in Greek the same four words are used in every edition Bible Hub lists. The Pulpit Commentary: "The parable concludes with the saying with which it began (Matthew 19:30), but with some inversion in the order of the words." This lesson's observation is that the story itself enacts the inverted order at 20:8, where the foreman pays "starting with the last ones hired and moving on to the first." Readers differ on what the saying means here. Chrysostom notes that in the parable "the first did not become last, but all received the same"; Meyer reads "first will be last" as "the former receive no more than the latter." The row joining 19:30 to 20:16 is connection conn-matt20-last-first-matt19.

**"For many are called, but few are chosen" (20:16).** The BSB footnote reads: "BYZ and TR include For many are called, but few are chosen." On Bible Hub's parallel page, the Byzantine Majority text, the Greek Orthodox text and both Textus Receptus editions add the clause; the SBL, Nestle, Westcott-Hort and Tischendorf texts end at "the first will be last." In the BSB text the saying stands only at 22:14, after the wedding banquet. The commentators divide. The Cambridge Bible: the clause "is omitted in the best MSS. The words are probably interpolated." Ellicott: "The better MSS., indeed, omit it here." The Pulpit Commentary: "This clause is omitted by א, B, and other manuscripts; but it has good authority, and is most probably genuine." Chrysostom's text includes it, and he comments on it. A second footnote reports a smaller Byzantine addition at the end of 20:7.

**The denarius.** The BSB footnote on 20:2: "A denarius was customarily a day's wage for a laborer; similarly in verses 9, 10, and 13." Older commentators priced it in their own money: Calvin's editor at "about sevenpence-halfpenny," as did Matthew Henry, who adds that it was "wages then enough for the day's support." On the BSB's note, the sum agreed at dawn was the customary one. In Matthew's BSB, "denarius" occurs in five verses (20:2, 9, 10, 13; 22:19, the coin shown for the tax), "wages" only at 20:8, and "vineyard" in ten verses, all in 20:1-8 and 21:28-41, the parables of the two sons and the tenants (script searches).

**The hours.** The BSB footnotes give the third hour as "about nine in the morning," the sixth and ninth as "about noon and again about three in the afternoon," and the eleventh as "about five in the afternoon." "Eleventh hour" occurs in the BSB only at 20:6 and 20:9 (script search).

**Payment at evening (20:8).** Several commentators read 20:8 against the law of wages. Ellicott: "It was one of the humane rules of the Mosaic law that the day-labourer was to be paid by the day, and not made to wait for his wages (Deuteronomy 24:15). This law the householder keeps, and his doing so is a feature in his character." Deuteronomy 24:15 reads, "You are to pay his wages each day before sunset, because he is poor and depends on them" (BSB), and Leviticus 19:13, "You must not withhold until morning the wages due a hired hand" (BSB). The link is graded in connection conn-matt20-wages-deut24.

**"Friend" (20:13).** The Greek is *hetaire*, "companion." Bible Hub's concordance lists three New Testament occurrences, all in Matthew and all in the vocative: here, to the guest "without wedding clothes" (22:12), and to Judas at the arrest (26:50); the same address thus goes to the grumbler, to the guest and to Judas. (The Byzantine and Textus Receptus texts also have *hetairois* at 11:16.) The BSB renders all three "Friend." Bengel names the grumbler "the ἑταῖρος" and writes that "all do fail who conduct themselves as" he does.

**"Are you envious because I am generous?" (20:15).** The Greek reads literally, "Is your eye evil because I am good?" (*ho ophthalmos sou poneros estin hoti ego agathos eimi*), in every edition Bible Hub lists. In this clause the editions differ only in the word that introduces it: the SBL, Nestle, Westcott-Hort and Tischendorf texts read *e* ("or"), the Byzantine, Greek Orthodox and Textus Receptus texts *ei* ("if"). The BSB translates the idiom rather than the words. Commentators gloss it. Barnes: "The Hebrews used the word evil, when applied to the eye, to denote one envious and malicious, Deuteronomy 15:9; Proverbs 23:6." Ellicott: the evil eye was, "as in Proverbs 28:22, that which looked with envy and ill will at the prosperity of others," and he notes the popular belief that such a glance could "injure." Gill quotes a rabbinic saying about giving "with a good eye," which he glosses "bountifully and generously." The BSB renders the Hebrew idiom in several ways, so an English reader does not see it. In Deuteronomy 15:9, where Bible Hub's interlinear gives "and is evil ... your eye," the BSB has "you look upon your poor brother begrudgingly"; in Proverbs 23:6 and 28:22, where the interlinear glosses the Hebrew *ra' 'ayin* word by word as "evil" and "eye," the BSB has "a stingy man." The Septuagint of Deuteronomy 15:9 (Swete) uses the verb: *ponēreusētai ho ophthalmos sou*, "your eye be evil" toward your brother. Within Matthew the same Greek, *ho ophthalmos sou poneros*, stands in 6:23, which the BSB renders "if your eyes are bad"; see connection conn-matt20-evil-eye-matt6. Mark 7:22 has *ophthalmos poneros* in its list of vices, which the BSB renders "envy." The English "envious" occurs in two BSB verses, Proverbs 24:19 and Matthew 20:15; "generous" occurs in Matthew only at 20:15 (script search).

**A rabbinic parallel.** The Jerusalem Talmud, Berakhot 2:8 (Guggenheimer's translation on Sefaria; Gill cites it by folio, "fol. 5. 3"), records a eulogy. When Rebbi Abun (Bun) bar Rebbi Ḥiyya died, Rebbi Zeïra eulogized him with a parable: "To a king who hired many workers and there was one worker who was exceptionally productive in his work." The king "took him and walked with him the long and the short. In the evening, the workers came to receive their wages and he gave him his total wages with them." The workers complained: "we were toiling the entire day and this one did toil only for two hours and he gave him his total wages with us!" The king answered: "This one produced in two hours more than what you produced all day long." Rebbi Zeïra applied it: "So Rebbi Bun produced in Torah in 28 years what an outstanding student cannot learn in a hundred years." On dating: Guggenheimer's note describes Rebbi Bun as "a student of Rebbi Yoḥanan and Rebbi Zeïra" who "died young," and Sefaria's index dates the compilation of the Jerusalem Talmud to 375-425. Gill quotes a version, citing Song of Songs Rabbah, Ecclesiastes Rabbah and the Jerusalem Talmud together, in which the king has "a vineyard"; Guggenheimer's note also lists the two midrashim. Those versions were not read here. Neither text cites the other. They share a hiring, an evening payment, full wages for a short day, and the complaint of those who worked all day. They differ in the answer. In the eulogy the king points to the worker's output: "This one produced in two hours more." In Matthew the landowner points to the agreement and to his own will: "Did you not agree with me on one denarius?" and "I want to give this last man the same as I gave you" (20:13-14). This lesson reports the two parables side by side and does not rank them.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 20:1-16 is built, not on what the construction teaches.

**Five hirings, two kinds of terms.** Only the first group has a stated sum, "a denarius for the day" (20:2). The third-hour group is promised "whatever is right" (20:4); the sixth- and ninth-hour groups get "the same thing" (20:5); the eleventh-hour group is simply sent (20:7, in the text the BSB follows). Calvin: the householder hires the first "at a fixed price" and the others "without an agreement."

**A reversed payment line.** The order of payment, last to first (20:8), lets the first hired watch others paid in full before their own turn, and "they assumed they would receive more" (20:10). Barnes: "Had those first hired been first paid, they would have departed satisfied, and the point of the parable would have been lost." The landowner's answer ends in two questions (20:15), and the grumblers do not reply.

## Positions

Two questions draw named, published disagreement. None is settled here; each reading is given in its holders' own words.

**1. What do the hours and the workers stand for?**

*The times of a human life.* Chrysostom: "the parable is spoken with reference to them who from earliest youth, and those who in old age and more tardily, lay hold on virtue; to the former, that they may not be proud, neither reproach those called at the eleventh hour; to the latter, that they may learn that it is possible even in a short time to recover all." He holds that the call went out to all, and that "some are called early in the morning, some at the third hour, some at the sixth, some at the ninth, some at the eleventh, when they would obey." Gregory the Great gives this reading as his second: "We may also see in these same varying hours the changing of the years in the life of every man," from "the childhood of our reason" to old age at the eleventh hour.

*The ages of history.* Gregory's first reading, in a homily preached on Septuagesima Sunday (Toale's translation; the Catena Aurea numbers it Homily 19 on the Gospels): "the morning of the world was from Adam to Noah: the third hour from Noah to Abraham: the sixth from Abraham to Moses: the ninth from Moses till the Coming of the Lord: the eleventh from the Lord's Coming till the end of the world." The vineyard is "the Universal Church, which, from the time of just Abel until the last of the elect" has "brought forth as many saints, as it has sent forth shoots." Meyer reports that Origen read the hours as epochs "(1) till the flood; (2) till Abraham; (3) till Moses; (4) till Christ; (5) till the end of the world".

*Israel and the Gentiles.* Gregory's first reading continues: the early workers "signify the ancient Jewish people, who, in their elect have from the beginning of the world endeavoured to serve God in true belief," and "at the eleventh hour the Gentiles were called," who answer "no man hath hired us; since no Patriarch had come to them, and no Prophet." Matthew Henry: "The direct object of this parable seems to be, to show that though the Jews were first called into the vineyard, at length the gospel should be preached to the Gentiles, and they should be admitted to equal privileges and advantages with the Jews." Poole: "This seems to be directly intended by our Saviour" (the same sentence goes on to the portrait of Jews named below). How this reading has been turned against Jews is named below.

*An answer to Peter's question.* Calvin: Christ "only declares that those who were first in point of time have no right to boast or to insult others; because the Lord, whenever he pleases, may call those whom he appeared for a time to disregard, and may make them equal, or even superior, to the first." The Cambridge Bible: "the only true explanation of its meaning to the disciples at the time must be reached by considering the question to which it is an answer," that is, 19:27. Meyer takes the hours as "various periods between the time of Christ and the close" of the age, at which believers enter the service of the kingdom.

*A story about work, not a code for God.* Amy-Jill Levine, a Jewish scholar of the New Testament, writes in *Short Stories by Jesus* (2014), as Kevin Spinale quotes her in *America* (2015), that many readers take up "the easy allegory that the landowner is God, and the parable becomes how we are to understand the divine. Once allegory enters, the real world is left behind, as well as any concern the parable—and Jesus, its teller—might have for issues of economics, employment, and the relationship between managers and employees" (p. 199). Her own summary: "the last hired benefit from the contract made with their coworkers; they benefit from an employer who is generous with his money," and "the rich recognize their responsibility to the less well off, a responsibility that includes not simply giving a handout, but hiring workers who can thus preserve their dignity" (p. 219).

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the life-stages reading, the Pulpit Commentary (arguing for the national reading): "if the hours represent the stages of human life at which Christians are called, surely, to make the parable concinnous, they ought to be the same persons who are invited on each occasion, not different ones." It adds that "If the 'penny' signifies eternal life, and the labourers are all the called, there is no satisfactory explanation" of the grumbling, since "none can murmur" in heaven. (Chrysostom and Gregory each answer that objection in their own way: for Chrysostom the grumbling shows only that the honor given "could even have begotten envy in others"; for Gregory the grumblers are the fathers who waited long for the Kingdom.) Meyer allows that it "can only be of service in the way of edifying application," but "it is not reconcilable with the historical sense of the passage." Against the ages-of-history reading, Meyer on Origen's epochs: "This view is decidedly forbidden by Matthew 19:29 f." Against the Israel-and-Gentiles reading, Calvin: "all subtleties of that sort are unseasonable; for the Lord makes no distinction in the bargain, but only in the time"; Meyer calls it "No less foreign." Against the answer-to-Peter reading, no source read here objects to it; the Pulpit Commentary, which argues for the national reading, itself calls the reply to Peter the parable's "primary application." No source read here answers Levine directly. Calvin's gloss, that Christ "compares the spiritual life to the earthly life, and the reward of eternal life to money," represents the reading she sets aside. This lesson does not decide among them; several readers above hold more than one.

**2. What does the equal wage say about reward?**

*A reward given by grace, not owed to merit.* Calvin: "since God defrauds no man of a just hire, He is at liberty to bestow on those whom He has lately called an undeserved reward." God "owes no man any thing," and "This is also the reason why he gives the name of a hire to the crown which he bestows freely." Calvin also limits the claim. He declines to reduce the parable to "this general proposition," that all will have equal glory "because the heavenly inheritance is not obtained by the merits of works, but is bestowed freely," because "Christ does not here argue either about the equality of the heavenly glory, or about the future condition of the godly." The Pulpit Commentary: "the reward is not of merit, but of free grace. This last thought pervades the whole similitude." Gill, on 20:8: eternal life may be called hire, "yet it will not be bestowed by way of merit."

*A reward given by grace to merit that is itself God's gift.* Haydock's Catholic commentary: "Jesus Christ represents heaven as given wholly as a just reward of meritorious labour in the vineyard, though some labour a shorter, and others a longer time, and God of his great goodness may give more to some than to others, while to all He gives at least their due." Witham, whom Haydock quotes, adds: "The rewards in heaven are also different. And they who are last called, if they labour with greater fervour, may deserve a greater reward than others called before them." Augustine, as the Catena Aurea quotes him: "Because that life eternal shall be equal to all the saints, a denarius is given to all; but forasmuch as in that life eternal the light of merits shall shine diversely, there are with the Father many mansions." Neither the Council of Trent's chapter on merit nor the Catechism's paragraphs on merit cite this parable, but they state the doctrine these readers apply. Trent (Session 6, chapter 16) says that nothing is lacking for the justified "to have truly merited eternal life, to be obtained also in its (due) time, if so be, however, that they depart in grace," and that God's "bounty towards all men is so great, that He will have the things which are His own gifts be their merits." The Catechism: "With regard to God, there is no strict right to any merit on the part of man" (§2007); "no one can merit the initial grace of forgiveness and justification, at the beginning of conversion. Moved by the Holy Spirit and by charity, we can then merit for ourselves and for others the graces needed for our sanctification, for the increase of grace and charity, and for the attainment of eternal life" (§2010); and "The saints have always had a lively awareness that their merits were pure grace" (§2011).

**Objections each reading faces.** Against the first reading, Haydock: "Calvin is rather unhappy in his choice of this parable to prove his favourite tenet, that salvation is not the reward of good works, but of faith alone, or predestination." (Calvin's commentary here says "no man ever obtained salvation by his merits," while declining to make the parable a proof of equal glory.) Meyer also judges that the proposition "that, in dispensing the blessings of the kingdom of heaven, God takes no account of human merit" does "not constitute the leading thought set forth in the parable, though, no doubt, it may be supposed to underlie it." Against the second reading, Gill holds that the saints "cannot merit anything at his hand, and much less eternal life." Commentators who do not use the language of merit here also speak of degrees: Ellicott, "There also there will be some first, some last, but the difference of degree will depend, not on the duration of service, nor even on the amount of work done, but on the temper and character of the worker," and Benson warns against "concluding" from the parable "that the rewards to be conferred after death ... will be equal in all that receive them." This lesson does not decide between them.

**A misuse to name plainly.** Holding the Israel-and-Gentiles reading is not the same as using it against Jews, and Gregory's version calls the early workers the Jewish people who "in their elect" served God "in true belief." But several readers in this lesson's sources pressed the reading further and made the grumbling a portrait of the Jewish people. In the Catena Aurea, Jerome, in one alternative, reads the grumblers as those called of old, pained at the Gentiles' grace; Hilary ties the murmur to what he calls the stubbornness of the Jewish nation since Moses; and Remigius gives the Jews the evil eye of 20:15, though he reads the one addressed in 20:13 as "all the believing Jews, whom he calls friends because of their faith." Poole attributes pride and envy to the Jews as a people. Benson and Gill, quoted elsewhere in this lesson on other points, also characterize the Jews as a people on these verses: Benson writes of Gentiles "despised and looked down upon with contempt by the Jews," and Gill on 20:15 of privileges given to Gentiles "whilst the Jews despise and reject them." The Pulpit Commentary, arguing for its national reading, concludes that "God's ancient people, once the first, become by their jealousy and hatred of others the last." What answered it came from several directions. From within the commentary tradition, Calvin rejected the reading for this parable: "There is no foundation, therefore, for what some have imagined, that these words are directed against the Jews." His sentence, though, repeats the hostile portrait while rejecting it for this parable, and his reason is that it would be absurd for such persons to receive "an equal hire with the children of God." Meyer called the reference to Jews and Gentiles "foreign" to the text. The Catholic Church's Second Vatican Council, in *Nostra Aetate* §4 (1965), said that "the Jews should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God, as if this followed from the Holy Scriptures," that God "does not repent of the gifts He makes or of the calls He issues," and that the Church "decries hatred, persecutions, displays of anti-Semitism, directed against Jews at any time and by anyone." Levine, writing as a Jewish scholar, reads the parable without the allegory (as Spinale reports), and her reviewer Spinale says her reading "strips away the notion that the last to be hired for the vineyard are the Gentiles and those who bicker about laboring all day are Jews who have lived in covenant with God for centuries." What the text says can be checked. The parable names no nation: the workers are only "the original workers" (20:10) and "this last man" (20:14). In Matthew's setting the parable follows Peter's question (19:27-30), and the grumbler is addressed as "Friend" (20:13). Matthew elsewhere does use the image of a vineyard taken from its tenants (21:41); that parable belongs to its own lesson.

A reader working through what these questions mean for their own standing before God needs a pastor or a trusted teacher in their own tradition, not a study note.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, a model of the shape and not an answer key. The learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 20:13: "But he answered one of them, 'Friend, I am not being unfair to you. Did you not agree with me on one denarius?'" (BSB)

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the landowner answers the complaint by pointing to the agreement made at dawn (20:2) and kept at evening (20:8); the grumblers do not say they were paid less than agreed. Whatever the parable says about God (Positions 1 and 2), its story depends on a hiring custom its hearers knew: an agreed day's wage, paid at day's end. Inference (this lesson's): a first audience would have recognized the agreement and the evening payment as the ordinary terms of day labor, the terms the law of Deuteronomy 24:15 protected.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** An agreed wage, paid in full and on time, is a fixed point in a working relationship; whether another person's pay is fair to *them* is a separate question from whether one's own agreement was kept.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: an agreement about pay, the keeping of it, and the difference between "was my agreement kept?" and "what did someone else receive?" Not carried forward: any rule about how employers must set pay, any claim that the landowner's "Do I not have the right to do as I please with what is mine?" (20:15) settles what fair pay is, and any verdict on workers who question their pay.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a parable's story-world, used by analogy; the parable is told about "the kingdom of heaven" (20:1), not as a labor rule. Promise scope: none. Nothing here promises that pay will be fair or that keeping an agreement prevents disputes.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. A seasonal farmworker agreed to a day rate and was paid it. A worker hired that afternoon was paid the same full day's rate. Some on the crew are angry, and a few talk about taking it out on the new hire.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: honesty in dealings; keeping one's own question clear. One faithful response: the worker checks what was agreed and what was paid; if the agreement was kept, they decline to treat the new hire as the problem; if they believe they were not paid what they are owed, they raise that directly with the employer, and if it is not resolved, with someone qualified (see Cautions).

**Cautions.** This bridge does not apply the landowner's words to any real employer. The Theology of Work Project warns that reading the parable as "Don't compare your pay to others" "can also be used to justify unfair or abusive labor practices. Some workers may receive lower wages for unfair reasons, such as race or sex or immigrant status." Questions about pay discrimination of that kind are for a licensed attorney or the relevant government agency. If you are not being paid what you are owed, that is a question for someone qualified, not a study note. In the United States, your state labor agency or the Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division (toll-free 1-866-487-9243; TTY users can dial 7-1-1; Monday to Friday, 8:00 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. local time, with hours varying by region) can say whether a complaint falls within their rules, and a licensed attorney can advise on pay owed under an agreement. Elsewhere, contact your local labor authority. This bridge is a worked example for the learner to test, not advice for any particular person.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose.

1. The parable is framed by "first ... last" (19:30) and "last ... first" (20:16), yet in the story everyone receives the same. Chrysostom says the first "did not become last." In what sense, if any, are the first last in 20:1-15?
2. Only the first group has a stated wage; the third-hour group is promised "whatever is right" (20:4). What would the story lose if every group had agreed to a denarius?
3. "Are you envious" (BSB) and "Is your eye evil" (Greek) can sound very different. What does the literal phrase add, and what does it lose, beside Matthew 6:23 and Deuteronomy 15:9?
4. Rebbi Zeïra's king answers with the worker's output; Matthew's landowner answers with the agreement and his own will. What does each answer require the complaining workers to accept?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) list the five hirings in order with what each group was promised. (b) Say in what order the workers were paid, and what the first hired said. (c) Give the literal Greek sense of "are you envious because I am generous?"

2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** (0:00-1:00) the setting in 19:16-30 and the frame of 19:30 and 20:16; (1:00-2:00) the denarius, the hours, payment at evening and Deuteronomy 24:15; (2:00-3:00) the evil eye and the Byzantine addition to 20:16; (3:00-4:00) Positions 1 and the Jerusalem Talmud parable; (4:00-5:00) Positions 2, the misuse and its answers.

3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own and answer it. Form your own first. If you are stuck, here is one: "The landowner says he may 'do as I please with what is mine,' so an employer may pay whatever he likes." (Check: what did the landowner actually pay against what he agreed, and what do the Theology of Work Project and Levine (as Spinale reports) say?) A second runs another way: "The early workers stand for the Jewish people, so the parable is about their envy." (Check: what question immediately precedes the parable in 19:27-30, and what do Gregory, Calvin and *Nostra Aetate* say?)

4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) It does not say what the hours stand for; Jesus gives no interpretation, and readers differ (Positions 1). (b) It does not settle whether heavenly rewards differ in degree; the denarius is the same for all, and readers on both sides of Positions 2 hold that degrees exist. (c) It does not name any nation; the workers are "the original workers" and "this last man." (d) It does not set a rule for wages; the landowner pays the agreed sum and, to the late workers, more than their hours.

5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words in both texts:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matthew 19:30 | motif | strong | The same Greek saying, inverted, on both sides of the parable; 20:1's "For" joins them (Meyer, Expositor's Greek Testament) |
| Matthew 6:22-23 | motif | plausible | The same Greek *ho ophthalmos sou poneros*, hidden by the BSB; the sense of 6:23 is disputed (Thayer) |
| Deuteronomy 24:14-15 | allusion | plausible | Wages paid by sunset; Ellicott, Pulpit, JFB and Gill cite it on 20:8; Greek shares "give ... the wage," but Matthew names no source |

Deuteronomy 24:14-15 is this lesson's Old Testament thread; it is a legal background, not a quotation. Defend one row in your own words, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`. Three candidates were considered and not authored. (a) **Deuteronomy 15:9.** Barnes cites it on 20:15, and its Septuagint has "your eye be evil" toward a needy brother. But the evil eye is a common idiom, also in Proverbs 23:6 and 28:22 and in the rabbinic sayings Gill reports, so no one verse can be shown to be the source. (b) **Isaiah 5:1-7.** Ellicott, Barnes and the Pulpit Commentary cite the vineyard of Israel on 20:1, but the shared image is one word; Matthew's tenants parable (21:33), which also turns on a vineyard, belongs to its own lesson. (c) **Matthew 22:14.** "Many are called, but few are chosen" joins 20:16 only in the Byzantine and Textus Receptus texts, so the link is a question about manuscripts rather than about Matthew's design.
