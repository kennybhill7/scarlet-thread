Matthew 6:19-34 closes the Sermon on the Mount's treatment of practical righteousness that began at 6:1, moving from the three named pieces of piety the previous lesson in this curriculum covered -- giving, prayer, fasting (6:1-18) -- to a different, overlapping concern: where a disciple's own trust and attention actually rest when it comes to material things. The unit falls into two halves joined by a stated connective word: a short run on treasure, inner light, and divided loyalty (6:19-24), and a longer run on anxiety over food, drink, and clothing that opens with "Therefore" (6:25, BSB), tying what follows directly back to what came before. This passage carries some of the most widely remembered lines in the whole Sermon on the Mount -- the treasure-and-heart saying (6:21), the two-masters saying (6:24), the birds-and-lilies illustrations (6:26, 6:28-30) -- and that familiarity is exactly the hazard this lesson was built to guard against. Every quotation below was checked directly against this app's own shipped BSB file (`web/public/bible/BSB/40.json`) before a word of this lesson was drafted, and a second automated check re-verified every BSB-attributed quotation in this lesson and its connection files as a literal substring of the real verse text after drafting. That discipline matters here specifically because this unit's most familiar English wording in wide cultural memory often comes from the KJV (the eye "single," not "good"; "take no thought," not "do not worry") or from loose paraphrase, not from the BSB's own words -- and because 6:22-23 carries a genuine, still-debated scholarly question about what it actually claims, reported below as a named Positions dispute rather than resolved. The second half of the unit (6:25-34) carries a different risk: it is a frequent target of a popular reading that stretches "all these things will be added unto you" (6:33, BSB) into something closer to a guarantee of financial comfort than the text's own wording, in the text's own setting, actually states. This lesson tests that popular reading against the passage's own stated scope in a worked Practice Bridge Example below, rather than assuming without checking either that the promise covers everything a modern reader might want or that it promises nothing at all.

## Context

**Treasure, stored two ways (6:19-21).** "Do not store up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy, and where thieves break in and steal" (6:19, BSB) opens a contrast completed two verses later: "But store up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where moth and rust do not destroy, and where thieves do not break in and steal" (6:20, BSB). The unit closes with the line most readers already know: "For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (6:21, BSB). The Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:19 (used here) explains that "rust" renders a Greek word (brosis, literally "eating") that may describe oxidation of metal or, alternatively, vermin -- mice or worms -- destroying stored grain or wood, alongside moths destroying stored fabric, two of the period's most common stores of wealth; thieves name a third, broader category of loss covering any kind of asset. The same article frames the underlying logic as "a very rational and economic argument for piety, somewhat similar to Pascal's Wager," with heavenly treasure read there as the more secure investment because it "cannot be threatened by others." This lesson did not find a comparably substantive, named scholarly division over what "treasure in heaven" materially consists of -- an accumulating personal merit against a purely relational or doxological reward -- in any source consulted here; the Wikipedia article's own commentary draws mainly on patristic writers (Chrysostom, Augustine, Jerome) rather than a documented live dispute among named modern scholars, so this lesson treats 6:19-21 as Context rather than forcing it into a Positions entry it cannot support with real sourcing.

**The eye as the body's lamp (6:22-23), and a real translation fork.** "The eye is the lamp of the body. If your eyes are good, your whole body will be full of light" (6:22, BSB); "But if your eyes are bad, your whole body will be full of darkness. If then the light within you is darkness, how great is that darkness!" (6:23, BSB). Two features of this app's own BSB wording are worth stating plainly before any interpretation is attempted. First, the verse opens with the singular "The eye" and then shifts, within the same verse, to the plural "your eyes" -- a checkable feature of the BSB's own translation. Second, the BSB's "good" is a different English rendering of the underlying Greek word (haplous) than the KJV's better-known "single," which stands behind the familiar but non-BSB phrase "if thine eye be single." A reader carrying that KJV wording in memory will bring a different starting assumption to this verse than the BSB's own text actually states -- which is exactly why the quote-fidelity discipline named above matters here specifically, and exactly why this verse becomes this lesson's one Positions dispute, reported in full below.

**Two masters, and how the BSB renders the second one (6:24).** "No one can serve two masters: Either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve both God and money" (6:24, BSB). The BSB renders the underlying word as "money" directly, rather than retaining the transliterated "mammon" that the KJV, NASB, and NKJV keep untranslated. The Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:24 (used here) reports that mammon "was a standard term for money or possessions" in the period's literature and "generally not used pejoratively," stating plainly that "there is no evidence that a god by this name was ever worshipped" -- against a popular later tradition (reported there, not independently traced further here) that personified Mammon as a named demon of greed. Jerome's own comment, quoted in the same article, states: "Mammon -- riches are so termed in Syriac." Leon Morris, per the same article, notes that the underlying Greek verb behind "serve" (douleuein) "literally means 'be a slave to,'" and David Hill, also per the same article, observes that a first-century laborer could in principle work for two different employers, but outright slavery to two separate masters was not possible -- read there as the reason the verse reaches for the stronger word rather than a weaker one for ordinary paid work.

**Who is being addressed, and what the "do not worry" unit actually covers (6:25-34).** The sermon's own narrative frame states that Jesus "began to teach them" (5:2, BSB) -- "them" referring back to "His disciples," who had come to Him once He "saw the crowds" and "went up on the mountain" (5:1, BSB) -- while the crowds also hear the sermon and react once it ends: "the crowds were astonished at His teaching" (7:28, BSB). "Therefore I tell you, do not worry about your life, what you will eat or drink; or about your body, what you will wear. Is not life more than food, and the body more than clothes?" (6:25, BSB) opens the unit, naming three specific concerns -- food, drink, clothing -- that the rest of the unit keeps returning to by name. The birds illustration follows: "Look at the birds of the air: They do not sow or reap or gather into barns—and yet your heavenly Father feeds them. Are you not much more valuable than they?" (6:26, BSB). Named commentators read this verse carefully rather than as a simple call to idleness: Martin Luther, per the Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:26 (used here), observed that "God provides food, but does not drop it in their beaks"; William Hendriksen, per the same article, worked through whether birds truly receive provision without effort and concluded God provides by having "created a food-filled world" and given birds the instinct to gather it; Harold Fowler, also per the same article, reads the comparison as being about human capacity (planning, storing, farming) rather than a claim that birds do nothing; John Nolland, per the same article, challenges the verse's biological precision, noting some creatures do store food and others do starve; and Daniel Harrington, per the same article, observes that Luke's version of the same saying names ravens specifically, a detail examined further in the connection to Luke 12:22-32 below. "Who of you by worrying can add a single hour to his life?" (6:27, BSB) and the lilies illustration follow: "And why do you worry about clothes? Consider how the lilies of the field grow: They do not labor or spin. Yet I tell you that not even Solomon in all his glory was adorned like one of these. If that is how God clothes the grass of the field, which is here today and tomorrow is thrown into the furnace, will He not much more clothe you, O you of little faith?" (6:28-30, BSB). The unit restates its opening concern almost verbatim before its conclusion: "Therefore do not worry, saying, ‘What shall we eat?’ or ‘What shall we drink?’ or ‘What shall we wear?’ For the Gentiles strive after all these things, and your heavenly Father knows that you need them" (6:31-32, BSB). William Barclay, per the Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:31-32 (used here), reads the anxiety named here as "impious" insofar as it represents doubt in God's care; Leon Morris, per the same article, draws a scope-defining distinction directly relevant to the Practice Bridge Example below: the passage "refers to God meeting the needs of his followers, not their desires." Chrysostom, per the same article, reads the relational address ("your Father," not merely "God") as the point: "He said not 'God knoweth,' but, Your Father knoweth, in order to lead them to higher hope." Pseudo-Chrysostom, also per the same article, reads Gentile anxiety there as a consequence of trusting "Fortune" rather than "Providence." The unit's conclusion follows: "But seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things will be added unto you" (6:33, BSB) -- the BSB's own exact wording says "the kingdom of God," not "His kingdom," a detail worth holding onto precisely since Luke's own shorter parallel (examined in the connection below) does say "His kingdom." A closing coda returns to the unit's refrain word one final time: "Therefore do not worry about tomorrow, for tomorrow will worry about itself. Today has enough trouble of its own" (6:34, BSB). Ulrich Luz, per the Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:34 (used here), contrasts an optimistic carpe-diem reading of this verse against his own preferred, more sober reading: "the evil of each individual day is so great and so overbearing that it is hard enough to get through one day, much less worry about those coming." Harold Fowler, per the same article, reads the verse as warning against presuming on a day "not yet granted," and the same article reports a close Rabbinic parallel, dayyah la-tzarah be-sha'atah, "the trouble of the hour is sufficient for it" -- stated there as evidence this teaching reflects a broader Jewish wisdom tradition rather than a unique Christian innovation.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 6:19-34 is built, not on what that construction is meant to teach.

**One stated connective ties the two halves together.** "Therefore" opens 6:25 (BSB), a direct grammatical link back to everything just stated in 6:19-24 -- the treasure contrast, the eye/lamp saying, and the two-masters saying. Whatever 6:25-34 is building toward, it is built, by the text's own word choice, as a consequence drawn from 6:19-24, not as an unrelated new topic simply placed next to it.

**A single refrain word structures the second half.** "Worry" (rendering the underlying Greek merimnao in this app's own BSB) recurs five times across ten verses: "do not worry about your life" (6:25), "by worrying" (6:27), "why do you worry about clothes" (6:28), "do not worry, saying" (6:31), and twice more in the closing verse, "do not worry about tomorrow, for tomorrow will worry about itself" (6:34). This is the same kind of checkable, word-for-word repetition the previous lesson in this curriculum found structuring 6:1-18's giving/prayer/fasting unit through its own two verbatim refrains; here, one word rather than one full clause carries the structural weight.

**The eye/lamp saying sits between two money-focused sayings, a fact about placement rather than about meaning.** 6:22-23 follows directly after the treasure contrast (6:19-21) and directly precedes the two-masters saying (6:24) -- a checkable fact about where Matthew places the verse, regardless of what that placement is taken to establish about the verse's own content. Positions below reports that this neighboring placement is itself part of the argument some readers make for reading 6:22-23 as continuous with the money material on either side of it, rather than as a free-standing statement about perception in general; this lesson states the placement as a textual fact and leaves what weight to give it to the named dispute below.

**Three illustrations answer the three named worries in order, before a final coda returns to one word alone.** The birds illustration (6:26) answers the "what will we eat or drink" half of 6:25's opening; the lilies illustration (6:28-30) answers the clothing half; the Gentiles-and-kingdom material (6:31-33) restates all three concerns together before resolving them into the single positive instruction of 6:33. The closing verse (6:34) then narrows the unit's own refrain word down to a single new object -- not food, drink, or clothing again, but "tomorrow" -- a checkable shift in what the repeated word is attached to from one occurrence to the next.

## Positions

One real, named dispute attaches to this passage with comparably substantive sourcing on more than one side; this lesson reports it without resolving it.

**What does "the eye is the lamp of the body... if your eyes are good... if your eyes are bad" (6:22-23) actually claim?** Three distinct, named readings are reported here in their own terms.

One reading holds that the verse states an ancient generosity/stinginess idiom, tied directly to its money-focused neighbors on either side. Dale C. Allison Jr., in a 1987 article in *New Testament Studies* ("The Eye is the Lamp of the Body," reported here through Francois Viljoen's 2009 article in *HTS Theological Studies*, which engages Allison's argument directly -- Allison's own article was not independently read for this lesson, flagged further in Sources and Limits), argues that the verse's meaning "can only be understood when read according to the ancient understanding of vision," under which the eye was thought to emit rays that acted on whatever they fell upon. Viljoen extends this into the specific ancient Jewish and Near Eastern idiom of the "good eye" and "evil eye": "The attributes most commonly associated with the evil eye are selfishness, envy, greed and the refusal to share one's means with others in need." On this reading, haplous ("good," BSB) names a generous eye and ponēros ("bad," BSB) names a stingy, envious one -- reading 6:22-23 as continuous with the treasure teaching just before it (6:19-21) and the "you cannot serve both God and money" saying just after it (6:24), rather than as a statement about perception generally. The Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:22 (used here) independently confirms the idiom itself: "This wording links this verse to the idea of the evil eye, which was often termed the 'ungenerous eye.'"

A second reading holds that the verse states a claim about single-minded moral focus rather than, or in addition to, financial generosity. The same Wikipedia article reports that in the Septuagint, haplous "translates [the] Hebrew for 'singleness of heart' or 'singleness of purpose'" -- an exclusive focus on God rather than a focus split between God and worldly concern. Harold Fowler, per the same article, reads "eye" as naming "the conscience and moral vision of the individual, both of which serve as guiding lights" -- a claim about the whole person's inward orientation, not narrowly about money. David Hill, also per the same article, adds a caution relevant to how far this second reading can itself be pushed: some scholars connect the verse's light/darkness language to the soul, but Hill notes this "was not a standard metaphor for the soul in Jewish literature of the period" -- a caution against assuming too quickly that 6:22-23 is making a general claim about inward spiritual illumination as such.

A third, mediating position treats the saying's ambiguity as likely deliberate rather than as a choice to be resolved either way. R.T. France and Fowler, per the same Wikipedia article, "speculate that this ambiguity is deliberate, and that the verse is speaking about both generosity and single mindedness, as both ideas are discussed in this part of Jesus' sermon."

This dispute is not simply a difference in translators' word choice, though translation choice is where it becomes visible to an English reader. The KJV's well-known "if thine eye be single" renders haplous as "single," sitting closer to the second reading above; the BSB's "if your eyes are good" renders the same word in a way more compatible with the first reading. A reader who already carries the KJV's wording in memory brings a different starting assumption to this verse than the BSB's own text actually states.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage states in its own setting to a concrete modern situation. This is a model of the shape, not an answer key -- the learner's own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 6:31-33: "Therefore do not worry, saying, ‘What shall we eat?’ or ‘What shall we drink?’ or ‘What shall we wear?’ For the Gentiles strive after all these things, and your heavenly Father knows that you need them. But seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things will be added unto you" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the three named worries are food, drink, and clothing -- survival-level necessities, not investment, savings, or any named form of surplus wealth -- and "all these things" in both 6:32 and 6:33 refers back grammatically to that same specific, named list, not to an open-ended category of material want. Jesus's own direct address in this sermon is to "His disciples" specifically (5:1-2, BSB: He "began to teach them"), even though "the crowds" also hear the teaching and react once it ends (7:28, BSB). Inference: for a first-century disciple who had left ordinary paid work to follow an itinerant teacher -- the same kind of economically dependent situation Jesus later describes for the Twelve, instructing them to "Take no bag for the road, or second tunic, or sandals, or staff" (10:10, BSB) -- a command not to worry about food, drink, and clothing addressed a real, present, basic-survival anxiety rather than a hypothetical one. This connects 6:25-34's audience to Matthew 10:9-10's description of discipleship's actual economic terms a few chapters later in the same Gospel; it is this lesson's own step beyond what 6:19-34 states about its audience in so many words, and it draws on a different scene (the Twelve being sent out in chapter 10) than the broader crowd-plus-disciples setting of the Sermon on the Mount itself, so the connection is suggestive rather than a proven identity of situation.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** Anxious preoccupation with whether one's basic needs will be met is not the posture this unit calls for in a disciple already committed to seeking God's kingdom first; the promise stated at 6:33 is scoped to those named basic needs, not to every material want or ambition a modern reader might bring to the verse.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the reordering of priority (seek the kingdom and its righteousness first; let provision for basic needs be a secondary trust rather than a primary anxiety) and the explicit scope of what is promised (food, drink, clothing -- not investment returns, career advancement, or blanket freedom from financial hardship). Not carried forward: any claim that every person who "seeks first the kingdom" is thereby shielded from hunger, poverty, or financial loss in this life -- the text itself states no timeline, no mechanism, and no claim of universal, individual, present-life guarantee, a point developed further in Cautions below.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: a repeated direct instruction ("do not worry," at 6:25, 6:28, 6:31, 6:34) paired with a stated reason ("your heavenly Father knows that you need them," 6:32) and a conditional promise ("seek first... and all these things will be added," 6:33) -- not an unconditional guarantee detached from the seeking it is conditioned on. Promise scope: 6:33 states that "these things" -- food, drink, and clothing, the specific list named at 6:31-32 -- will be added to those who seek God's kingdom and righteousness first; it does not state an amount, a timetable, or a guarantee against hunger, poverty, illness, or financial loss in this life for any individual case. The Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:33 (used here) reports Augustine's own caution against reading this promise as unconditional in every individual's experience: "When we read that the Apostle suffered hunger and thirst, let us not think that God's promises failed him; for these things are rather aids," since God, as a physician, "knows when He will give and when He will withhold, as He judges most for our advantage." The Wikipedia article on Matthew 6:31-32 (used here) separately reports Leon Morris's related distinction already quoted in Context above: the passage "refers to God meeting the needs of his followers, not their desires" -- a scope limit on what "these things" names, stated by a named commentator and checkable directly against the verse's own grammar.

**Modern domain and situation.** Personal financial stewardship during a season of real income instability. A believer who has recently left a stable salaried job for full-time ministry or missionary work finds themselves, four months in, short on rent with no clear next paycheck date, and is told by a well-meaning friend that worrying is itself a sin and that enough faith will produce the money.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: distinguishing trust in God's provision from a passive refusal to plan, communicate need, or work -- not treating the mere suppression of anxious feeling as the substance of what this unit asks. One faithful response: the believer continues seeking practical, ordinary means consistent with their situation -- communicating the shortfall to a sending organization or church, seeking available work, budgeting carefully -- while refusing to let the shortfall become a controlling, defining preoccupation, trusting that basic provision is the kind of thing 6:33 actually names, without treating a difficult season as proof either that their own faith has failed or that more "faith" alone will resolve it.

**Cautions.** This does not mean financial hardship among genuine disciples is always a sign of insufficient faith, and it does not mean a difficult season should be read as evidence a divine promise has failed -- both readings claim more certainty about the mechanism of God's provision than 6:33's own wording states, and both have caused real harm to people in genuine financial distress who were told their suffering was their own spiritual failure. It also does not mean the passage promises nothing at all, or that ordinary prudence -- saving, budgeting, planning -- is somehow in tension with it; the unit addresses anxious preoccupation, not reasonable preparation, and Jesus's own instruction to the Twelve elsewhere in this Gospel assumes real material support will still be needed and received through ordinary means, not through suspending practical action altogether. A reader facing a genuine, severe financial crisis -- eviction, inability to feed a family, overwhelming debt -- needs a pastor, a financial counselor, or a qualified caseworker, not a study note; this bridge is a worked example for the learner to test against their own situation, not a universal financial plan.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. Allison and Viljoen read 6:22-23's "good eye"/"bad eye" through an ancient generosity/stinginess idiom tied to its money-focused neighbors; France and Fowler read the same ambiguity as deliberately holding a generosity reading and a single-mindedness reading together at once. What would you need to check about how haplous and its opposite are used elsewhere in the New Testament and in roughly contemporary non-biblical Greek, to judge whether the verse's own immediate neighbors (6:19-21, 6:24) should be allowed to settle its meaning, or whether that imports an idiom from nearby verses into a saying that may have circulated independently in an earlier form?
2. The BSB renders Matthew 6:24's key word as "money," while the KJV, NASB, and NKJV keep the transliterated "mammon" untranslated. What would you need to know about why a translation team chooses to translate a word rather than transliterate it, to judge whether "money" or "mammon" better serves a reader meeting this verse for the first time with no inherited assumption about what "mammon" already means?
3. Augustine's caution and Leon Morris's "needs not desires" distinction, both quoted in the Practice Bridge Example above, limit what 6:33's promise claims, but neither states exactly where the line falls between a genuine need the text promises to meet and a want the text does not address. What would you need to see -- about the rest of Scripture's teaching on material provision, or about how the earliest readers of this Gospel actually understood and lived out this promise -- to draw that line with more confidence than this lesson's own Practice Bridge Example draws it?
4. Matthew places the "two masters" saying (6:24) and the "do not worry" unit (6:25-34) back to back, joined by "Therefore" (6:25). What would you need to establish about how tightly Matthew intends these two units to be read together, as opposed to two originally separate sayings Matthew has simply placed in sequence, before concluding that 6:25-34's own teaching on anxiety is specifically a teaching about money and possessions, rather than a broader teaching about anxiety of any kind that happens to follow a saying about money in the text as it now stands?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) state in order what 6:19-34 says, unit by unit, before saying anything about what it means for modern practice: the treasure contrast (19-21), the eye/lamp saying (22-23), the two-masters saying (24), and the "do not worry" unit built around three named concerns -- food, drink, clothing (25-34). (b) Name the one word that recurs as a refrain through 6:25-34 ("worry," at 6:25, 6:27, 6:28, 6:31, and twice at 6:34) and state what each occurrence is attached to. (c) Recite 6:33 in the BSB's own exact wording -- "seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things will be added unto you" -- without substituting "His kingdom" from memory of a more familiar paraphrase; the BSB's own text says "the kingdom of God" at this specific verse (compare Luke's shorter parallel, which does say "His kingdom," in the connection to Luke 12:22-32 below).
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the treasure contrast and its placement next to the eye/lamp saying (see Literary Design); (1:00-2:00) the eye/lamp dispute itself, naming at least two of the three readings in their own terms (see Positions); (2:00-2:30) the "two masters" saying and the BSB's "money" rendering of mammon (see Context); (2:30-4:00) the "do not worry" unit's own three illustrations and what 6:33's promise is actually scoped to, including at least one named caution against over-reading it (see Practice Bridge Example); (4:00-5:00) one open question from above, stated on purpose as the last thing taught.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first; the point of this prompt is that you do the forming, not that you pick from a list. If you are stuck, here is one worked candidate, stated at full strength: limiting 6:33's promise to "food, drink, and clothing" the way this lesson's Practice Bridge Example does is itself an unjustified narrowing -- the verse says "all these things," broad enough to cover whatever material need a modern reader actually has, and a study note that scopes the promise down to bare survival is quietly teaching that God's care for His people is thinner than the text itself states. Before you answer it, notice what the Practice Bridge Example actually does: it grounds the scope claim in the verse's own grammar (6:32's and 6:33's "these things" both refer back to the specific list named at 6:31 -- food, drink, clothing -- not to an undefined category), not in an assumption imported from outside the text, and it cites Augustine's and Morris's own stated cautions rather than asserting the limit as this lesson's own unsupported judgment. A second worked candidate, running the other direction: if Augustine is right that God "knows when He will give and when He will withhold," does that caution risk doing the opposite work -- letting every unanswered material need be waved away as God's own wise withholding, in a way that makes 6:33's promise unfalsifiable and therefore not really a promise at all?
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 6:19-24 does not, in its own wording, specify a dollar amount, a percentage of income, or any other measurable threshold separating ordinary stewardship of money from the divided loyalty the "two masters" saying warns against -- the text names a stark either/or about ultimate allegiance, not a quantified boundary a reader can apply mechanically to their own finances. (b) Matthew 6:25-34 does not, in its own wording, state that financial hardship, hunger, or material loss experienced by a genuine disciple is evidence of insufficient faith or a broken promise -- the unit instructs against anxious preoccupation and states a scoped promise about named basic needs; it does not state a universal, guaranteed, present-life outcome for every individual case, a point Positions and the Practice Bridge Example above both develop at length.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection from this passage and defend it: name its type, its evidence label, and point to the words in both texts that support that grade.
