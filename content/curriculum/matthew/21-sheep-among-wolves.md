Matthew 10:16-42 is the second half of the speech Jesus gives the Twelve when he sends them out. The first half (10:1-15, the previous lesson) tells them where to go and how to travel. This half tells them what will happen to them: "Behold, I am sending you out like sheep among wolves" (10:16, BSB). It moves from courts and floggings (10:17-23), to the reason the disciples should not be afraid (10:24-33), to the hardest words in the speech, "I have not come to bring peace, but a sword" (10:34, BSB), and from there to family, the cross, and finally a cup of cold water (10:35-42). This lesson does three things. It checks, clause by clause, a quotation of Micah 7:6 that Matthew never introduces as a quotation. It reports what named commentators say about three hard sayings, without choosing among them. And because sayings about turning family members against each other, and about hell, can land hard on a reader's own life, it says plainly where a reader should go for help.

## Context

**What happens, in order.** Jesus warns that "they will hand you over to their councils and flog you in their synagogues" (10:17, BSB) and that the disciples will stand "before governors and kings as witnesses to them and to the Gentiles" (10:18, BSB). They are not to worry about what to say: "it will not be you speaking, but the Spirit of your Father speaking through you" (10:20, BSB). Families will split: "Brother will betray brother to death, and a father his child" (10:21, BSB). "When they persecute you in one town, flee to the next" (10:23, BSB). Then comes a run of reasons not to fear: "A disciple is not above his teacher" (10:24); "nothing concealed that will not be disclosed" (10:26); "Do not be afraid of those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul" (10:28); the sparrows and the numbered hairs (10:29-31); and the promise and warning about confessing or denying Jesus "before men" (10:32-33). The sword saying follows (10:34-36), then the conditions of being "worthy of Me" (10:37-38), the saying about losing and finding one's life (10:39), and the closing sayings about receiving (10:40-42). The next verse marks the end of the speech: "After Jesus had finished instructing His twelve disciples, He went on from there" (11:1, BSB).

**Much of 10:17-22 reappears in Mark 13.** Checked in this app's BSB, Matthew 10:21 and Mark 13:12 match almost word for word ("Brother will betray brother to death, and a father his child"), and Matthew 10:22 and Mark 13:13 are identical: "You will be hated by everyone because of My name, but the one who perseveres to the end will be saved." In Mark these words sit in Jesus' speech about the end of the age, not in the sending of the Twelve. Matthew repeats the last line in his own end-time speech: "But the one who perseveres to the end will be saved" (24:13, BSB). Note that the BSB says "perseveres," not "endures" as many older English versions do. What "to the end" refers to is read two ways on Biblehub's commentary page for 10:22: Barnes reads it as "to the end of life, be it longer or shorter," while Meyer, citing Theophylact, Beza and Fritzsche, glosses it in Latin as "usque ad finem horum malorum" (to the end of these evils). Whether the verse bears on the doctrine of perseverance is not taken up here. Lesson 15 in this curriculum (Matthew 7:13-29) reports four traditions on that doctrine.

**The sparrows and the hairs (10:29-31).** "Are not two sparrows sold for a penny? Yet not one of them will fall to the ground apart from the will of your Father" (10:29, BSB). Lesson 13 used the birds of 6:26 for a lesson about provision ("your heavenly Father feeds them"). Here the setting is different. The birds come right after a warning about people who can kill, and the point drawn is "So do not be afraid; you are worth more than many sparrows" (10:31, BSB). The BSB's "apart from the will of your Father" includes the word "will." The King James wording, printed at the head of Biblehub's commentary page, is "without your Father," and Bengel records "without the will of your Father" as a reading found in Irenaeus, Tertullian, Augustine and several early versions. Luke's form of the saying has "five sparrows sold for two pennies" (Luke 12:6, BSB). The Pulpit Commentary and Jamieson-Fausset-Brown both suggest that the buyer of two pennies' worth got a fifth bird thrown in. In this app's BSB, "sparrow" occurs in Matthew only at 10:29 and 10:31 (checked by script).

**"Hell" in 10:28 translates Gehenna.** The BSB reads, "fear the One who can destroy both soul and body in hell" (10:28). The Cambridge Bible notes, "Literally, in Gehenna." Denny Burk, in an essay for The Gospel Coalition (2020), explains that the Greek *geenna* "derives from the Hebrew gê hinnōm, which means 'Valley of Hinnom'," a place "best known as the site of child sacrifices" in the time of the kings, and he adds that "there is no evidence that the Valley of Hinnom was ever used as a garbage dump." That last point is his, and this lesson did not check it further. "Hell" occurs seven times in this app's BSB Matthew (5:22, 5:29, 5:30, 10:28, 18:9, 23:15, 23:33; checked by script). What "destroy" means here is a named dispute, reported in Positions below.

**Who is "the One" in 10:28?** Most of the commentators read for this lesson say God. Meyer writes, "It is God that is meant, and not the devil," and names Olshausen and Stier as holding the other view. The Pulpit Commentary and Bengel point to James 4:12, where God is "the One who is able to save and destroy" (BSB). The commentary on Matthew in the Expositor's Greek Testament disagrees: "Who is that? God, say most commentators. Not so, I believe," and asks, "Would Christ present God under this aspect in such close connection with the Father who cares even for the sparrows?" Its own answer is "fear not the persecutor, but the tempter, not the man who kills you for your fidelity, but the man who wants to buy you off, and the devil whose agent he is." Ellicott reports that "Not a few of the most devout and thoughtful commentators" have read it this way, and he rejects the reading, noting that "We are nowhere taught in Scripture to fear the devil." The Cambridge Bible lists both readings without choosing.

**The household clauses quote Micah 7:6.** Matthew never says "as it is written" here, but the BSB itself prints 10:35-36 inside quotation marks. Set side by side in this app's BSB:

- Micah 7:6: "For a son dishonors his father, a daughter rises against her mother, and a daughter-in-law against her mother-in-law. A man’s enemies are the members of his own household."
- Matthew 10:35-36: "For I have come to turn ‘a man against his father, a daughter against her mother, a daughter-in-law against her mother-in-law. A man’s enemies will be the members of his own household.’"

One clause is identical ("a daughter-in-law against her mother-in-law"), and two more match in all their key words ("a daughter... against her mother"; "A man’s enemies... the members of his own household"), with Micah's "rises" and "are" against Matthew's bare "against" and "will be." The first clause differs: Micah's son "dishonors his father," while Matthew has "a man against his father." Matthew also turns Micah's description into a first-person saying of Jesus ("I have come to turn"). The Greek tells the same story. In the Septuagint as printed on Biblehub, the daughter rises *epi* (against) her mother, while Matthew's Greek uses *kata* in each pair and has the rare verb *dichasai*, "to set at variance" (which the Cambridge Bible says "occurs here only in the New Testament"). The Pulpit Commentary's judgment is that "The wording is hardly taken from the LXX." The commentators describe the borrowing with different words. Barnes says "This is taken from Micah 7:6," Jamieson-Fausset-Brown says the saying "is quoted, as is the whole verse, from Mic 7:6," the Expositor's Greek Testament says the passage "reproduces freely Micah 7:6," Ellicott calls the words "partly... an echo of Micah 7:6," and Meyer calls it an "Involuntary recollection of Micah 7:6." Those differences are part of what the connection below has to weigh.

**Micah 7:6 in its own setting.** In Micah the verse is part of a lament over a society where trust has broken down. The verse before it says, "Do not rely on a friend; do not trust in a companion" (7:5, BSB), and the verse after it turns: "But as for me, I will look to the LORD; I will wait for the God of my salvation" (7:7, BSB). The household lines were later read as a sign of the last days. The Mishnah (Sotah 9:15, read on Sefaria) places them under "the times of the approach of the Messiah": "A son will disgrace a father; a daughter will rise up against her mother, a daughter-in-law against her mother-in-law. A man’s enemies will be the members of his household." David Sim (*HTS Teologiese Studies*, 2000) writes that the division within families "as attested by Mic 7:6 was seen in both Jewish and Christian circles as a sign of the end-time woes," citing 1 Enoch, Jubilees, 4 Ezra, Mark 13:12 and Matthew 10:21. That is a fact about how the verse was read. It does not settle what Matthew intended.

**What named commentators say the "sword" (10:34) means.** A saying about bringing a sword could be misread as a call to violence. This lesson did not read a source that documents particular cases of that, so it names none. What follows is what the sources that were read say the word means. R. T. France, as quoted in Wikipedia's article on the verse (a tertiary source), says, "The sword Jesus brings is not here military conflict, but, as vv. 35–36 show, a sharp social division which even severs the closest family ties." Sim writes that "The sword in Matthew 10:34 is not meant literally," that "Matthew's Jesus is no zealot or revolutionary," and that in light of 5:9, 5:38-48 and 26:52 "it is impossible to believe that the sword he brings in 10:34 symbolises the call to armed conflict against the oppressors of Israel." He reports Davies and Allison's reading: "As the sword splits in half, so does Jesus divide families," and that the sword stands for the trials of the end-time ("The absence of peace and the presence of the sword is a sign of the great tribulation"). Sim's own proposal goes further. Drawing on the Qumran War Scroll and Revelation, he suggests the sword may also stand for "the defeat of the enemies of God" in a final battle and for "the eternal punishment of the wicked," though he calls his reading "more suggestive than definitive." Among older commentators, Gill suggests that "By the 'sword' may be meant the Gospel, which is the means of dividing," noting that Luke has "division" instead. The Cambridge Bible says, "The contrast is rather between union and division than between peace and war." Bengel glosses it as "violent division," "proceeding from the discord of families, mentioned in Matthew 10:35, to wars and murders." Chrysostom, in the Catena Aurea, says that "this combat is not of His setting before them, but of the plots of the world." No source read for this lesson reads the verse as a command to take up arms. Matthew's own text has Jesus say, at his arrest, "all who draw the sword will die by the sword" (26:52, BSB).

**The first "cross" in Matthew.** "Anyone who does not take up his cross and follow Me is not worthy of Me" (10:38, BSB). A search of this app's BSB finds the noun "cross" first here. The word at 8:18 is the verb ("to cross to the other side"). The noun comes next at 16:24, just after the first announcement that Jesus "must be killed" (16:21, BSB), and then in the passion account (27:32, 27:40, 27:42). Commentators disagree about what hearers would have heard at 10:38. Jamieson-Fausset-Brown calls the saying "the more remarkable as our Lord had not as yet given a hint that He would die this death." The Expositor's Greek Testament says, "There is here no necessary allusion to the death of Jesus Himself by crucifixion," though it adds that Jesus "must have known quite well when He uttered the words what awaited Himself." Meyer reads the phrasing as a sign that the passage was placed here later. The Cambridge Bible notes that Galileans "would know too well what was meant by 'taking the cross'" from Roman executions of rebels.

**Receiving (10:40-42).** The speech ends with a chain: "He who receives you receives Me, and he who receives Me receives the One who sent Me" (10:40, BSB), and even "a cup of cold water to one of these little ones because he is My disciple" will not go unrewarded (10:42, BSB). The previous lesson, on 10:1-15, takes up receiving and rejecting the messengers. This lesson notes only that the speech closes on welcome, after many verses on rejection.

## Literary Design

Notes on how 10:16-42 is built, not on what the arrangement is meant to teach.

**"Do not be afraid," three times.** The middle section is held together by a repeated command: "So do not be afraid of them" (10:26), "Do not be afraid of those who kill the body" (10:28), and "So do not be afraid" (10:31). Each is paired with a reason: hidden things will come out, people can only kill the body, and the Father counts even the hairs. Between the second and third comes the one thing the passage does say to fear (10:28b).

**A ladder of "not worthy of Me."** The phrase "is not worthy of Me" ends three clauses in a row (10:37-38): the one who loves father or mother more, the one who loves son or daughter more, and the one who does not take up his cross. Bengel calls it "An ascending climax: to prefer Christ to parents, children, and, in the next verse, himself." Earlier in the chapter "worthy" is said of the worker (10:10), of the host in a town (10:11) and of a home (10:13, all BSB). Here it is turned toward the disciple.

**Rejection, then reception.** The passage begins with being handed over (10:17) and ends with being received (10:40-42). Between them, "before men" frames a pair: confessing (10:32) and denying (10:33). This is a checkable feature of the order. What Matthew means by it is not stated.

## Positions

Three named disputes attach to this passage. The first is short. The second and third reach into readers' lives, and each ends with a pastoral note.

**Is the division in 10:34-35 Jesus' purpose, or only its result?** "I have come to turn" (10:35, BSB) reads like a statement of purpose. Barnes, Benson, Gill and Poole all read it as a result, not an aim. Barnes writes, "Christ did not here mean to say that the object of his coming was to produce discord and contention... but he means to say that such would be one of the effects of his coming." Benson writes, "this is not the design, though it be the event of his coming." The Pulpit Commentary says, "The result is not in any sense the final cause of his coming." Meyer, writing on the Greek, disagrees in part: "what Jesus expresses is a purpose,—not the final design of His coming, but an intermediate purpose." Ellicott holds the two together: the conflict was a consequence "freely accepted for the sake of the greater good that lies beyond it," and so "involves, in fact, a purpose." This lesson reports the range and does not choose.

**What does "more than Me" (10:37) require, and how does it relate to Luke's "hate" (Luke 14:26)?** Matthew has "Anyone who loves his father or mother more than Me is not worthy of Me" (10:37, BSB). Luke's parallel says, "If anyone comes to Me and does not hate his father and mother and wife and children and brothers and sisters—yes, even his own life—he cannot be My disciple" (Luke 14:26, BSB). The difference is real, and named readers explain it in at least three ways.

*A comparison: "hate" means "love less."* On this reading Luke's "hate" is a way of ranking two loves, and Matthew's "more than Me" states the same thing plainly. The Cambridge Bible names Genesis 29:31 as the usual support for this reading (and then qualifies it, below). In Genesis 29, Jacob "loved Rachel more than Leah" (29:30, BSB), and the next verse calls Leah "unloved" (29:31, BSB). Biblehub's interlinear glosses the Hebrew word behind "unloved" as "hated," and the same participle stands behind "unloved" in Deuteronomy 21:15. Gill on Luke 14:26 writes, "not that proper hatred of any, or all of these, is enjoined by Christ... but that these are not to be preferred to Christ, or loved more than he, as it is explained in Matthew 10:37." He goes on at once: "yea, these are to be neglected and forsaken, and turned from with indignation and resentment, when they stand in the way of the honour and interest of Christ, and dissuade from his service." Poole says of Luke's wording, "the sense is the same, for by hatred there is only meant displacency, and a setting them in his esteem below Christ." The Geneva Study Bible notes that the words "are spoken in a comparative way." Jerome, in the Catena Aurea, quotes the Song of Songs, "Order love in me," and adds, "He forbids not to love parent or child, but adds emphatically, 'more than me'."

*A conditional reading: when family stands in the way.* The Cambridge Bible's note on Luke 14:26 cites Genesis 29:31 and then says, "It is not so much the true explanation to say that hate here means love less... as to say that when our nearest and dearest relationships prove to be positive obstacles in coming to Christ, then all natural affections must be flung aside." For that reading it points to Deuteronomy 13:6-9, 21:19-21 and 33:8-9. Jamieson-Fausset-Brown also cites Deuteronomy 33:9 on Matthew 10:37, where Levi "said of his father and mother, ‘I do not consider them’" because "he kept Your word" (BSB). A sentence later the Cambridge note adds that "A reference to Matthew 10:37 will shew that 'hate' means hate by comparison." Ellicott, on Matthew 10:37, reads the stronger form this way: "though the man may not and ought not to cease to love, yet he must act as if he hated—disobey, and, it may be, desert—those to whom he is bound by natural ties, that he may obey the higher supernatural calling." Jerome's comment, quoted above, also has this side: "if a necessity should occur that the love of parents and children comes into competition with the love of God, and where both cannot be preserved, remember that hatred of our kindred becomes then love to God."

*A stronger reading: more than comparison.* Bengel, on Luke 14:26, says the hatred "must be understood not merely in the comparative [hate, i.e. love less] or conditional and qualified sense, but even absolutely." He limits what is to be hated to "whereinsoever father, etc., or self are inconsistent with love to Christ," and he describes this hatred as "at once high-spirited and yet at the same time removed from all bitterness of feeling."

These readings differ on how strong Luke's word is and on whether Matthew's "more than Me" explains it or softens it. They agree that love for Jesus comes first. None of them presents hostility toward family as an end in itself, though Gill speaks of turning from relatives "with indignation and resentment" where they stand in the way, and Bengel of "a contempt and hatred of self and of the whole creature" so far as it "is subject to vanity." Gill, on Matthew 10:37, writes that "The design of these words, is not at all to lessen the due affection of children to their parents." Meyer writes, "The inalienable rights of family affection remain intact, but in subordination to the love of Christ." Elsewhere in Matthew, Jesus quotes "Honor your father and mother" (15:4; 19:19, BSB) and condemns a practice that let a man use a religious gift to avoid supporting his parents (15:5-6). This lesson does not decide among the readings.

*A pastoral note.* Families do break over faith, and some readers of this lesson live with that. Some have been cut off by their families for following Jesus. Others are being pressed by a church, a leader or a group to cut off their own families, and are being told that verses like these require it. In the readings reported above, the hard choice arises where family ties and following Jesus come into conflict, and it is the disciple's own choice. None of these commentators describes a leader or a group requiring someone to cut off family. Whichever situation is yours, a study note is not the place to work it out. Please talk with a pastor you trust, ideally one outside any group involved, or with a licensed counselor. The National Domestic Violence Hotline lists "Isolating you from family, friends, or other people in your life because it makes someone easier to control" among the signs of an abusive relationship. If that pressure comes from a partner or someone at home, the Hotline can help (in the United States, call 1-800-799-7233 or text START to 88788). If it comes from a church or other group, the International Cultic Studies Association (internationalculticstudies.org) offers support for people affected by "high-control groups and environments," and for their families. If anyone is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number.

**What does "destroy both soul and body in hell" (10:28) mean for the final fate of the lost?** Christians who all accept hell as a real judgment disagree over whether it ends in the destruction of the person or in conscious punishment without end. This verse is often central to that dispute, because of the word "destroy" (Greek *apolesai*). Two positions are reported here, each from its own advocates' words.

*Final destruction (annihilationism and conditional immortality, which Stott distinguishes).* John Stott, in *Essentials* (1988, published in the United States as *Evangelical Essentials*), discusses this verse directly. He notes that when the verb is active, "'destroy' means 'kill'," and argues: "If to kill is to deprive the body of life, hell would seem to be the deprivation of both physical and spiritual life, that is, an extinction of being." He adds that the soul's natural immortality, and so its indestructibility, "is a Greek not a biblical concept." He calls eternal conscious torment "traditional orthodoxy, for most of the church fathers, the medieval theologians and the Reformers held it," and he ends: "I do not dogmatise about the position to which I have come. I hold it tentatively. But I do plead for frank dialogue among Evangelicals on the basis of Scripture." He asks that the annihilation of the wicked "should at least be accepted as a legitimate, biblically founded alternative." Edward Fudge, author of *The Fire That Consumes*, said in a 2002 interview with *Modern Reformation*: "Jesus warned that God is able to destroy both soul and body in hell (Matt. 10:28). The clear implication is that he intends to do just that." For Fudge, "eternal punishment" means being "punished" with "eternal destruction" (2 Thessalonians 1:9). He calls his position "a minority view, which contradicts several ecclesiastical creeds," and says that this "should not matter, since we all agree that truth is learned from Scripture and not from polls or human creeds."

*Eternal conscious punishment.* The Westminster Confession of Faith (33.2) states that "the wicked who know not God, and obey not the gospel of Jesus Christ, shall be cast into eternal torments, and be punished with everlasting destruction from the presence of the Lord, and from the glory of his power." The Confession uses "destruction" too, and places it alongside "eternal torments." Alan Gomes, writing in the *Christian Research Journal* (1991), answers Stott's argument about *apollumi* and *apoleia* by quoting Charles Hodge: "To destroy is to ruin. The nature of that ruin depends on the nature of the subject of which it is predicated." He adds Roger Nicole's example of a car "wrecked" or "totalled" even when its parts are not "melted or scattered away." Denny Burk (The Gospel Coalition, 2020) lists Matthew 10:28 among Jesus' sayings on hell and argues that the related Pauline word "destruction" means "ruin or loss, not annihilation." Among older commentators on Biblehub, Gill reads "destroy" as "to torment and punish both body and soul... for neither soul nor body will be annihilated; though this he is able to do," and Jamieson-Fausset-Brown calls the verse "A decisive proof this that there is a hell for the body as well as the soul," adding that both, "we are assured, will exist for ever."

One lexical note, quoted by the Pulpit Commentary from Joseph Agar Beet, applies to both sides: words of this class denote "utter and hopeless ruin; but they convey no idea whether the ruined object ceases to exist or continues a worthless existence." Each side above has an answer to that. This lesson does not settle the question.

*A pastoral note.* For some readers this is not an abstract question. It may come with grief for someone who has died, or with fear about where they themselves stand. The verse itself was spoken to people facing persecution, and a few verses later the speech says, "So do not be afraid; you are worth more than many sparrows" (10:31, BSB). If this question weighs on you, a pastor who knows you is the right person to talk with, not a study note.

## Practice Bridge Example

One worked walk across the bridge, from what the passage says in its own setting to a modern situation. It is a model of the shape, not an answer key. Your own attempt in the Apply section is a separate, required step.

**Passage element.** Matthew 10:16: "Behold, I am sending you out like sheep among wolves; therefore be as shrewd as snakes and as innocent as doves" (BSB).

**Original-audience meaning (a modeled inference, not a cited fact).** Observation: the instruction is given to the Twelve as they are sent out, and the next verse names the danger ("beware of men"). The two halves pull against each other. Shrewdness without innocence would mean using the wolves' methods. Innocence without shrewdness would mean walking into traps. Matthew Henry's Concise Commentary reads the pairing this way: the disciples "were to avoid all things which gave advantage to their enemies... and all underhand measures," and "Be ye harmless as doves. Not only, do nobody any hurt, but bear nobody any ill-will." Inference: for the Twelve, this meant being careful about real danger without becoming deceptive or vengeful.

**Enduring principle (the learner's inference, labeled as such).** When a person is under real pressure from people with power over them, alert caution and plain integrity are meant to go together. Neither one excuses giving up the other.

**Bridge.** Carried forward: the pairing of watchfulness with harmlessness when facing a real threat. Not carried forward: the apostles' mission, their particular persecutors, or the promise in 10:19-20 of words given "in that hour." That promise is made to those being handed over to councils, and this bridge does not claim it for anyone else.

**Application class and promise scope.** Application class: an instruction to the Twelve, carried over by analogy rather than as a command addressed directly to the reader. Promise scope: none. Nothing in 10:16 promises that shrewdness and innocence will keep anyone safe. The very next verses expect flogging.

**Modern domain and situation.** Work. An office manager notices that a supervisor has been changing timesheet records. When she raises it privately, the supervisor hints that her job could be at risk.

**Response type and faithful response.** Response type: integrity held together with prudence. One faithful response: she makes accurate, dated notes of what she saw. She learns her company's formal reporting process, or gets outside advice, before she acts. She reports through that channel rather than through gossip. She does not alter records herself, does not retaliate, and does not hide what she knows.

**Cautions.** This is not legal advice. Reporting wrongdoing can have legal and financial consequences, and a person in this situation should talk to an employment lawyer, a union representative, or the appropriate reporting authority. The bridge also does not mean that every conflict at work is "sheep among wolves," or that a person who stays silent out of real fear has failed. It is an analogy you are testing, not a rule.

## Questions to Carry

These are left open on purpose. The lesson does not answer them, and you are not expected to answer them today.

1. "You will not reach all the towns of Israel before the Son of Man comes" (10:23, BSB) is one of the most discussed verses in Matthew, and this lesson did not research it. What would you need to know about how Matthew uses "the Son of Man comes" elsewhere (for example 16:28, 24:30, 26:64) before deciding what event 10:23 has in view?
2. The speech begins by limiting the mission: "Do not go onto the road of the Gentiles... Go rather to the lost sheep of Israel" (10:5-6, BSB). A few verses later the disciples will be witnesses "to the Gentiles" (10:18). What in the text explains the shift? And does it matter that 10:17-22 reappears in Mark's end-time speech (Mark 13:9-13)?
3. The Expositor's Greek Testament argues that "the One" in 10:28 cannot be God because 10:29-31 speaks of the Father's care. Ellicott, Meyer, Bengel and the Pulpit Commentary point to James 4:12. Read 10:26-31 as a unit. Which reading fits the flow of "do not be afraid... fear... do not be afraid" better, and why?
4. Matthew's Jesus says "I have come to turn" family members against each other (10:35), and he also quotes "Honor your father and mother" (15:4; 19:19). How would you state both without dropping either one? What does Micah 7:6's own setting, a lament followed by "But as for me, I will look to the LORD" (7:7), add to that?

## Teach-Back Prompts

Work from the text and your own notes on it. The five prompts below follow the shape every lesson in this course uses.

1. **Blind explain.** Without notes: (a) list the main movements of 10:16-42 in order (sent among wolves; handed over; do not be afraid; fear the One; sparrows; confess and deny; not peace but a sword; not worthy of Me; lose and find life; receiving). (b) Quote 10:34 in the BSB's own words. (c) Say which Old Testament verse 10:35-36 quotes, and name one clause where the wording differs.
2. **Five-minute outline, with citable reference points.** A workable frame: (0:00-1:00) the setting in the sending of the Twelve, and the overlap with Mark 13:9-13 (see Context); (1:00-2:00) the three "do not be afraid" commands and the one command to fear (see Literary Design); (2:00-3:00) Micah 7:6 compared with Matthew 10:35-36, clause by clause, with the Mishnah's later use of it (see Context and the Micah connection); (3:00-4:00) what named commentators say the "sword" means, including Sim and France (see Context); (4:00-5:00) one of the two longer Positions disputes, with at least two named sources on each side.
3. **Likely objection.** State the strongest objection to a reading of your own, then say how you would answer it. Form your own objection first. If you are stuck, here is one: "Calling 'hate' in Luke 14:26 an idiom for 'love less' is just a way of softening a hard saying." Before you answer, look at what the lesson actually reports: the Hebrew behind "unloved" in Genesis 29:31; Matthew's own "more than Me"; and Bengel and the Cambridge Bible, who reject a purely comparative reading. Here is a second candidate, running the other way: "Reporting annihilationism next to the Westminster Confession treats a minority view as equal to the historic position." To answer it, state what Stott himself says about which view is "traditional orthodoxy," and what each side says the word "destroy" can and cannot mean.
4. **What this passage does not establish.** Choose one and state the textual basis. (a) Matthew 10:34-36 does not say that anyone should use a sword. In Matthew's own text, the only command about a literal sword is "Put your sword back in its place" (26:52, BSB). (b) Matthew 10:37 does not, in its own wording, tell anyone to stop loving, supporting or honoring their parents. It ranks loves, and the commentators who read it most strongly still limit it to cases of conflict with following Jesus. (c) Matthew 10:28 does not, by itself, settle the duration of punishment. Both positions in Positions above appeal to it.
5. **Defend one connection, or give a reasoned `no_warrant_yet`.** Choose a connection and defend its type and label from the words of both texts. The five attached to this lesson:

| Passage | Type | Label | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Micah 7:6 | quotation | strong | One clause identical and two more matching in all key words, printed by the BSB as a quotation; no citation formula, and the first clause differs ("a son dishonors" against "a man against") |
| Luke 12:51-53 | parallel | strong | Same saying and order; "to bring peace to the earth" identical; "sword" against "division" |

The Micah row is this lesson's thread connection back to the Old Testament. Matthew gives it no formula, so it is graded on its wording alone. Three candidates were considered and not authored. **Ezekiel 38:21** ("every man’s sword will be against his brother," BSB) has both a sword and conflict between brothers. Robert Gundry proposed it as the source of Matthew's "sword," but Sim, who reports the proposal, calls it "certainly possible" yet "by no means conclusive," and notes that Matthew's list of family members follows Micah, not Ezekiel. **Luke 2:35** ("a sword will pierce your soul as well," BSB) is linked by Ellicott, but the only shared word is "sword." **Isaiah 66:24** stands behind Mark 9:48's description of hell, and Burk argues that Jesus' teaching on hell draws on it. But Matthew 10:28 uses none of its words (no worm, no fire), so no connection from this verse was authored.
