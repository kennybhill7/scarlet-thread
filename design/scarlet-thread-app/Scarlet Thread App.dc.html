00 · The world

Stone, storm light, one red rope

The films are the design system. Rock is desaturated near-black. Light is warm and comes from one direction, usually low. The only saturated thing in the frame is the thread — a heavy scarlet rope lying on real ground. The interface is what the frame allows: art at full bleed, type set into the dark, and almost no chrome.

Palette, sampled from the frames

This replaces the navy-and-gold pair currently in globals.css. Navy reads as a dashboard; the films are neutral stone. Gold stops being the interface accent and becomes what it is in the frames — light. Scarlet is promoted from “thread connection accent” to the identity colour, used once per screen and never for text.

The rope — the one piece of ornament

In the films the thread has thickness, shadow and slack. In the interface it is never a 1px line and never a gradient. It appears in exactly three forms:

8px, rounded caps, a darker underside — progress and the ridge path.
3px vertical rule — a verse or a passage that carries a live thread.
SEVENS · 9 Thread tag — 14% wash, hairline border, count always shown.

Never a glow behind text, never a scarlet button, never scarlet body copy. If the rope is on screen twice, one of them is wrong.

Surface rules
1Art is full bleed or it is absent. No image sits inside a rounded card with a border.
2Radius is 0–4px on the shell. The only round things are the device itself and a tap target.
3Separation comes from a 1px #23272d hairline, not from a lighter box. Panels stack; they do not float.
4Text over art always sits on a bottom-up scrim to #07080a, never on a blur.
5Scripture is Spectral on parchment or midnight — never on the stone shell, never in Inter.
6Scene copy states what the text puts in front of you. Never what it means.
7An unfinished day is never red, never a broken streak, never a gap on the mountain.

Weather moves. Interface does not.

2 The question queue

3 “Three times is a thread”

The app watches for repetition in your own words — three observations sharing a phrase or a shape — and offers exactly one prompt: “You have noticed this three times. This turns the one rule the whole system rests on into something the app helps you keep, instead of something you have to remember at 6am.

Build: print stylesheet + a share token. The outline assembly already exists in the Teach section.
5 Two-person mirror

Invite one person to the same mirror pair for the week. You both read and write privately Monday to Saturday; on Sunday the two sets of lines appear side by side, and neither of you saw the other's until then. Build: a pairing row + a reveal timestamp. Conviction sections never included, ever.
6 The woven year

Every chapter you study adds a strand to the rope; every thread you link twists two strands together. At the end of a year the app renders your mountain — the real painting, with your rope thickened where you worked and thin where you did not — as a poster you can print. Not a stats page. An object. The reward for a year of study should look like the thing you were following, not like a fitness report.

Build: one render job over the year's entries; the vista and the rope geometry already exist.
Deliberately not proposed

Streaks and badges. A social feed. Commentary or an assistant that explains passages — the app's whole discipline is that you write what you see before anyone tells you what it means. Push notifications beyond one optional daily reminder. Anything that turns eleven stages into levels.

The Switchback: real plates, drawn path

Direction 1 — real image assets. Not because vector can't be beautiful, but because the thing being asked for is ground, and photographic ground is the only ground. The constraint that produced the flat diagram was the wrong constraint, and you already named it. Below is what to build against instead.

The decision, in one paragraph

Ship terrain-only photographic plates with the rope and waypoints drawn on top in SVG. The panorama in this repo cannot be that plate: the ribbon and all eleven numbered pins are painted into it. That is the actual blocker, and it is bigger than fidelity — a pin baked into a JPEG cannot move when a stage's chapter count changes, cannot grow when study data grows, and cannot be a tap target. So the art gets re-rendered as landscape and nothing else. Everything interactive is a layer above it. Option 2 (filters over flat fills) can't close a medium gap, and option 3 is a real design language but it isn't the one you were shown and said yes to, so it would be me overturning your decision rather than serving it.

What the code lays elements onto
Five plates, stacked vertically Not one tall panorama — five, numbered top-down: plate-1-summit, plate-2-upper, plate-3-mid, plate-4-lower, plate-5-foothills, in assets/plates/. One term throughout this spec: a plate is the image, and there is no other name for it. Each is a full-width horizontal strip of terrain, stacked in a single scroll column. The plate is the reflow unit — when proportional spacing changes because a stage gained chapters, the plate’s height stretches. Mid-mountain terrain absorbs ±20% vertical stretch invisibly; a whole composed panorama does not.
Each plate holds one mirror pair, at one altitude This is why plates beat a zigzag: the ascent segment sits on one side of a plate and its mirror descent segment on the other, at the same altitude — Layer 3 above draws the five rules that prove it, all at identical altitude. Mirror-pair-at-matching-altitude stops being an assertion in the data model and becomes something the eye reads directly. Plate 3 physically contains the Flood and the World Judged.
The rope is one SVG path across all five plates One <svg> absolutely positioned over the whole column, viewBox="0 0 W H" where H is the summed plate height. Three strokes, in this order, all with stroke-linejoin:round: an 11px #4a0c10 shadow offset 3px down at 55% opacity, an 8px gradient face (#f2635c → #e5352f → #a8161c, top to bottom), and a 1.5px #f7877f highlight offset up-left at 50%. That stack is what makes it read as a rope with an underside rather than a sine wave — the flat red line in the shipped build has neither shadow nor highlight, which is most of why it looks like a diagram.
The route is extracted once, then it is data The route was read out of climb-vista.png — whose painted ribbon the artist laid on walkable ground — and is now committed as assets/plates/route.json (40 anchors and the emitted d, in plate coordinates). The app ships that file. It does not sample pixels at build time: the finished plates are terrain-only by art-brief point 1, so there would be nothing to detect. Extraction is a one-time authoring step, and it is recorded here because it is how the route was obtained and how it would be re-obtained if the mountain is ever repainted: trace a connected centreline rather than a per-column average — where a switchback doubles back, two ribbon branches share an x column and their mean lands on neither, inventing a downhill kink — so take the disjoint vertical runs per column, follow the branch nearest the previous column, median-filter over ~21 columns to absorb branch hops, then decimate to ~40 points and smooth with a Catmull-Rom spline whose controls sit a sixth of the way past each anchor. What six failed attempts at inventing a trajectory established: a rope authored in abstract coordinates always reads as an overlay, because nothing underneath it is ground.
Waypoints snap to the sampled path — one direction of dependency Addressed as (plate, x%, y%), never absolute pixels against a composed image. A waypoint’s altitude comes from its plate; its x comes from wherever route.json’s d crosses that altitude — evaluated against the emitted spline, not the anchor array it was decimated from. Those two datasets disagree at the extremum, which parks the summit pin ~10px off the crest. The dependency runs one way only — path from art, waypoints from path — so the two can never drift, and there is no second rule that derives the path from the pins. A chapter-count change moves a waypoint within its plate; a plate height change moves it with the plate. Both work without touching art. Sizes: 12px dormant, 15px begun, 22px current with a scarlet fill and a bone ring. Tap box padded to 44px, transparent.
Page weight — the real numbers Plates export at 1536×~380 WebP q78 ≈ 90–150 KB each, so ~600 KB for all five, and only the first two are needed for first paint. The remaining plates lazy-load as the user scrolls. Ship a 24×6 blurred LQIP inline per plate so the rope and pins are never floating on empty black. Nothing ships as PNG — the 2.1 MB climb-vista.png in the repo is a design artifact, not an asset.
Scroll behaviour & reduced motion An ordinary vertical scroll — no scroll-jacking, no pinned sections. A plate may pan horizontally by up to 3% of its own width as it passes through the viewport, which is the only parallax allowed. Under prefers-reduced-motion: no pan, no rope draw-on, plates static, path rendered at full length immediately. Every state stays legible with motion off, so nothing here is load-bearing.
Desktop is the same layers, rotated ≥ 1100px, the plates compose back into the landscape panorama and the scroll becomes horizontal drag — the geometry already signed off in The Climb.dc.html. Same plate set, same waypoint coordinates, different assembly. Do not author a second art set for desktop.
Art brief for the re-render — hand this to whoever generates the plates
1Terrain only. No ribbon, no road, no path, no pins, no numbers, no text. If the generator insists on a trail, it gets painted out before export. The route is not lost with it — it lives in assets/plates/route.json, and the code draws it.
2Light stays continuous across all five plates: low warm sun from the left, storm mass on the right. That gradient is the narrative — dawn at Genesis, weather at Babylon — so it cannot reset from one plate to the next.
3Each plate needs a walkable shelf, and it is not a free choice: it must follow the route already committed in assets/plates/route.json. Paint the plate with that path overlaid as a guide layer, put believable ground under every point of it, then delete the guide before export. Sheer cliff face where the route runs leaves the rope nowhere to lie; the shelf must read as continuous ground and meet its neighbours at the seams, because the path traverses the join rather than stopping at it. If the terrain genuinely cannot carry a stretch of the route, send the route back for revision — do not paint around it, because the code will draw it regardless.
4Plates are contiguous, never overlapping: five slices whose heights sum to the source height exactly. If overlap is wanted for blending, the shared rows must be feathered with a mask-image gradient — stacking duplicated rows hard is what produces a visible horizon line at every seam.
5Desaturated stone, one saturated colour reserved. Nothing in the terrain may be scarlet — the rope has to be the only red object in the frame or it stops reading as the thread.
6The eleven stage scenes already in assets/scenes/ stay exactly as they are. They are the full-bleed scene view, a different job from the plates, and they need no re-render.

Sequencing note: the plates are the only blocking dependency. Everything structural — plate stacking, the waypoint coordinate model, the three-stroke rope, lazy-loading, reduced-motion — can be built and tested against the five crops in assets/plates/ that the layer diagram above is running on. The finished plates then drop in behind it with no code change, because the rope reads route.json and never the pixels: the stand-in crops and the terrain-only plates are interchangeable as far as the code is concerned. That is the point of committing the route rather than detecting it.
