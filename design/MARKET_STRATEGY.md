# Scarlet Thread: making it a million-dollar app

A studio-quality Bible app already exists in outline. This is what I'd change, add, and cut to make it something people pay for and talk about — organized by what actually drives revenue and retention, not just polish.

---

## 0. The mission constrains the model: depth cannot be a paywall

You said it plainly: the goal is taking someone from limited knowledge to deep understanding. That has to be the test every decision below is run through — including the monetization plan in section 4. If the promise line, the covenant rail, or the Israel sub-arc are paywalled, the app is selling the very thing it exists to give away, and it stops being a teaching tool the moment someone can't afford to go deeper. I've revised section 4 accordingly: **the entire learning ladder — every rail, every layer of connection, all 11 stages — is free, forever.** Monetization has to live entirely in things adjacent to understanding, not in understanding itself: teaching/export tools, group and institutional features, physical objects. More on this below.

The product's real design problem, then, is not "what do we sell" — it's **how does one interface serve someone who has never opened a Bible and someone who reads Hebrew for fun, without either one feeling lost or bored.** That's a ladder, not a mode switch.

### The three-rung ladder

**Rung 1 — Orientation.** Someone with no background needs to know four things before anything else matters: there is one story, it has a shape, they are standing somewhere in it, and reading it themselves (not being told about it) is the point. The mountain *is* this rung — it should be the entire experience for the first week. No thread mechanics, no covenant rail, no typology vocabulary yet. Just: here is the shape, here is Genesis 1, read it, tell me one thing you saw.

**Rung 2 — Pattern.** Once someone has a few stages of observations behind them, they're ready to notice that things repeat — a word, an image, a promise. This is where threading (as it exists today) belongs, and where the timeline and covenant rails should switch on: not as advanced features, but as the natural next question a Rung-1 user starts asking on their own ("wait, didn't I see this before?"). The app should *offer* rung 2, not require it — a banner, not a wall: "You've noticed repetition three times. Want to see where else it shows up?"
 
**Rung 3 — Synthesis.** Typology, the promise line, the Israel sub-arc, Mirror Split — the tools for someone who already trusts the shape and wants to see the whole skeleton at once. These stay exactly as powerful as designed, but they should be reachable, not front-loaded. A "Go deeper" affordance that's always visible but never in the way.

Critically: **all three rungs are the same free product.** The ladder is a UX sequencing problem (progressive disclosure), not a paywall. Nobody should ever hit a lock icon on their way to understanding the Bible. Section 4 explains what to charge for instead.

---

## 1. The core insight, stated plainly

Every Bible app on the market does one of two things: it's a **reference tool** (search, commentary, translations — YouVersion, Logos) or it's a **habit tracker with verses in it** (streaks, plans, badges). Nobody has built a **map**. Scarlet Thread's mountain — one continuous shape, ascent and descent, mirror pairs across the summit — is a genuinely novel answer to the question every serious reader eventually asks: *how does this all fit together?* That is the product. Everything else is in service of making that map feel real, personal, and worth returning to.

The million-dollar version doesn't add features. It makes the map **tangible** — something you can point to, watch change, and be proud of — and makes the daily habit feel like *building* that object rather than *logging* an activity.

---

## 2. Make the object real (the biggest lever)

Right now the mountain is a static illustration with pins. The single highest-leverage change is turning it into a **living object that visibly changes as you study** — this is what makes progress tangible instead of abstract.

- **The rope thickens where you've worked.** Not a progress bar — the actual rope on the actual mountain gets visually thicker and more saturated on stages with more observations and threads. A stage you've spent five sessions on looks different from one you've spent twenty minutes on. This is truth-telling, not gamification: the object reflects real attention.
- **The woven year (already proposed).** At year's end, render the user's actual mountain — their rope thickness, their thread density — as a poster. This is the single best acquisition mechanic available: people share physical/printable objects that represent a year of their own effort. A fitness app's "your 2026 in review" gets screenshotted. This gets *printed and hung up.*
- **Session end state.** After the daily loop, don't show "streak: 14 days." Show the mountain, zoomed to where you just were, with the new thread visibly added to the rope. The reward is *seeing the object change*, not a number going up.

This is where the "million-dollar" framing earns itself: apps that turn effort into an artifact the user is proud of (Strava's year map, Duolingo's tree, Notion's graph view) build word-of-mouth that no push notification can buy.

---

## 3. Make the connections tangible, not just documented

From the last round of design work — ship these in order of leverage:

1. **The promise line as an isolatable strand.** One switch that dims every other thread and lights up Genesis 3:15 → Abraham → Judah → David → Isaiah 7:14 → Christ. This is the "one story" claim made visible instead of asserted. It's the best five-minute demo the app has — this is what converts a skeptical first-time user, and it's what a youth pastor shows a room to make the sale for the whole app.
2. **The kings/prophets timeline rail.** Nearly free to build, solves real and constant confusion, works for every Bible app's core problem: readers don't know *when* they are.
3. **The covenant rail.** Same reasoning — a fixed answer to "what applies to me right now" that most readers never get taught explicitly.
4. **Mirror Split** (read Genesis 3 beside Revelation 20, scroll-locked). This is the second-best demo moment after the promise line, and it's the feature that makes people say "I've never seen it laid out like that."

Ship the demo-moment features first. They're what turns a free trial into a subscription and a subscriber into someone who shows a friend.

---

## 4. Monetization shape

Given the mission in section 0: **understanding itself is never for sale.** Not ads, not a marketplace, and not a paywall on any rung of the ladder — the mountain, all 11 stages, threads, the covenant/timeline rails, typology, the promise line, the Israel sub-arc, are free forever, for everyone, no account required to start. That's not generosity marketing — it's the only version of this product that isn't self-contradicting.

Charge instead for the layer *around* personal understanding — the things that are genuinely separate goods:

- **Teach ($6–9/mo or ~$60/yr):** the Teach pack (for-me vs for-room, printable/shareable), the woven-year poster render and export, two-person paired mirror. These aren't "more Bible" — they're tools for taking your own understanding and giving it to someone else, which is a different job the app can reasonably charge for.
- **One-time purchase** ($40–60 lifetime) for the same Teach tier, aimed at the Obsidian/vault crowd this grew out of — people who resent subscriptions but will pay once for a tool they trust to last. Small, influential audience.
- **Church/group licensing** — flat annual fee per church for a leader dashboard (aggregate participation only, never individual conviction notes) plus bulk Teach-pack sharing. This is the real revenue path: one pastor onboarding forty families beats forty individual trials, and churches already budget for curriculum.
- **Physical goods:** the woven-year poster, printed and shipped, not just exported as a PDF. People already pay $30–40 for a printed Strava year-map or a custom star-map poster; a printed mountain with your own rope on it is a stronger emotional object than either, and it's pure margin on top of a feature you built anyway.

Do not sell data, do not sell placement, do not run ads next to Scripture. The audience that would pay well for this app is the audience most allergic to those models — and it's also an audience that would rightly leave the moment gaining understanding required a card on file.

## 5. Retention mechanics that fit this product's ethics

The existing design already rejects streaks and badges — correctly, for this audience. Retention has to come from the object and the habit being intrinsically worth returning to, not from guilt. Concretely:

- **The Sunday review is the real retention engine, not the daily loop.** Weekly, reflective, low-pressure check-ins retain better long-term than daily-streak pressure for a habit this personal — daily-streak apps see brutal 90-day drop-off precisely because a missed day feels like failure. Lean into making Sunday review the anchor: notify once, on Sunday evening, and nowhere else by default.
- **The question queue (already proposed)** — a dormant question resurfacing exactly when the text can answer it — is a retention mechanic disguised as a study feature. It gives the app a legitimate, non-manipulative reason to be worth opening beyond "it's Tuesday."
- **Thread maturity as a slow-burn reward.** A thread that's touched five times across both halves of the mountain should visually "complete" in some quiet way (not a badge — maybe it's the one thread rendered gold instead of scarlet, marking it as fully woven). Small, rare, earned by real engagement over months, not days.

---

## 6. What NOT to build (protect the thesis)

- No AI-generated insights, summaries, or "what this means." The entire credibility of the product rests on *your own noticing* being the content. An AI commentary layer is the fastest way to become indistinguishable from every other app and to hollow out the one thing users are paying for.
- No public social feed, no likes, no follower counts. The two-person mirror is the ceiling for social — private, mutual, time-boxed. A feed turns private conviction into performance and will visibly rot the tone of the app within a year.
- No streaks, flame icons, or point totals. Already correctly rejected in the current design — hold the line under revenue pressure. This is a brand asset, not just a UX choice.
- No infinite scroll or algorithmic "recommended for you" reading order. The mountain is the algorithm. Anything that replaces "next stage on the climb" with "here's what we think you'll like" breaks the whole premise that this is one story with one shape.

---

## 7. Twelve-month build order

1. **Now:** ship the current core (Climb, reader with thread lens, daily loop, workspace, threads, Sunday review) — this is already good and should go to real users, not stay in design forever.
2. **Months 1–2:** rope-thickens-with-use rendering, session-end "watch the mountain change" moment, kings/prophets timeline rail, covenant rail. These are cheap, structural, and make the free tier feel alive.
3. **Months 2–4:** promise-line isolation, typology arrows, Mirror Split. These are your demo moments, and they stay free; build paid Teach/export and group features around the audience that those moments create, not around access to understanding.
4. **Months 4–6:** Israel sub-arc (user-test heavily before shipping — this is the riskiest UX bet in the whole plan), Teach pack v1.
5. **Months 6–9:** two-person mirror, church/group licensing dashboard, woven-year render.
6. **Months 9–12:** the question queue, third-sighting prompt (the quiet AI-adjacent-but-not-AI pattern-matching features) — save these for last because they're the easiest to get wrong (false positives feel like surveillance) and the least urgent for revenue.

---

## 8. The one-sentence pitch this all serves

*"Every other Bible app helps you read a verse. This one helps you watch the whole story become one rope in your own hands — and shows you the object at the end of the year."*

If a feature doesn't serve that sentence, it's a distraction, however good the idea is on its own.
